The old seer sat by the fire and spoke of the nine worlds, and the young warriors leaned closer to hear her words above the crackling of the flames. She told them of Yggdrasil, the great ash tree whose roots reach down into the wells of wisdom and whose branches hold up the sky itself. Three roots it has, she said, and three wells lie beneath them: the well of Urd where the Norns weave the threads of fate, the well of Mimir where wisdom sleeps in dark water, and the dark spring Hvergelmir from which all rivers flow.

The warriors asked her about the runes, and she drew them in the ash with a charred stick. Each rune is a mystery, she whispered, a secret sung by Odin when he hung nine nights upon the windswept tree, wounded by his own spear, learning the songs that shape the world. Fehu is wealth that must flow like water, lest it stagnate and turn to poison. Uruz is the wild ox, raw strength untamed. Thurisaz is the thorn that guards the rose, the giant-force that tests the worthy.

She spoke then of the coming winter, the Fimbulwinter, three winters with no summer between them, when brother shall fight brother and the bonds of kinship shall break like ice on a spring river. The wolf shall swallow the sun, and darkness shall cover the middle world. Yet even in that darkness, she said, the seeds of the new world sleep beneath the snow, waiting for the thaw that must surely come, for the wheel of wyrd turns ever onward and no night lasts forever.

The smith among them asked about the forging of great blades, and the seer smiled. A blade is born twice, she said: once in the fire where the steel learns its shape, and once in the blood where it learns its purpose. The finest swords carry names and histories, and they remember every hand that held them. Gram, the sword of heroes, was broken and reforged, and so are we all broken and reforged by the trials the Norns set before us.

Then a young shieldmaiden asked the question they all carried in their hearts: what becomes of those who fall in battle? The seer grew solemn, and the fire seemed to burn lower. Half go to Freyja's hall, she said, the bright Sessrumnir where the chosen warriors feast among golden fields. Half go to Odin, to Valhalla, where they train each day for the final battle at the end of time. But those who die of sickness or old age travel the dark road to Hel's hall, where the dead sit in mist and memory.

The night grew deep and the stars wheeled overhead, cold and distant as the eyes of the gods. The seer wrapped her cloak tighter and spoke her final prophecy: that the old ways would fade like footprints in melting snow, that new gods would come from southern lands bearing a strange tale of a man upon a tree. But she said the roots run deeper than any axe can reach, and what sleeps in the blood of the north shall wake again when the world has need of it. The runes remember, the stones remember, and the wind carries the old songs still, for those with ears to hear them.
