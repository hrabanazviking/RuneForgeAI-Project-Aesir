For forty-one years, Tomas Reed kept the light at Harrow Point, a black granite tower standing on a narrow spit of rock where the bay meets the open sea. He rose every evening at dusk, climbed the one hundred and twelve iron steps to the lamp room, and lit the great lens that turned on its mercury bath, sending two white flashes every twenty seconds across the water. In winter the storms came down from the north and shook the tower so hard that the lamp itself seemed to tremble, and Tomas would sit through the night with his hand resting on the iron railing, listening to the sea strike the rocks below and counting the seconds between the flashes the way other men count their heartbeats. Ships that passed in the night depended on that rhythm: two flashes, a pause, two flashes again, a signature written on the dark that every captain on that coast knew by heart. Tomas kept a logbook in a hand as steady as the light itself. He recorded the weather, the names of vessels that passed, the state of the tide, and the birds that struck the glass in migration season and fell stunned to the gallery, where he would gather them gently and set them free at dawn. In all his years he missed lighting the lamp only once, on the night his wife died, and even then the assistant keeper, a boy from the village named Elias, climbed the steps in his place and the light never truly went out. When Tomas finally retired, the lighthouse board replaced the flame with an electric lamp and the lens with a smaller modern optic, and the tower that had needed a keeper's whole life now needed only a visit from a technician twice a year. Tomas moved to a cottage in the village where he could still see the light from his window, two flashes every twenty seconds, and he told the children who gathered at his fence that the light was not a machine but a promise: that someone was watching, that the rocks were marked, and that no ship traveling that coast in the dark was ever truly alone.
