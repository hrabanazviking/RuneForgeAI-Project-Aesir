## 2. The problem as framed

Tree-based speculative decoding (SpecInfer, SpecTr, Medusa — waves 2/13) organises draft candidates into a token tree and verifies the whole tree in one target pass. Sequoia's opening claim is that three things about that practice are broken, and that they are broken for one connected reason: **the tree has been designed as if acceptance probability were the only price in the system.**

1. **Tree shape optimised for acceptance alone misprices verification cost.** Prior speedup models — the paper names the original speculative-decoding analysis and SpecTr's computation model explicitly — treat verification time as a **constant**, independent of how many tokens are being verified: `Speedup(n) = G(n) / (1 + c·n)`, with the `1` standing in for "one verification pass" whatever its width. That approximation is survivable while trees are small, because a small tree's verification pass really does cost about the same as a single-token pass on a memory-bound GPU. It fails exactly where Sequoia wants to operate: once the tree is large, verification time grows with tree size — the target must attend, score, and emit logits for every node — so a tree that maximises *expected accepted tokens* can easily *lose* wall-clock time. The optimal tree is therefore not a property of the draft/target pair alone; **it is a function of the hardware's cost curve** — how verification time `t(n)` actually grows with `n` on the machine in front of you, against the draft cost `c` on that same machine. Different hardware has a different `t(n)`, hence a different optimal size *and* depth, and no fixed tree can be right on two machines at once.
2. **Existing tree shapes do not scale to larger budgets.** The structures in use — `k` independent sequences branching from the root (SpecInfer), full `k`-ary trees (Medusa) — have *bounded* expected yield: however large the speculation budget grows, the expected number of tokens generated per verification pass asymptotes to a constant (the paper's Table 1 gives the bounds: sequence `1/(1−P₁)`, `k` independent sequences `1 + Pₖ/(1−P₁)`, `k`-ary tree `1/(1−Pₖ)`). In the regimes where speculation pays most — above all **offloading**, where one target pass is so expensive that amortising it over many accepted tokens is the whole game — the budget *wants* to be hundreds of tokens, and the incumbent shapes waste it.
3. **Existing sampling/verification is not robust across decoding hyperparameters.** SpecInfer and SpecTr sample draft children **i.i.d. with replacement**. At low temperature the draft distribution sharpens onto a few tokens; if the draft's confident token is wrong, the sampler can propose that same wrong token repeatedly, burn the entire branching budget on copies of one mistake, and reject them all. The paper shows both methods degrading sharply at low temperature for exactly this reason (its Figure 3). A verification scheme whose acceptance collapses in one temperature regime is not a general accelerator.

The research question the paper sets itself follows directly: *how can we design an optimal tree-based speculative decoding method to maximise speedups on modern hardware?* — where "optimal" must mean optimal against measured cost, not optimal against acceptance alone.

## 3. Core mechanism in our own words

Sequoia is three separable components, each answering one limb of §2. The draft model, the target model, and the exactness contract of speculative sampling are inherited unchanged; everything below is tree construction, tree sampling, and tree sizing.

### 3.1 Dynamic-programming tree construction (scalability)

The construction rests on one modelling assumption, stated plainly in the paper: the **positional acceptance assumption** — the probability that verification accepts a token depends only on its **child position** `k` (first child, second child, …) within its parent, not on the token's identity or deeper history. Under that assumption a verification algorithm is summarised by an **acceptance vector** `p = (p₁, p₂, …)`, where `pₖ` is the probability of accepting the `k`-th child of an already-accepted node. (The vector is measured empirically per draft/target pair — the evaluation uses 200 examples to measure it before evaluating on a disjoint set.)

Given `p`, each node `v` of a tree gets a **score** `f(v)` = the product of the `pᵢ` along the path from the root to `v` — i.e. the probability that the whole path to `v` is accepted. The paper's Proposition 3.4 then gives the expected number of tokens generated by verifying tree `𝒯` in closed form:

> F(𝒯) = Σ_{v ∈ 𝒯} f(v)

Tree construction is now a clean constrained optimisation: **find the tree of size `n` that maximises F(𝒯)**. Its optimal substructure is what makes it tractable — writing `c(n)` for the best achievable F over all trees of size `n`, the optimum decomposes over how the `n−1` non-root nodes are distributed among the root's child subtrees:

> c(n) = max over allocations Σaᵢ = n−1 of [ 1 + Σᵢ pᵢ · c(aᵢ) ]

— and **dynamic programming** solves the recurrence for every `n` up to the budget (with a depth-bounded variant, since drafting time is proportional to tree depth, for the size-`n`, depth-≤`d` problem). Two consequences matter for the corpus. First, the DP tree's expected yield is **unbounded** in budget: under a power-law acceptance condition (below), the paper proves `G(n) ∈ Ω(b·log n / log log n)` — yield grows roughly logarithmically forever, where every incumbent shape's yield is capped (Table 1 lists Sequoia's bound as ∞). Second, the construction is *offline and cheap*: given a measured acceptance vector, the optimal tree for any `(n, d)` is computed once, in advance — which is also what lets the implementation capture the whole speculation step in CUDA Graphs.

### 3.2 Sampling without replacement (robustness)

Sequoia keeps SpecInfer's recursive tree-verification skeleton and changes how children are drawn from the draft distribution `Q` against the target distribution `P`:

1. **Sample without replacement.** Once a token has been proposed and rejected at a node, it is removed from the draft distribution (`D[xᵢ] ← 0`, renormalise) and can never be proposed again at that node. The sampler *cannot make the same mistake twice* — the low-temperature failure of §2 is removed by construction.
2. **Uniform fallback.** If every token with non-zero draft probability has been sampled and rejected, the draft distribution is replaced by the **uniform distribution over all not-yet-sampled tokens**, so speculation continues into the tail instead of stalling.

The residual bookkeeping is standard speculative sampling carried through the without-replacement draws (`R ← norm(max(R − D, 0))` on each rejection), and the paper proves (Appendix C.1) that the output distribution remains **identical to the target model's**. The theoretical payoff is stated as two properties no prior verifier holds simultaneously (Theorem 3.7): the **optimal-transport property** (at `k = 1`, acceptance `1 − ‖P−Q‖₁/2`, the best any verifier can do — the high-temperature strength of SpecInfer/SpecTr) and the **cover property** (if the draft's support covers the target's, acceptance reaches 1 within at most `k` speculations — the low-temperature strength of top-`k` sampling). SpecInfer and SpecTr hold only the first; top-`k` holds only the second; Sequoia holds both. Empirically the rejection rate then decays as a power law, `rₖ ≈ 1/kᵇ` — which is precisely the acceptance condition the §3.1 scalability theorem consumes, so the two components are designed to feed each other.

### 3.3 Hardware-aware tree optimiser (the cost model)

This is the component the wave exists for. Sequoia replaces the constant-verification-time speedup model with:

> Speedup(n, d) = G(n, d) / ( t(n) + d·c )

where `G(n, d)` is the DP-computed expected yield of the best tree of size `n` and depth `d`, **`t(n)` is the measured, hardware-dependent time to verify `n` tokens, normalised by the time to verify 1 token**, and `c` is the measured draft-token cost on the same normalisation. The procedure is deliberately mundane: **measure `t(n)` and `c` empirically on the inference hardware** (the paper's Figure 4 plots forward-pass time against `n` for its model/hardware pairs), compute `G(n, d)` from the DP, and **grid-search `(n, d)`** for the pair that maximises predicted speedup. For batch size `b > 1` the denominator becomes `t(b·n) + d·c`, since the verifier must verify a tree per batch element.

The theory section explains why this step is forced rather than optional: because `G` now grows only (sub-)logarithmically while `t(n)` grows at least linearly for large `n`, the speedup expression has a **finite interior optimum** — there is a best tree size, it is not "as large as fits", and its location is set by where the measured `t(n)` curve bends. Prior work could ignore `t(n)` only because its capped `G` made the optimum occur before `t(n)` grew; Sequoia's improved `G` pushes the optimum into the region where the cost curve matters. The empirical content of that claim is in §4: the optimiser's chosen sizes differ by an order of magnitude between regimes — **64–128 nodes on-chip, 768 nodes in offloading** — on the same model family.

## 4. Reported results — as reported, with hardware context

All figures are the paper's own (v2); nothing is re-derived. **Implementation:** Hugging Face Transformers + Accelerate, CUDA Graphs; sampling without replacement accelerated with the exponential-sort algorithm. **On-chip setup:** draft models JackFram/Llama-68M (JF68M), JF160M, and Sheared-Llama-1.3B (SL1.3B); targets Llama2-7B, Llama2-13B, Vicuna-33B; datasets C4, OpenWebText, CNN/DailyMail; prompt and generation length 128 tokens each; 200 examples to measure the acceptance vector, a disjoint 200 for evaluation. **Offloading setup:** draft Llama2-7B, target Llama2-70B, on an L40; the authors' own offloading baseline runs at 5.6 s/token, stated to match DeepSpeed-Zero-Inference (5.5 s/token).

**Headline (abstract, v2):** on an **A100**, up to **4.04×** (Llama2-7B), **3.73×** (Llama2-13B), **2.27×** (Vicuna-33B). In the **offloading setting on L40**, Llama2-70B at **0.56 s/token**, a **9.96×** speedup over the authors' optimised offloading system — also stated as 9.7× over DeepSpeed-Zero-Inference and 19.5× over Hugging Face Accelerate.

**On-chip detail (paper Table 4, A100; speedup, mean tokens/step in parentheses; tree config = size, depth):**

| Target | Draft | T | Best cell | Sequoia | SpecInfer best |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Llama2-7B | JF68M | 0 | C4, tree (128, 10) | **4.04× (5.08)** | 3.70× (4.11) |
| Llama2-7B | JF68M | 0.6 | C4, tree (128, 7) | 3.18× (3.92) | 2.47× (2.97) |
| Llama2-13B | JF68M | 0 | C4, tree (64, 9) | **3.73× (4.20)** | 3.30× (3.64) |

Across all on-chip cells Sequoia beats both SpecInfer tree shapes (`5×8` and `8×8`) in every row of the table. Note the pattern the optimiser produces, stated by the authors themselves: optimal on-chip trees are **64–128 nodes**; larger targets get *smaller* optimal trees, because their verification time grows faster with `n`.

**Offloading detail (paper Table 5, L40; target Llama2-70B, draft Llama2-7B, tree size 768 in every row):** C4 at T=0 — **9.96× (12.18 tokens/step), 0.56 s/token**, vs SpecInfer `16×48` at 6.46× (8.66); C4 at T=0.6 — 8.26× (10.12), 0.69 s/token; OpenWebText T=0 — 8.14× (9.83), 0.69 s/token; CNN/DailyMail T=0 — 8.78× (10.46), 0.64 s/token. The 768-node, depth-17–23 trees are the cost model working as designed: when one target pass costs seconds, verification width is nearly free relative to drafting depth, and the optimum moves two orders of magnitude out from the on-chip setting.

**Component ablations (as reported):**

- *Tree construction:* Sequoia trees generate up to **33% more tokens per decoding step** than `k` independent sequences at tree sizes ≤ 512 (JF68M draft, Llama2-13B target, T=0.6, CNN/DailyMail), and keep growing with budget where the baseline asymptotes (paper Figure 2 / §4.2.1).
- *Sampling/verification:* up to **65%** speedup over SpecInfer and **27%** over top-`k` sampling-and-verification from the robustness fix alone (introduction's summary figures; §4.2.2 reports the comparison across temperature and top-`p` with the tree held fixed).
- *Hardware-aware optimiser:* selecting `(n, d)` by the measured `t(n)` yields up to **~38–40%** higher speedup than fixed tree sizes (paper Table 3: e.g. Vicuna-33B/SL1.3B on A100 — hardware-aware 2.37× vs fixed `n=128` 2.09× vs fixed `n=256` 1.72×; Llama2-13B/JF68M on L40 — 3.26× vs 3.16× vs 2.51×). Table 3 is also the cleanest single exhibit for §2's thesis: in both rows the *larger* fixed tree generates **more tokens per step** (5.09–5.30 vs 4.40–4.41) and is **slower** — acceptance-optimal and wall-clock-optimal visibly come apart.

**Context-length honesty note:** Sequoia's evaluation is a **short-context** evaluation — 128-token prompts, 128-token generations. Nothing in the paper measures acceptance vectors, `t(n)`, or speedups at long context; the long-context half of this wave is TriForce's, and any transfer of Sequoia's numbers to long-context serving is an extrapolation the paper does not make (see §7 on the RULER interaction).

## 5. Position vs waves 2/13 + ledger #46

**Against wave 2 (foundations).** Leviathan et al. / Chen et al. contribute the chain, the rejection-sampling exactness proof, and the first speedup model — the model whose constant-verification-time term Sequoia explicitly generalises. Medusa contributes the parallel-head tree whose `k`-ary shape Sequoia's Table 1 uses as a bounded-yield foil. EAGLE contributes feature-level drafting; Sequoia is drafter-agnostic (its evaluation drafts with ordinary small *models* — JF68M/JF160M/SL1.3B and, for offloading, Llama2-7B itself), and nothing in its three components presumes any particular drafter, only a measurable acceptance vector.

**Against wave 13 (tree verification, second generation).** SpecInfer generalises the *verifier* — any tree, verified exactly in one tree-attention pass — but builds that tree from a static preset and samples its children i.i.d. with replacement; Sequoia can be read as SpecInfer's three deferred problems taken up in order: which tree (DP-optimal under a budget), drawn how (without replacement), and how big (hardware-measured). EAGLE-2 generalises the tree from the *draft* side: per-context, confidence-ranked reshaping, where the ranking signal is the drafter's own calibrated confidence. The two are complementary answers to the same defect — a static shape spending budget by position alone. EAGLE-2's signal is **contextual and online** (this step's confidences); Sequoia's is **statistical and offline** (a measured acceptance vector, position-only by assumption, plus a measured hardware curve). EAGLE-2 can adapt within a single generation; Sequoia cannot — its tree is fixed before decoding starts — but Sequoia prices the one thing EAGLE-2's model leaves out: EAGLE-2 fixes its verification budget `m` (48–60 in its paper) as a hyperparameter, while Sequoia *derives* the budget from `t(n)`. A complete system wants both: Sequoia's sizing wrapped around EAGLE-2's per-step allocation. TriForce's ablation (§5.2.3 of the companion dossier) already gestures at the composition from the other direction, estimating gains from running Sequoia trees inside its hierarchy.

**Against IDEA-LEDGER #46 — the precise statement.** #46 records the small-card verdict: *tree verification pays at width ≈2–3 and low-tens total nodes (≈8–16), depth over breadth, budget measured locally* — derived in wave 13 for **short/medium context, batch 1, on a 6 GB-class card (the RTX 2060 regime)**, from SpecInfer's flattening acceptance table and EAGLE-2's datacentre-sized budgets, and explicitly labelled judgement (evidence C) pending Q21's local measurement. Sequoia's relation to that verdict, stated exactly:

- **Sequoia generalises #46's reasoning; it does not overturn #46's conclusion inside #46's regime.** #46 says "the budget must be measured locally." Sequoia supplies the missing formalism for *what* to measure and *how the budget follows from it*: measure `t(n)` and `c`, compute `G(n, d)` from an acceptance model, maximise `G/(t + d·c)`. On hardware where `t(n)` rises steeply — a small card whose per-node attention, KV-write, and logits costs are paid from the same narrow bandwidth that makes speculation pay — that formula's optimum *is* a narrow, shallow-budget tree. #46's numbers are, in Sequoia's language, a claimed point on one machine's cost curve. The verdict's *method* is thereby strengthened from dossier judgement to a published, proved optimisation procedure.
- **Sequoia bounds #46's scope: narrowness is a conclusion about a cost curve, not a law about trees.** The same formula, on the L40 offloading curve where a target pass costs 5.6 s, returns a **768-node** tree and the paper's largest speedup (9.96×). Anyone quoting #46 as "wide trees don't pay" without its regime qualifiers is refuted by Sequoia's Table 5; quoted *with* its qualifiers (small card, on-chip weights, short/medium context, batch 1), it stands untouched — Sequoia's own on-chip optima (64–128 nodes on A100/L40-class cards, shrinking as the target grows) already slope in #46's direction as verification gets relatively more expensive, and the 2060 sits further down that same slope.
- **Q21 is Sequoia's measurement, localised.** Q21 asks for tokens-per-pass vs wall-clock tok/s against node budget on the RTX 2060, plus the confidence≈acceptance calibration curve for the exact (target, head) pair. In Sequoia's terms that is: measure `t(n)` on the 2060, measure the acceptance vector `p` for the local pair, and let the DP + grid search *compute* #46's budget instead of asserting it. Sequoia therefore converts #46 from a verdict awaiting a measurement into a verdict awaiting *its own prescribed* measurement — the strongest form of support a later paper can give an earlier judgement without testing the same hardware. What Sequoia does **not** do: measure anything on a 6 GB card, at batch 1 with a resident chat context, or with a quantised GGUF target — so #46's specific numbers (≈8–16 nodes) remain unmeasured local judgement, neither confirmed nor refuted here. Refinement of the entry itself is the coordinator's to bank; this dossier's statement is that the refinement available is *generalise the method, bound the scope, leave the numbers as Q21's to settle*.

## 6. Applicability

### (a) Personal long-context chat and the ggml-class stack

*[Engineering judgement, labelled as such; the paper's speedups are A100/L40 numbers and are not imported as expectations.]*

Of the two papers in this wave, Sequoia is the cheap one to adopt — and it is cheap precisely because its expensive-sounding components are all **host-side**. The DP tree construction is an offline computation over a measured acceptance vector (milliseconds of ordinary CPU code, run once per draft/target pair, cacheable). The hardware-aware optimiser is a profiling pass (`t(n)` for a ladder of `n`, one draft-cost constant) plus a grid search — again host-side, again once per machine. Sampling without replacement is sampler logic: an exponential-sort / Gumbel-style draw with a rejected-set mask, no kernel surgery beyond what a tree verifier already requires. **What Sequoia needs from a ggml-class stack is therefore: (i) the tree-attention verification substrate itself** — ancestor-only masks, depth-based positions, tree-structured KV with rollback — which is wave 13's priced kernel investment, unchanged by this paper; **(ii) an exposed, tunable tree budget** (size and depth as runtime knobs, not compiled-in constants — the same exposure IDEA-LEDGER #27's note already demands of the shipped EAGLE-3 path); and **(iii) a one-time profiling hook** to measure `t(n)` on the user's card at the user's context lengths. Given (i)–(iii), Sequoia's contribution is a few hundred lines of host code and a calibration file. Without (i), nothing in Sequoia is adoptable at all — it is a paper *about* trees, and a chain-only runner has no tree for it to optimise; its chain-form residue is the sizing discipline itself (choose γ by a measured cost curve rather than by habit), which the corpus already applies to draft length under #46's companion reasoning.

For the personal long-context chat workload specifically: Sequoia's optimiser becomes *more* valuable as context grows, because `t(n)` steepens with context length (every verified node attends over the whole accumulated history), so the optimal tree at 100K context is smaller than at 1K on the same card — a fixed budget chosen at short context is guaranteed wrong at long context, and Sequoia is the corpus's only tree paper that makes the budget a measured function of the operating point. Its acceptance vector, however, is context-blind (position-only, measured offline); at long context the honest design pairs Sequoia's sizing with EAGLE-2-style per-step allocation, per §5.

### (b) The losslessness standard

Sequoia claims distribution-faithful acceleration: the sampling-without-replacement verifier is *proved* (Appendix C.1) to reproduce the target model's output distribution exactly, at all temperatures — indeed robustness across temperature is the claim the cover/optimal-transport theorem exists to support. **The corpus's grading discipline does not take that claim on trust, however good the proof.** Under the rule stated across this corpus (and enforced in the Aesir work as raw-oracle comparison): a "lossless" or "distribution-identical" claim is verified by comparing the accelerated system's outputs/logits against the unaccelerated target run as oracle — teacher-forced rank and logit-gap on the raw prompt, per the corpus's comparator discipline — not by citing the paper's theorem. The theorem tells us the *algorithm* is exact when implemented as specified; the oracle comparison is what tells us the *implementation in front of us* is that algorithm. Sequoia's own history argues for the rule: its v1 and v2 abstracts already differ in headline numbers (see §1), a reminder that even a correct paper's record drifts between versions, and only measurement against the local oracle settles what a local build does.
