## 2. The problem as framed

Colocation — running prefill and decode on the same GPUs, batched together — does two distinct kinds of damage, and the paper is careful to separate them because they have different cures:

1. **Interference.** A prefill step takes far longer than a decode step. Batched together, the decode steps in the batch are delayed by the prefill, so TPOT (time per output token) stretches; run separately but on the same GPU, the phases queue behind each other instead. Prioritising one phase's scheduling starves the other's latency. The paper's motivating measurement (its Figure 1): serving a 13B model (512-token inputs, 64-token outputs) on **one A100-80GB**, existing colocated systems sustain a maximum **goodput of ~1.6 requests/second** at 90% SLO attainment — the rate is set by whichever of the TTFT or TPOT constraint binds harder, and interference makes both bind early.
2. **Coupling.** Even if interference were scheduled away, colocation forces both phases to share one resource allocation and one parallelism strategy. But the phases want different things (§3): prefill is compute-bound and barely benefits from batching; decode is bandwidth-bound and lives on batching; their best tensor/pipeline-parallel shapes differ, and differ again with arrival rate and SLO tightness. A colocated deployment must pick one compromise configuration for two workloads.

Underneath both is a metric argument, and it is the paper's most durable contribution. Applications do not have one latency; they have **two**: **TTFT** (time to first token — the prefill phase's report card) and **TPOT** (time per output token — decode's). A request that is fast on one and slow on the other is a failed request. Raw throughput (tokens/second, counting everything) rewards systems for work users experience as late. The paper therefore optimises **per-GPU goodput: the maximum request rate that can be served while meeting both the TTFT and the TPOT SLO for at least the target fraction of requests (90% in the main evaluation), divided by the GPUs provisioned.** Goodput is throughput *with the latency promise kept*, priced per GPU — i.e. cost per acceptable query. The motivating example's punchline: served independently, the same 13B workload's prefill phase alone sustains 5.6 rps per GPU and decode alone 10 rps; a 2-prefill + 1-decode GPU split serves 10 rps total = **3.3 rps per GPU, 2.1× the colocated figure** — before any cleverness, purely from uncoupling.

## 3. Core mechanism in our own words

**Disaggregation.** Prefill and decode run as separate instance pools on different GPUs. A request is prefilled on a prefill instance, which produces the first token and the prompt's KV cache; the KV cache is transferred to a decode instance, which generates the remaining tokens. Interference is eliminated structurally — there is no shared GPU for the phases to fight over — and each pool can be sized, batched, and parallelised for its own SLO.

**Per-phase design rules (the paper's §3 analysis).** Disaggregation is only worth its transfer cost if each pool is run the way its phase wants:

- *Prefill instances* are compute-bound: a single 512-token sequence already saturates an A100 for the 13B model studied, so batching beyond a critical input-length threshold (the paper's *L_m*) buys no efficiency and only adds queueing delay for everyone in the batch. Prefill batches stay small. For parallelism, the paper models prefill as an M/D/1 queue and shows the crossover analytically and empirically: **intra-op (tensor) parallelism wins at low arrival rates and under strict TTFT SLOs** (it cuts execution time, the dominant term), while **inter-op (pipeline) parallelism wins at high rates** (it cuts queueing delay, which dominates once the queue builds).
- *Decode instances* are bandwidth-bound: batching is the whole game, because one decode step per request barely uses the GPU. Freed from prefill competition, a decode instance can accumulate large batches; its ceiling becomes KV-cache memory capacity. For parallelism the rule inverts in emphasis: **intra-op when the TPOT SLO is stringent** (to cut per-step latency), **inter-op or plain replication beyond that** (to scale rate near-linearly).

**Placement: the goodput search.** Given a model, a workload (arrival process + input/output length distributions), TTFT/TPOT SLOs, and an attainment target, DistServe searches for the placement — (a) parallelism strategy for prefill instances, (b) parallelism for decode instances, (c) how many of each, (d) where they sit on the cluster — that maximises per-GPU goodput. Because profiling every candidate on real hardware is prohibitive, the search runs against a **simulator** (discrete-event, with analytical execution-time models); the paper validates it against the real system with **< 2% SLO-attainment error** across the rates tested. Two algorithm variants handle two network realities:

- **High node-affinity** (fast cross-node fabric): optimise each phase's parallelism independently for best per-GPU goodput (binary search over rates in simulation), then replicate instances until the target traffic rate is met.
- **Low node-affinity** (slow cross-node links — the paper's own testbed had only **25 Gbps** between nodes): add the constraint that a prefill instance and its decode partner keep the **same pipeline stage on the same node**, so KV transfer rides intra-node **NVLink (600 GB/s peak between A100s)** instead of the weak fabric. The search space grows (intra-node combinations must be enumerated jointly) and the algorithm takes minutes — acceptable, the paper notes, because it runs once per deployment.

**KV transfer cost accounting — the number that decides whether any of this is sane.** The paper does the arithmetic in the open (§3.3): the KV cache of one **512-token request on OPT-66B is ≈ 1.13 GB** (OPT uses multi-head attention — deliberately the worst case for transfer). At an arrival rate of 10 rps, hiding the handoff needs 11.3 GB/s ≈ **90 Gbps** sustained — within the InfiniBand fabrics the paper cites (up to 800 Gbps) and trivially within NVLink. The measured cost in the real system (§4) came out far below even that: transmission was **< 0.1% of total request latency** for OPT-175B on ShareGPT, and **> 95% of requests saw < 30 ms** of transfer delay, despite the 25 Gbps cross-node testbed — because the low-affinity placement kept transfers on NVLink. The lesson is conditional, and the paper states the condition: disaggregation is cheap *when placement respects bandwidth*; it is a bandwidth-aware placement algorithm with a serving system attached, not a free split.

**Implementation shape.** An orchestration layer over a custom engine: ~6.5K lines of Python (placement module, OpenAI-compatible frontend, orchestration: dispatch, KV transmission, result delivery) and ~8.1K lines of C++/CUDA (parallel execution engine; Ray actors as GPU workers; NCCL cross-node, asynchronous `cudaMemcpy` intra-node; continuous batching, FlashAttention and PagedAttention integrated). Online scheduling refinements sit on top of the placement (§4.3 of the paper), including mitigation of the FCFS "convoy effect" in prefill queues; fault tolerance across the new prefill→decode dependency is explicitly left as future work.

## 4. Reported results — as reported, with hardware and model context

All figures are the paper's own (v3), on datacentre hardware, under SLO constraints. **Nothing below is a raw-throughput number, a single-user number, or a consumer-hardware number** — every gain is a goodput gain: rate sustainable at ≥ 90% SLO attainment, per GPU.

**Setup (§6.1).** Testbed: **4 nodes, 32× NVIDIA A100-80GB SXM** (NVLink intra-node, 25 Gbps cross-node — hence the low-affinity algorithm in most experiments). Models: **OPT-13B, OPT-66B, OPT-175B, all FP16, all multi-head attention** — chosen, the paper says, precisely because MHA maximises KV size and therefore transfer pressure; GQA/MQA models would only make disaggregation look better. Workloads and SLOs (paper Table 1): chatbot on ShareGPT — OPT-13B TTFT 0.25 s / TPOT 0.1 s; OPT-66B 2.5 s / 0.15 s; OPT-175B 4.0 s / 0.2 s; code completion on HumanEval — OPT-66B, 0.125 s / 0.2 s; summarisation on LongBench (inputs capped at 2,048 by OPT's positional limit) — OPT-66B, 15 s / 0.15 s. Poisson arrivals. Baselines: **vLLM** (colocated; intra-op 1/4/8 for the three model sizes) and **DeepSpeed-MII** (colocated with chunked prefill — the Sarathi-style baseline; it could not serve OPT-175B at all in their configuration).

**End-to-end (§6.2), at 90% SLO attainment:**

- **Chatbot (ShareGPT, all three OPT sizes):** DistServe sustains **2.0×–4.6× higher request rate than vLLM** and **1.6×–7.4× higher than DeepSpeed-MII**; at fixed rate it withstands **1.8×–3.2× tighter SLOs than vLLM** (1.7×–1.8× vs MII). The paper's diagnosis of vLLM's failure mode is specific: vLLM met TTFT for most requests but its SLO attainment was dragged down by TPOT violations — colocated prefill work stretching decode steps, exactly the interference of §2.
- **Code completion (OPT-66B):** **5.7× higher rate and 1.4× tighter SLO than vLLM**; 1.6× / 1.4× vs DeepSpeed-MII. Both systems end up TTFT-bound here; DistServe wins by giving prefill instances more intra-op parallelism automatically, cutting prefill execution latency.
- **Summarisation (OPT-66B, long inputs):** **4.3× higher rate and 12.6× tighter SLO than vLLM**; 1.8× / 2.6× vs DeepSpeed-MII. This is the source of the abstract's "12.6×" — a *summarisation, vs-vLLM, SLO-tightness* figure, not a general multiplier.
- **Headline reconciliation:** "up to **7.4×** more requests" is the top of the vs-DeepSpeed-MII chatbot range; "up to **12.6×** tighter SLO" is summarisation vs vLLM. Quoting either without its workload and baseline is misquoting the paper.
- **Placement is non-obvious, which is the point:** for OPT-175B on ShareGPT the search chose prefill inter-op = 3, intra-op = 3 and decode inter-op = 3, intra-op = 4 — an asymmetric split no colocated system can express and, the paper argues, not one an operator would find by hand.
- **Ablation (§6.4, simulated):** "vLLM++" — vLLM given its *best* parallelism by exhaustive search — performed **the same as stock vLLM**. Parallelism tuning cannot recover what interference takes; that null result is the paper's strongest evidence that disaggregation, not configuration, is the active ingredient. The unconstrained (high-affinity) placement beats the constrained one in simulation, pricing the bandwidth constraint honestly.

**Where the paper itself limits the claim (§7).** Two admissions worth more than the headlines: in **throughput-optimised offline** settings (no latency SLOs worth the name), chunked-prefill piggybacking may be preferable because it fills every batch to the compute bound; and in **resource-constrained settings — "only a few or even a single GPU"** — DistServe's design space "is significantly limited", it "struggles or even fails" to find useful allocations, and the authors recommend **non-disaggregated systems** there. §6a takes them at their word.

## 5. What it changed in practice

DistServe (with Splitwise, §7) turned prefill/decode disaggregation from an observation into the default architecture question for large-scale serving — "aggregated or disaggregated, and if disaggregated, at what pool ratio?" Its specific ideas surface in production systems as follows (public state verified 2026-11-15; see §8):

- **Engines grew a disaggregated mode.** vLLM ships disaggregated prefill behind a **KV-connector** abstraction (`KVTransferConfig`; producer/consumer roles) with LMCache, Mooncake and NVIDIA NIXL as transfer backends — and its docs remain honest in DistServe's spirit: the feature is marked **experimental** and stated **not to improve throughput**, only to let TTFT and tail latency be tuned separately (corpus: `runners/vllm.md` §4.4). SGLang runs prefill/decode-disaggregated pools with Mooncake/NIXL transfer and, in recent versions, zero-downtime role switching between pools — a softening of DistServe's static placement (corpus: `runners/sglang.md` §4.4). TensorRT-LLM exposes the same split.
- **Orchestrators productised the placement search.** NVIDIA Dynamo (1.0 GA March 2026) is DistServe's architecture as a platform: separate prefill/decode worker pools, NIXL KV transfer, a KV-aware router — and a **Planner** that dynamically reallocates GPUs between pools from live TTFT/ITL metrics, plus an AIConfigurator that recommends pool sizes and parallelism from model, GPU budget and SLOs. That is the paper's offline placement algorithm turned into a control loop and a sizing tool. The Kubernetes-native llm-d project follows the same shape.
- **A production existence proof at scale: Mooncake.** Moonshot AI's serving platform for Kimi (FAST '25 Best Paper) is a KV-centric disaggregated architecture — separate prefill and decode clusters over a pooled, RDMA-backed KV store — reported in production at **> 100 billion tokens/day**, with the project reporting Kimi handling **75% more requests** under SLOs on real workloads (higher figures, 59–498%, come from trace-replay simulations and are labelled as such by their sources). Mooncake's transfer engine and store are now standard KV-connector backends for both vLLM and SGLang. Whatever one thinks of any single multiplier, disaggregation is no longer a paper architecture: it is how at least one top-tier consumer chatbot is actually served.
- **Goodput became the reporting standard.** Post-DistServe serving evaluations increasingly report rate-at-SLO (or SLO-tightness at rate) rather than bare tokens/second — the paper's metric framing is now the field's honesty norm, and the reason §4 of every dossier in this wave can state its numbers comparably.

## 6. Applicability, honestly scaled

### (a) Ollama on a 6 GB consumer GPU, single-user chat

**Disaggregation itself does not apply, and the paper says so first** (§4, final bullet): with one GPU there are no pools to split across, no second device to hold decode while prefill runs, and no interconnect budget to spend — the design space the placement algorithm searches is empty. Do not build a two-process prefill/decode split on one 6 GB card in imitation of DistServe; the KV would be "transferred" across PCIe or loopback at real cost to buy isolation that a scheduler can provide for free in-process. What transfers is the *thinking*, in four pieces:

1. **TTFT and TPOT are separate budgets — in the UI as much as the engine.** DistServe's foundational move is refusing to report one latency. For a single user the SLO language translates directly: the user experiences "how long until it starts" (TTFT) and "does it stream smoothly once started" (TPOT / worst inter-token gap) as different failures with different causes and different fixes. The improved Ollama should measure, display, and tune them separately — e.g. a long-prompt turn that is slow to start but smooth is a *prefill* problem (fix: residency/prefix reuse, IDEA-LEDGER #1/#5), while a turn that starts fast and stutters is a *decode-path* problem (fix: keep prefill work and CPU bookkeeping out of decode steps, #18/#25). One undifferentiated "tokens/second" hides which disease the user has.
2. **Goodput, miniaturised: count only turns that kept the promise.** The single-user analogue of rate-at-SLO is a *stall budget*: a turn is "good" if its first token arrives within a chosen bound and no inter-token gap exceeds a chosen bound. Optimising the runner for that count — instead of for peak tok/s in a benchmark with no other load — is what leads to Sarathi-style chunking and decode-first scheduling (§ sibling dossier) rather than to throughput theatre. It also gives the build a falsifiable target on the actual pain case: the 14,712-token turn (52.1 s of silence in the recorded baseline) fails any plausible TTFT budget; the fix is judged by whether it stops failing, not by a fleet multiplier.
3. **Disaggregation in time, not space.** The interference DistServe removes with hardware can be *reduced* on one GPU with scheduling: never let background prefill-class work (memory extraction, summarisation sidecars, a second persona's prompt ingestion) share steps with a live decode; give the live decode the GPU in decode-maximal steps and let prefill-class work fill bounded leftover budget (Sarathi-Serve's policy), or run it strictly between turns. The principle — *the phases have separate budgets and one must not be allowed to spend the other's* — survives the loss of the second GPU intact.
4. **KV transfer accounting is the local spill/restore arithmetic.** DistServe's 1.13 GB-per-512-token example (MHA OPT-66B) is the same computation the 6 GB runner needs for its own tiering decisions (IDEA-LEDGER #10/#11): how many bytes is this session's KV, and does moving it (GPU → RAM → disk and back) beat recomputing it? The numbers differ by orders of magnitude in our favour — a GQA 7–8B model's KV per token is a small fraction of OPT-66B's MHA figure, and the corpus's measured restore-vs-recompute case (2.14 s vs 160.7 s for a 7.3K-token session on a CPU-only box) shows the local verdict — but the *discipline* is DistServe's: price the handoff in bytes and bandwidth before choosing the architecture, and never assume the transfer is free or that it is prohibitive. Measure it.

### (b) Project Aesir

- **Aesir's serve is disaggregated in the dimension it actually has: devices, not machines.** Aesir runs on heterogeneous silicon under one roof (Strix Halo: CPU + integrated GPU + NPU, unified memory) under an accelerator-first law (NPU, then GPU, then CPU). DistServe's per-phase analysis maps onto that law directly: prefill is the compute-bound phase and belongs on the strongest compute device; decode is the bandwidth-bound phase and belongs where bandwidth-per-watt is best — which on a unified-memory APU may not be the same device. Aesir should evaluate *phase-to-device assignment* as an explicit design axis for its resident-session serve, with the same honesty DistServe applies: the assignment is a measured placement decision per model, not a doctrine.
- **The handoff already exists — it is the snapshot.** On unified memory there is no KV to ship across a network, but Aesir's hybrid models have the analogous problem in harder form: recurrent state cannot be sliced, so phase/session transitions move via whole-state snapshots (Q35-5: bit-exact restores, ~158.9 MB SSM+conv per snapshot for the 27B class, bounded store of 4 per session). DistServe's contribution to that design is the accounting habit: a snapshot is a *transfer* with a byte size and a bandwidth (here, memory-copy bandwidth), and the serve should budget snapshot placement and restore cost the way DistServe budgets KV placement — including its placement constraint lesson (keep the producer and consumer of a handoff on the fastest shared path; on an APU, that means snapshots live in the unified pool, never staged through a slow tier mid-turn).
- **Goodput is the right scoreboard for the serve gate.** Aesir's testable serve should be graded the DistServe way, scaled down: not peak tok/s, but *sessions served within stated TTFT and inter-token-gap budgets* — including while a second session prefills. That single metric would have caught, in one number, the failure mode the whole HS campaign was built against (a resident session's turn stalling behind a 14,712-token re-evaluation). The placement search itself miniaturises too: Aesir's measured-fit gate (choose weight residency by measured memory) is the same search with a smaller space — per-phase device, per-model residency, per-session snapshot budget — and should be run by measurement, per DistServe's simulator discipline, rather than by intuition.
- **What Aesir should not import:** multi-instance replication, cross-node placement, and any design that assumes a second machine. Aesir's serve target is one user's hardware; its disaggregation budget is spent entirely inside the box.

## 7. Cross-links

- `papers/sarathi-serve.md` (this wave) — the colocated answer to the same interference. The two papers argue with each other in print: DistServe (§2.2/§7) holds that chunked prefill mitigates but cannot eliminate interference and pays for TPOT with TTFT; Sarathi-Serve (§6) holds that disaggregation wastes prefill-side KV capacity and demands fast interconnects. Both are right about the other's cost; the choice between them is a hardware-count question, which is why §6a takes Sarathi's medicine and DistServe's metrics.
- `papers/splitwise.md` (this wave) — the independent, simultaneous arrival at phase-splitting (submitted a month before DistServe), differing in centre of gravity: heterogeneous hardware and power/cost provisioning rather than goodput and parallelism search. Where they agree — phases differ, colocation couples them, KV transfer is affordable on cluster fabrics — the claim is about as established as this field gets.
- `papers/orca.md` — continuous batching, the colocated substrate both disaggregation papers react against; DistServe's baseline behaviour (TTFT met, TPOT violated) is Orca/vLLM-style batching measured under two SLOs at once.
- `runners/vllm.md` §4.4 — disaggregation as shipped in vLLM: KV connectors, producer/consumer roles, LMCache/Mooncake/NIXL backends, and the docs' own "experimental, no throughput gain" caution.
- `runners/sglang.md` §4.4 — SGLang's PD-disaggregated deployment shape, Mooncake transfer, and role switching.
- `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md` — **#11** (decouple model lifetime from session lifetime; spill idle, never running) is the single-machine descendant of this paper's uncoupling instinct; **#10** (session persistence with a validity envelope) is its KV-handoff arithmetic applied to disk; **#18** (chunked prefill) is the rival medicine the ledger ranks above disaggregation for this target, for exactly the reasons §6a gives.
