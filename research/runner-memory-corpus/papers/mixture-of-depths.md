## 2. The problem as framed

A vanilla transformer spends its FLOPs **uniformly**: every token, at every layer, gets a full self-attention + MLP block, whether the prediction at that position is trivial or hard. The paper opens on the analogy — not all problems need the same effort, and not all tokens need the same processing to predict well — and on the observation that the uniform spend is a choice, not a requirement: if some tokens' predictions are already easy at some depths, the compute spent deepening them is a budget spent where it isn't needed.

The framing constraint is what makes the paper distinctive: the authors want conditional compute that **current hardware can actually run well**. General conditional-computation schemes produce dynamic computation graphs — variable tensor shapes decided on the fly — which fight the static-graph, known-shape execution that accelerators are built around. So the problem is posed as: *given a fixed total compute budget, smaller than a vanilla transformer's, set in advance — can the network itself learn where along the sequence and the depth to spend it?* The budget is an input to the design, not an outcome of runtime decisions.

## 3. Core mechanism in our own words

**One expert, skippable.** MoD borrows MoE's routing machinery and points it at depth instead of width. At a routed block, each token faces exactly two paths: (1) the ordinary block — self-attention followed by MLP — or (2) the **residual connection**: pass through unchanged, at ~zero cost. A token's *depth* — how many blocks actually process it — becomes a learned, per-token, per-layer variable. Unlike MoE, routing governs attention as well as the MLP, so a skipped token is also not made available as a key/query at that block: routing decides both which tokens get updated and which tokens others can attend to there.

**The router and the capacity.** Per routed block:

1. A router — a single learned linear projection of the token's embedding — emits **one scalar weight per token**, expressing the router's preference for that token to receive the block's compute.
2. **Expert-choice top-k:** the block itself selects the *k* tokens with the highest router weights (per sequence; capacity C is defined per batch element). Expert-choice, rather than token-choice, is chosen deliberately: it guarantees exactly k tokens arrive (perfect load balance, no auxiliary balancing loss needed), and it lets relative weight magnitude decide which tokens most need the block. Tokens outside the top-k take the residual path.
3. Selected tokens' block outputs are **multiplied by their router weights** before being added back — this puts the router weights on the gradient path, so the language-modelling loss itself trains the router. (The paper reports this suffices; weighting the bypass path too was tried and not needed.)
4. **Capacity is the budget dial.** k is set a priori as a fraction of sequence length. The paper swept capacities from 12.5% to 95%, routing either every block or every other block. The winning configuration — used for the headline isoFLOP analysis — is **12.5% capacity (k = 256 of a 2,048-token sequence; 87.5% of tokens bypass each routed block), routed every other block**, with full-capacity blocks interleaved. Aggressive reduction kept helping down to 12.5% and degraded beyond it; interleaving full blocks was "crucial" — frequent full-attention opportunity is what makes the sparse blocks safe. Learned routing is not decoration: a stochastic-routing control (top-k over Gaussian noise) performs drastically worse than both MoD and the vanilla baseline.

**Training integration.** MoD is trained in from scratch in the paper's experiments: routers, capacities and interleaving are part of the architecture during pre-training (batch size 128, sequence length 2,048, cosine schedules; model sizes swept 60M–3B parameters). There is no post-hoc conversion of a vanilla checkpoint — the routing behaviour, including the empirical pattern that frequently-routed tokens correlate with higher-entropy (harder) predictions, is learned jointly with the weights under the ordinary language-modelling objective plus the sampling machinery below.

**The causality problem, and the precise inference property.** Top-k over a sequence is **non-causal**: whether a token makes the cut depends on router weights of tokens that come *after* it, which do not exist yet during autoregressive sampling. This is the paper's central serving problem, and its solution defines what "MoD at inference" actually is:

- **Option 1 — auxiliary loss:** a binary cross-entropy loss during training, with the router's own outputs as logits and its top-k selections as targets, centring the router's sigmoid at 0.5 — selected tokens are pushed above 0.5, unselected below. At sampling time the router output alone, thresholded causally, then mimics the top-k decision. Cost as reported: ~0.2–0.3% effect on the primary objective.
- **Option 2 — auxiliary predictor:** a small MLP (same inputs as the router, stop-gradient) trained to predict whether a token will be in the top-k. It does not affect the language-modelling objective and, as reported, does not significantly impact step speed.
- Either way, the prediction task is reported **easy: upwards of 97% accuracy soon into training, ~99% in the autoregressive evaluation**, and switching from top-k to predictor-based routing at sampling shows little performance degradation on the paper's held-out evaluation (256,000 sequences, 500M tokens).

So the serving-friendly property must be stated **precisely, in two halves**: (i) at *training* (and at prefill-style parallel processing, where the full sequence exists), shapes are perfectly static — exactly k tokens per routed block, known tensor sizes, total FLOPs fixed by the capacity choice before any data arrives; the dynamism is only in *which* tokens fill the fixed slots. (ii) At *autoregressive sampling*, the guarantee changes character: routing becomes a per-token causal prediction (router threshold / predictor), so the *per-step* count of processed tokens is no longer hard-capped by construction the way the training-time top-k capped it — what is inherited is a router trained so that its causal decisions reproduce the budgeted allocation with ~97–99% fidelity, and an architecture whose expected compute per step is the budgeted fraction. MoD is therefore **not** a dynamic-exit scheme (nothing decides "this token is done, stop the forward pass" mid-flight, and there is no data-dependent control flow in the block shapes at training time), but neither is its sampling-time compute as rigidly static as its training-time compute — the static-graph claim belongs to the top-k formulation, and the paper is honest that sampling runs on a learned approximation of it.

**MoDE.** The mechanism composes with MoE (Mixture-of-Depths-and-Experts): *staged* (MoD routing before the MoE block, so tokens can skip attention too) or *integrated* (a "no-op" expert added among the MLP experts, one routing operation). Reported: the gains compound, and integrated MoDE beats merely shrinking expert capacity in a conventional MoE — explicitly learning to choose the residual path matters, versus being dropped by overflow.

## 4. Reported results — as reported, with context

All figures are the paper's own. Experiments are pre-training studies on decoder-only transformers, 60M–3B parameters, sequence length 2,048, run as isoFLOP analyses at total training budgets of **6×10¹⁸, 2×10¹⁹, and 1×10²⁰ FLOPs**. The paper does not centre a named serving GPU benchmark; step-speed figures are training/sampling step rates on equivalent hardware within the study, and FLOPs-per-forward-pass is used as the tightly-correlated proxy (paper's statement) where wall-clock is not plotted.

- **IsoFLOP quality:** MoD "drags the baseline isoFLOP curve down and to the right" — at equal total training FLOPs, the optimal MoD model reaches **lower loss** than the optimal vanilla baseline and is larger. Headline magnitude (introduction): up to **~1.5% improvement in final log probability** at equivalent training FLOPs and equivalent wall-clock training time.
- **IsoFLOP speed:** there exist non-optimal-but-pareto MoD variants that match or beat the isoFLOP-optimal baseline's loss while stepping much faster — the worked example: a 220M MoD variant slightly outperforming the 220M isoFLOP-optimal baseline while stepping **upwards of 60% faster** in training (the figure caption says 66% faster for the specific plotted model), at approximately equal total wall-clock training time. The abstract's general claim: models matching baseline performance at equivalent FLOPs/wall-clock require **a fraction of the FLOPs per forward pass** and can be **upwards of 50% faster to step during post-training sampling**.
- **Why the speed exists (paper's decomposition):** two multiplicative sources — (1) fewer FLOPs *per parameter* (routed blocks process 12.5% of tokens; note the quadratic bonus the paper's arithmetic implies for attention at capacity C: query-key cost scales as C², so a 12.5%-capacity block's attention matmul is ~1/64 the vanilla cost, its MLP ~1/8), and (2) the isoFLOP-optimal MoD model being bigger means a *smaller* MoD model can match the baseline — fewer parameters *and* fewer FLOPs per parameter. A predictive rule falls out: the isoFLOP-optimal MoD model for a budget is the one whose FLOPs-per-forward-pass equals the optimal baseline's — size the model to spend the saved FLOPs back (the paper finds adding depth beats adding width when doing so).
- **Autoregressive evaluation:** on 256,000 held-out sequences (500M tokens), predictor-based (causal) routing preserves the training picture — MoD variants exist that beat the isoFLOP-optimal baseline while using fewer FLOPs per forward pass; the top-k→predictor switch costs little (see §3 for the 97–99% router-prediction accuracy).
- **Memory (reported briefly, not studied extensively — paper's own caveat):** at larger sizes some MoD variants needed fewer total devices (smaller TPU topology) than equivalently sized baselines, and the authors anticipate KV-cache savings during sampling, since tokens that skip a block contribute no KV there. No KV-cache size table is reported; treat the KV benefit as the authors' stated expectation, not a measured headline.
- **Routing analysis:** visualisations of a trained interleaved model show some tokens engaging nearly every block and others bypassing whenever possible; frequently-processed tokens correlate with higher-entropy predictions — consistent with (not proof of) "compute follows difficulty."

## 5. Position in the corpus

**The kinship with wave 24 (early exit).** LayerSkip (`papers/layerskip.md`) and SkipDecode (`papers/skipdecode.md`) share MoD's founding observation: uniform depth over-spends on easy tokens. All three make the depth a token actually traverses variable. That is where the kinship ends, and the difference is structural:

- **Early exit is a *stopping* rule.** A LayerSkip token proceeds layer by layer and, at a decision point, *terminates* — it takes no later layers at all, and the decision is made online, per token, during the forward pass (confidence or a trained exit head). SkipDecode fixes the exit layer per *position* by a schedule, precisely because per-token online decisions break batch regularity. In both, the compute a token receives is a **prefix** of the network: layers 1…E.
- **MoD is a *routing* rule.** A token may skip layer 7 and be processed again by layers 8, 10, 12 — its compute is an arbitrary **subset** of the network, not a prefix (the paper explicitly contrasts itself with halting/early-exit on exactly this point, and speculates re-entry after a skip — attending alongside tokens that took every layer — is a useful property exit cannot offer). And the *amount* of compute is decided **before the data arrives**: capacity k per block is a fixed architectural constant, so at training time the batch's shapes, FLOPs and memory are known constants — **batching-regular by construction**.
- That last property is exactly the one wave 24 lacked. SkipDecode's position-locked schedule (every token at position t exits at the same layer) exists as a workaround: it buys batch regularity by surrendering per-token adaptivity — the schedule is blind to which tokens are hard. MoD keeps per-token adaptivity (the router reads the token) *and* regularity (exactly k tokens per block regardless of which they are) — at training time. The honest asterisk, from §3: at autoregressive sampling the top-k is replaced by a causal predictor, so the regularity at decode is statistical (~97–99% decision fidelity), not the hard combinatorial guarantee training enjoyed. The corpus should record MoD as *solving the batching problem at training/prefill and approximating the solution at decode*, which is still a stronger serving shape than either wave-24 design offers.
- **Training posture differs too.** LayerSkip's recipe (layer dropout + shared-head early-exit loss) can be fine-tuned into an existing model; SkipDecode's schedule likewise presumes fine-tuning under it. MoD as published is a **pre-training architecture** — routers and capacities are present from the start, and the paper offers no retrofit path (§6a).

**Vs MoE (`papers/mixtral.md` and the MoE line).** MoE routes tokens *sideways* to one of several same-cost experts — total compute ~constant, capacity spent on specialisation. MoD routes *downward-or-not-at-all* — total compute genuinely shrinks. MoDE (§3) shows the axes are orthogonal and compound.

**Vs the KV-selection line (waves 1/3, 17).** Those designs choose which *past* a token may read; MoD chooses which *layers* a token passes through — and, as a side effect the paper flags but does not quantify, which layers ever write a KV entry for it. It is the corpus's sparsity line rotated ninety degrees: sparsity over depth instead of over context.

## 6. Applicability

### (a) Retrofit honesty — trained-in; detect-and-honour only

MoD offers **no retrofit path, and none should be improvised.** The router weights are learned jointly with the model under the language-modelling objective; the interleaved full blocks, the 12.5% capacity, and the block weights' adaptation to a world where most tokens skip most routed blocks are all baked in during pre-training. Bolting a router onto a trained vanilla GGUF and skipping 87.5% of tokens at every other block would not be "MoD at a different operating point" — it would be structured layer-dropping with a learned-looking gate, and the paper's own stochastic-routing control (drastically worse than baseline) is the nearest published evidence for what unlearned routing does. Unlike DuoAttention (this wave's other dossier), there is not even a frozen-model calibration that produces the missing artefact: the missing artefact is the model's entire training history.

The runner posture is therefore **detect-and-honour**, in the same class as LayerSkip-trained checkpoints: if a checkpoint declares MoD structure (router weights present in the file, per-block capacity/interleaving metadata), the runner must implement expert-choice routing at prefill and the causal predictor/threshold routing at decode **faithfully** — honouring the declared capacities rather than "improving" them — because the model's quality claims are conditioned on exactly that allocation. A runner that runs an MoD checkpoint with all tokens through all blocks is not running the model the paper evaluated (it would also forfeit the entire speed case); a runner that invents capacities is running a model nobody evaluated. As of this dossier, MoD remains a research architecture without a mainstream open-checkpoint ecosystem — the honouring path is specified now so it is cheap later, the same discipline the corpus applies to early-exit declarations (IDEA-LEDGER's early-exit entry, priced in `papers/layerskip.md` §6 / `papers/skipdecode.md` §§3, 6).

For Project Aesir the transferable asset is not the checkpoint format but the **shape discipline**: fixed per-block token budgets with fluid token identity is the one conditional-compute formulation whose training-time cost is knowable before the batch is seen — the property a from-scratch local architecture would want if Volmarr ever commissions one, and the correct citation when "why not just early-exit?" is asked of such a design.

### (b) The 6 GB arithmetic — what MoD does and does not buy on the small card

Priced against `memory/kv-cache-design.md` §7.2 (Llama-3.1-8B-class on the 6 GB card; 614 MiB KV budget; f16 `kv_rate` 131,072 B/token → 4,912 tokens), honestly and briefly: **MoD buys nothing for that case, because that case's model is not an MoD model.** Its benefit, where an MoD checkpoint exists, would be on the *compute* axis — upwards of 50% faster sampling steps as reported (§4) — and, by the authors' unquantified expectation, on KV: a token that skips a block writes no KV entry at that block, so an interleaved 12.5%-capacity model's KV footprint per token is materially below a same-shape vanilla model's (roughly: full KV at the interleaved full blocks, ~12.5% of a block's KV at routed blocks — for the every-other-block layout, on the order of ~55–60% of vanilla, as *our arithmetic from the layout*, not a paper measurement). On the 6 GB card that would stretch the same 614 MiB from 4,912 to roughly 8,000–8,500 tokens — a real but smaller factor than DuoAttention's GQA operating point delivers for models that actually exist as GGUFs today, and purchasable only with a checkpoint that does not currently exist in the local catalogue. Recorded so the comparison is on file; the actionable 6 GB lever in this wave is DuoAttention, and MoD's lever is step speed for a future trained-in architecture.
