## 2. The problem as framed

Atom's framing (§3 of the paper, a roofline analysis before any algorithm) is the founding statement of this wave's thesis: **at serving batch sizes, weight-only quantisation stops buying anything except memory.**

The argument runs in three steps:

1. **Unbatched decode is memory-bound, and batching is the fix the serving world actually uses.** A single decode step is a matrix–vector product: a whole weight matrix is loaded to perform very few multiplications, so GPUs sit under-utilised. Batching many requests turns the dense layers (QKV generation, output projection, MLP) into genuine matrix–matrix products, amortising the weight load across the batch and raising arithmetic intensity until the dense layers become **compute-bound**.
2. **Once the dense layers are compute-bound, weight-only quantisation's bargain collapses.** In a W4A16 scheme the weights are stored in 4 bits but must be dequantised to FP16 before the multiply, because the activations are FP16 — so the arithmetic still runs at FP16 rates (the paper's reference point: an A100 delivers 1,248 INT4 TOPS and 624 INT8 TOPS against 312 FP16 TFLOPS). Weight bytes shrink; compute throughput does not move. Worse, weight-only schemes leave the KV cache in FP16, so the self-attention layers — which cannot be batched across requests, because no two requests share a KV cache — stay bounded by full-precision KV traffic. The paper's runtime breakdown (Llama-7B, varying batch) puts dense + self-attention layers at over 90% of execution time: the two bottlenecks are exactly the two things weight-only quantisation does not accelerate.
3. **The obvious next step — quantise activations too, all the way to INT4 — was known to destroy accuracy.** Prior W4A4 methods (the paper compares SmoothQuant repurposed at 4 bits, OmniQuant, QLLM) show perplexity increases from large to catastrophic (see §4: naive W4A4 round-to-nearest scores WikiText-2 perplexity 2,382.73 on Llama-7B against an FP16 baseline of 5.68). INT8 weight–activation quantisation (SmoothQuant's regime) is accurate but leaves the INT4 tensor cores unused and halves, rather than quarters, the memory traffic.

So the problem is a *systems* problem wearing an algorithm's clothes: the format (W4A4) only pays if the kernels can execute it natively, and the format is only accurate if its error mechanisms (outlier channels, coarse scales, stale calibration statistics) are engineered away **without** reintroducing the irregular memory access and extra operators that would eat the kernel win. A format without kernels and a scheduler that respect it wins nothing end-to-end — Atom is the first paper in this corpus to build all three layers (algorithm, fused kernels, serving integration) as one design and measure the result end-to-end.

## 3. Core mechanism in our own words

Atom is W4A4 — 4-bit weights, 4-bit activations — with a 4-bit KV cache, held together by four mechanisms. Symmetric quantisation for weights and activations; asymmetric for the KV cache; group size 128 throughout the evaluation.

**Mixed precision by reordering — the outlier channels are kept, but segregated.** The central accuracy mechanism. Activation channels are profiled on calibration data (128 randomly sampled WikiText-2 sentences); channels are ranked by the square-sum of their activations, and the top **128 channels are designated outliers and quantised to INT8** instead of INT4. The ablation (§4) shows why this is the load-bearing choice: keeping the outliers in high precision takes Llama-7B W4A4 perplexity from 2,382.73 (plain RTN) to 11.37, and moving them from FP16 down to INT8 costs only a further +0.02 — INT8 is expressive enough for the outliers, so nothing in the deployed model is FP16. The systems problem is that outlier channels are scattered through the tensor, and scattered mixed precision means irregular memory access — the failure mode of LLM.int8()-style decomposition. Atom borrows and repurposes RPTQ's reordering: activation channels are **reordered so the outlier channels sit contiguously at the end of the matrix**, and the weight matrix is reordered offline to match (a one-time static cost, since the outlier set is fixed by calibration). The online activation reorder is fused into the preceding operator, at a reported 0.5% of runtime. One kernel then handles a regular INT4 block and a regular INT8 tail — mixed precision with dense, predictable access patterns.

**Fine-grained group quantisation, fused into the GEMM.** Both weights and activations are quantised per group of 128 elements along the channel, each group with its own scale. Group quantisation is the second-largest accuracy term in the ablation (+group-128 takes perplexity from 11.39 to 6.22). Its systems cost is real: a grouped GEMM produces one partial result per group, each under a different scale, and those partials must be dequantised to FP16 and summed. Atom fuses that dequantise-and-accumulate **into the GEMM kernel itself**, in place, so the partials never make a round trip to memory. This fusion — not the group size — is the design decision; unfused, group quantisation's overhead is exactly what QServe (§5, and the companion dossier) later measures at 20–90% for the W4A4 family.

**Dynamic activation quantisation, fused into the producer.** Activation scales are not precomputed from calibration data; they are computed per activation matrix at inference time, so each input is quantised against its own actual distribution. The overhead is hidden by fusing the quantisation into the prior operator (the ZeroQuant trick). Weights, being static, get the offline treatment instead: GPTQ is applied during weight quantisation (worth −0.09 perplexity in the ablation), with grid-searched clipping factors (0.9 for activations, 0.85 for weights, per the evaluation setup).

**KV-cache quantisation, fused into attention.** The KV cache is quantised asymmetrically to low bit (INT4 in the ablation) per attention head, and its dequantisation is fused into FlashInfer, alongside PagedAttention-style memory management. The paper's justification is structural: softmax normalisation dampens the effect of key-cache error on the output, and its profiling shows the value cache exhibits outliers far less than the activations do — the KV cache is, in its terms, *more amenable to quantisation* than the tensors the rest of the paper fights over. The measured cost is small: +0.12 perplexity for INT4 KV in the ablation.

**Serving integration.** Atom is integrated into an end-to-end serving framework (the paper's reference [Chen 2023b]) with continuous-batching-style request replacement (finished requests are swapped for new ones, Orca-style, first-come-first-served), ShareGPT-derived request-length distributions for the workload, and batch sizes swept from 8 to 256. The design rule visible throughout: **every quantisation side-operation — reorder, quantise, dequantise, re-quantise — is fused into an operator that already exists**, so the low-bit data path is the only path, never a detour.

## 4. Reported results — as reported, with context

All figures are the paper's own. Hardware context matters and is easy to get wrong in secondary summaries: **the efficiency evaluation was run on a 24 GB RTX 4090 (CUDA 12.1)**, not a datacenter card — the A100 figures in the paper are cited tensor-core peak rates used in its roofline argument, not its measurement platform. The quantisation process itself was run on a single RTX 6000 (Llama-65B quantised in ~4 hours, against >6 for SpQR and up to 16 for OmniQuant, as reported).

**Accuracy — perplexity.** WikiText-2, W4A4 (paper Tables 1 and 3):

| Model | FP16 | Atom W4A4 | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Llama-2-7B | 5.47 | 6.03 | +0.56 |
| Llama-2-13B | 4.88 | 5.26 | +0.38 |
| Llama-1-7B | 5.68 | 6.16 | +0.48 |
| Llama-1-30B | 4.10 | 4.54 | +0.44 |
| Llama-1-65B | 3.53 | 3.89 | +0.36 |

Baselines at W4A4 on Llama-2-7B for scale: SmoothQuant 83.12, OmniQuant 14.61 — Atom is not incrementally better than the prior W4A4 field, it is in a different regime. On PTB and C4 (Llama-1-7B) Atom scores 9.62 / 7.70 against FP16's 8.80 / 7.08 — the sub-1-point increase holds across all three corpora, which matters for calibration hygiene: the outlier set and clipping were calibrated on WikiText-2 samples, and PTB/C4 are therefore genuinely held-out perplexity evidence. At W3A3 Atom degrades sharply but remains coherent (Llama-2-7B: 15.07) where the baselines collapse entirely (SmoothQuant W3A3: no finite result).

**Accuracy — zero-shot.** Six tasks (PIQA, ARC-easy/challenge, BoolQ, HellaSwag, WinoGrande) via lm-eval, Llama-1 family, W4A4 average accuracy, FP16 → Atom: 7B 64.04 → 61.78; 13B 66.39 → 64.66; 30B 67.46 → 67.07; 65B 70.97 → 69.57. The evaluation text states the losses as 2.3 / 1.7 / 0.4 / 1.4 points respectively. (The introduction's "1.6% average drop" summary does not reconcile exactly with these tables under any obvious averaging — quoted here as the paper's own summary claim, with the table figures above as the auditable record.)

**Ablation — where the accuracy comes from** (Llama-7B, WikiText-2, paper Table 4). FP16 5.68 → W4A4 RTN 2,382.73 → +keep 128 outliers in FP16: 11.37 → outliers to INT8 instead: 11.39 → +group size 128: 6.22 → +clipping: 6.13 → +GPTQ: 6.04 → +INT4 KV cache: 6.16. Two mechanisms (mixed precision, grouping) do nearly all the work; everything else is refinement, and the KV cache is nearly free. This ordering is the dossier's most reusable fact.

**Efficiency — kernels.** Fused GEMM, Llama-7B configuration, sequence 1024, RTX 4090: at batch 512 Atom's matrix multiplication is **3.42× FP16 and 1.92× INT8**; at small batch sizes the GEMM is memory-bound and weight-only quantisation performs comparably — the W4A4 advantage *grows with batch*, exactly as the §2 framing predicts. Self-attention with the quantised KV cache fused into FlashInfer: at batch 128, **1.79× over INT8 KV and 3.52× over FP16 KV**, with throughput rising linearly as KV bits fall.

**Efficiency — end-to-end serving.** ShareGPT workload, batch 8–256, same-memory comparison: **up to 7.73× the FP16 throughput and 2.53× the INT8 (W8A8) throughput at the same latency target**; the introduction additionally reports 5.5× over W4A16. Two honesty markers in the paper's own presentation: (i) part of the gain at fixed memory is *capacity* — 4-bit weights and KV admit larger batches under the same memory budget, and the throughput comparison is made at fixed memory, so the number compounds a per-batch kernel win with a batch-size win; (ii) where the RTX 4090's memory could not hold the configuration, results are **simulated** (KV caches reused from a subset of requests while preserving access patterns and all computation) and drawn as dashed lines — the headline maxima should be read with that caveat attached. Latency per token stays below the FP16/INT8 baselines at every batch size measured, and below 100 ms even at batch 256, as reported.

## 5. Position in the lineage

The chain this wave was commissioned to state, with what each step assumed the previous one had solved:

**SmoothQuant (wave 5) — migration.** SmoothQuant proved activation quantisation is survivable if difficulty is *moved*: a calibration-fitted diagonal rescaling shifts outlier magnitude from activations onto weights, making W8A8 essentially lossless on stock INT8 GEMMs. What it assumed: that 8 bits is enough headroom on both sides of the migration. At 4 bits the assumption fails — Atom's own tables show SmoothQuant's approach at W4A4 scoring 83.12 perplexity on Llama-2-7B. Migration redistributes a fixed quantity of difficulty; at INT4 there is nowhere left to redistribute it *to*.

**Atom (this dossier) — systematised mixed precision.** Atom assumes SmoothQuant's diagnosis (outliers are channel-structured and persistent) but abandons migration for *segregation*: identify the outlier channels once, hold them in INT8, group-quantise everything else at INT4, and spend the engineering budget on making that irregular scheme execute regularly (reordering, fusion everywhere). It assumes the outlier set is *small, stable, and knowable from calibration* — 128 channels, fitted on 128 WikiText-2 sentences, fixed for the life of the model. That assumption is its strength (static weights, static reorder, no runtime detection) and its exposure (a distribution shift that moves the outliers moves them into the INT4 bulk). Atom also assumes a serving stack exists to integrate into — its throughput claims are claims about a system, not a format.

**QuaRot (wave 16) — rotation theory.** QuaRot rejects Atom's premise: instead of identifying and exempting outlier channels, change the basis so outliers *do not exist* — a randomised Hadamard rotation, fused into the weights via computational invariance, spreads outlier energy across all coordinates, after which plain scalar quantisation suffices with **zero** channels kept in high precision. Wave 16's dossier records the comparison directly: group-wise QuaRot matches Atom's perplexity (Llama-2-7B 5.93 vs Atom-128G 6.03) with an outlier-channel count of 0 against Atom's 128. What QuaRot assumes that Atom did not: that you may rewrite the model's weights by an orthogonal transform offline, and that the two places fusion cannot reach (the FFN down-projection input, post-RoPE keys) can pay for online Hadamard transforms at runtime.

**QServe (companion dossier) — rotation + system co-design.** QServe takes QuaRot's rotation for block inputs, SmoothQuant's migration for block outputs and (as SmoothAttention) for keys, Atom's reordering idea recast as salience-grouping — and then argues the whole W4A4 programme was measuring the wrong bottleneck: the dequantisation work on low-throughput CUDA cores eats the tensor-core win, so it retreats one step to W4A8 and rebuilds the kernels around that. It assumes the previous steps solved *accuracy*; its contribution is showing accuracy was no longer the binding constraint — kernel main-loop overhead was.

**The honest counterpoint (wave 16's verdict, carried explicitly).** Every win in this chain is measured **at serving batch sizes, on datacenter-class GPUs, with throughput as the objective**. The premises invert for the batch-1 personal case: decode is a memory-bound GEMV where arithmetic intensity is ~1 MAC per element regardless of precision, so INT4 tensor-core rates are unreachable in principle, not merely unimplemented; the dense layers never become compute-bound, so quantising activations buys no compute; and the batch-size-at-fixed-memory compounding in Atom's headline number has no batch to compound. What survives the inversion is only what was ever about *bytes moved* — weight bytes and KV bytes. §6 grades Atom piece by piece under that inversion rather than resolving the tension rhetorically.

## 6. Applicability

### (a) A 6 GB batch-1 runner — which pieces survive

Roofline terms first, because they decide everything below. At batch 1, decode processes one token: each dense layer is a matrix–vector product whose arithmetic intensity is ~1 MAC per weight element loaded. The token rate is therefore set by **bytes of weights streamed per token ÷ memory bandwidth**, full stop. Activation quantisation cannot improve that term — the activation is a single row, its traffic is negligible next to the weight matrix it multiplies, and the multiply itself is not the bottleneck, so running it on a faster arithmetic unit changes nothing measurable. Atom's fused-GEMM win is a large-batch phenomenon and does not transfer. Judged piece by piece:

- **KV-cache quantisation — SURVIVES, and is Atom's batch-1 legacy.** Attention at batch 1 is also a GEMV-shaped, memory-bound operation, but its traffic is the *KV cache*, which grows with context while weight traffic is constant. Every byte removed from the KV cache is a byte not streamed on every decode step, and capacity removed is context bought — on a 6 GB card running an 8B-class model, the corpus's worked arithmetic (`memory/kv-cache-design.md`) shows KV precision, not weight precision, is what decides whether an 8K context fits at all. Atom's specific contributions here — asymmetric quantisation per head, dequantisation fused into the attention kernel, and the empirical finding that INT4 KV costs only +0.12 perplexity — are exactly the shape the local runner needs, and they converge with the independent KV line (KIVI/KVQuant, wave 3) rather than competing with it. **This is the one piece of Atom that survives the batch-1 collapse intact.**
- **Group-quantised weights — SURVIVES, but it was never Atom's to give.** Group-128 scales at 4 bits is, structurally, the GGUF K-quant shape the local ecosystem already ships: K-quants group weights into super-blocks of 256 with sub-block scales that are themselves quantised (two levels of scale metadata — see `runners/llamacpp-ggml.md`). Atom's ablation independently confirms the design from the serving side: grouping is worth ~5 perplexity points at W4A4, the second-largest single term. The convergence is the finding — a datacenter serving system and a local weight-only ecosystem, optimising for opposite bottlenecks, arrived at grouped 4-bit weights with hierarchical scales. For the local runner this is validation of the incumbent default, not a new feature.
- **Mixed-precision outlier retention (128 channels in INT8) — DOES NOT SURVIVE as designed.** Its purpose is to make *activation* quantisation accurate; at batch 1 there is no activation quantisation to protect. Its weight-side echo does exist locally — K-quant recipes already promote sensitive tensors (classically parts of `attn_v` and `ffn_down`) to Q6_K, which is mixed precision by tensor role rather than by channel — but that is the ecosystem's own prior invention, and Atom's channel-level machinery (calibration-fitted outlier sets, runtime reordering) buys a weight-only runner nothing it does not already have cheaper.
- **Dynamic activation quantisation and the reorder/fusion apparatus — MOOT at batch 1.** They exist to hide the overhead of operations a batch-1 weight-only runner never performs. Their transferable residue is a design ethic, not a mechanism: *any* quantisation side-work must be fused into an operator that already runs, or it will be paid for in the only currency batch-1 decode has — latency on the critical path.

### (b) The build campaign's quant provisioning (IDEA-LEDGER #42 lineage)

IDEA-LEDGER #42 provisions weight quants **by lineage, not by bit count** — an artifact's family predicts its failure modes. Atom extends the ledger's map with a family the ledger's scalar/codebook/rotation taxonomy did not yet name distinctly: the **serving-system family** (Atom, and QServe after it), whose artifacts are not files but *format + kernel + scheduler bundles*. Labelled judgment, for provisioning by device class:

- **Datacenter / batched-serving device class:** Atom-lineage W4A4 is a legitimate provisioning choice *only as a whole system* — the artifact to provision is the serving stack that implements its fusions, never a "W4A4 model file" in isolation. A W4A4 checkpoint divorced from Atom-class kernels defaults to dequantise-then-FP16 execution and inherits the worst of both worlds (4-bit accuracy cost, no low-bit compute). Provisioning rule: for this family, the unit of inventory is (format, kernel library, GPU generation) — Atom's own numbers are RTX 4090 numbers; they do not transfer across GPU generations by courtesy.
- **Personal / batch-1 device class (the 6 GB target):** do not provision Atom-lineage artifacts at all. Provision weight-only grouped 4-bit (GGUF K-quant family) for weights and a separately-chosen KV precision (q8_0 default, sub-8-bit tiers per #19/#20) — which is precisely Atom decomposed into the two pieces that survive §6(a), each sourced from the family that does it best locally. The ledger's rule bites here: "W4A4" and "W4 weights + q4 KV" can describe the same nominal bit budget while being entirely different goods with different failure modes; provisioning by the label would conflate them.
- **Calibration provenance becomes a provisioning field.** Atom's outlier set is fitted on 128 WikiText-2 sentences. Any serving-family artifact carries fitted state (outlier sets, clipping factors, reorder indices) whose quality is bounded by its calibration data — the ledger's #34 grading discipline (grade on data the quantiser never saw) applies to procurement as much as to building: an Atom-class artifact should be provisioned with its calibration corpus named, or graded as unprovenanced.
