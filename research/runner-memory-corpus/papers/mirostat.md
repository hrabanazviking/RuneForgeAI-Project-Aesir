## 2. The problem as framed

The paper opens from a dissatisfaction with the entire top-$k$ / top-$p$ / temperature family: these methods "generate high-quality text after parameter tuning" but the tuning is **ad hoc** — it depends on the model and on the length of text to be generated — and, worse, "not much is known about the control they provide over the statistics of the output." A user sets $k$ or $p$ without any principled way to know what property of the resulting text they have actually chosen.

The paper then builds the variable it argues *should* be chosen: **perplexity** (equivalently, cross-entropy rate, equivalently average surprise — surprise of a token being $-\log P_M(x \mid x_{<i})$ under the model, perplexity $= 2^{\text{cross-entropy}}$). Three published/experimental planks support it:

1. **Human quality peaks at intermediate perplexity.** Citing large-scale human evaluation (Zhang et al., 2020), the paper notes human-judged quality is highest for a specific range of likelihoods — text that is neither too predictable nor too surprising. If quality lives in a perplexity band, a decoder that can *hold* perplexity in that band controls quality directly.
2. **Repetition is a function of cross-entropy, not of the sampler.** The paper's experiments (§4) find percentage repetition falls near-linearly as observed cross-entropy rises, and that this curve is "almost independent of the sampling method but slightly dependent on the model used" — fix the model and the cross-entropy, and you have fixed the repetition rate, whichever sampler got you there.
3. **Fixed-parameter samplers drift with length — the two traps.** For low $k$ / $p$, observed perplexity *drops* as generation proceeds, correlated with excessive repetition: the **boredom trap**. For large $k$ / $p$, perplexity *rises* with length, correlated with incoherence: the **confusion trap**. Human-written text, by contrast, settles to a roughly constant cross-entropy rate. A fixed knob therefore cannot hold a long generation in the quality band even if it starts there — the statistic the knob indirectly controls walks away. This length-drift argument, not any single-step defect, is the paper's core motivation for closed-loop control.

The theoretical groundwork is **Zipf's law**: word frequencies follow $p(i; s, N) \propto 1/i^{s}$ with $s$ close to 1 for human language. Under Zipfian statistics the paper proves its §3 results: cross-entropy grows **approximately linearly in $p$** for top-$p$ sampling, but **nonlinearly in $k$** for top-$k$ — steep for small $k$, saturating at large $k$ (little gained beyond $k \approx 2000$ in its analysis). Temperature enters the same framework as a rescaling of the Zipf exponent, $s \to s/T$. These results explain *why* the incumbent knobs are such poor control surfaces — one is a near-linear dial for the wrong quantity, the other is a saturating one — and they supply the formula mirostat's controller inverts.

## 3. Core mechanism in our own words

Mirostat is a **feedback controller wrapped around top-$k$ sampling**. The user supplies one meaningful number: a target surprise $\tau$ (target cross-entropy rate; target perplexity is $2^{\tau}$). The controller's job is to keep the observed surprise of the generated text at $\tau$, token by token, for a generation of any length.

The state is a single scalar, $\mu$ — interpretable as the current maximum-surprise budget — initialised at $\mu = 2\tau$. Each step (the paper's Algorithm 1):

1. **Estimate the distribution's Zipf exponent** $\hat{s}$ from the current step's top probabilities (the paper fits it over the top $m = 100$ tokens).
2. **Invert the §3 theory to get $k$:** choose the top-$k$ width whose predicted surprise matches the current budget,
$$k = \left(\frac{\hat{\epsilon}\, 2^{\mu}}{1 - N^{-\hat{\epsilon}}}\right)^{1/\hat{s}}, \qquad \hat{\epsilon} = \hat{s} - 1,$$
where $N$ is the vocabulary size. (The $\mu = 2\tau$ initialisation, rather than $\tau$, is because $k$ is computed from the surprise *at the truncation boundary*, not the mean surprise of the pool; the paper notes this "works well in practice, and the rest is taken care of by feedback.")
3. **Sample** the next token $X$ by ordinary top-$k$ sampling with that $k$.
4. **Measure the error:** $e = \mathfrak{S}(X) - \tau$ — how much more (or less) surprising the actually-chosen token was than the target.
5. **Update the budget:** $\mu \leftarrow \mu - \eta \, e$, where $\eta$ is the **learning rate**. A too-surprising token ($e > 0$) shrinks $\mu$, narrowing the next pool; a too-predictable token widens it. Then loop.

Two properties deserve emphasis. First, the controlled quantity is a *running statistic of the output*, not a shape parameter of one step's distribution — mirostat is the only member of the classic sampler family with memory, and that memory is exactly what lets it resist the length-drift of §2: if the text starts sliding toward repetition (surprise falling), the controller keeps widening the pool until surprise returns to target. Second, the paper's appendix gives **Mirostat 2 (Algorithm 2)**, a distribution-free variant: sort tokens by surprise, truncate all tokens whose surprise exceeds $\mu$, renormalise, sample, and apply the *same* update $\mu \leftarrow \mu - \eta e$. It drops the Zipf fit and the $k$ inversion entirely — $\mu$ becomes a direct surprise ceiling — at the cost of a sort whose complexity depends on target and vocabulary. The paper reports the two algorithms performing "almost identically" on cross-entropy control and repetition, and chose Algorithm 1 for its human experiments on constant-overhead grounds. The distinction matters for §5: the mirostat that ships in llama.cpp-class runners as "Mirostat 2.0" is this appendix algorithm, not the main text's.

One honest limitation is in the paper itself: control saturates for high target surprise (above ≈5 in its GPT-2 experiments), because the algorithm only truncates the tail — the untruncated model's own baseline surprise is around five, and pushing observed surprise *above* baseline would require truncating probable tokens too, "which would reduce the quality of the generated text, hence not considered here."

## 4. Reported results — as reported, with context

All experiments are on **GPT-2 (117M parameters)** unless stated — a 2020-era model, which bounds how far the numbers transfer (§5, §6). Reported in the paper's frame:

- **Control accuracy.** Plotting observed cross-entropy against the knob for each method (4 texts × 200 tokens per setting): top-$k$'s observed cross-entropy rises steeply then saturates in $k$ (matching the theory), top-$p$'s rises near-linearly in $p$, and both fluctuate substantially at any fixed setting. Mirostat's observed cross-entropy tracks its target $\tau$ "with low variance for surprise values less than five" — the paper's central empirical claim is this *controllability*, not a quality score.
- **Repetition law.** Across samplers, temperatures, and $n$-gram orders, percentage repetition is a near-linear decreasing function of observed cross-entropy rate, essentially one curve per model. Sentence-level ($n$-gram, larger $n$) repetition falls to ≈0 beyond a cross-entropy threshold (≈2.5–3 for GPT-2; 6-gram repetition vanishes above ≈3) — hence the paper's practical corollary: "setting target perplexity value beyond a threshold yields negligible sentence-level repetitions." GPT-2 XL (1558M) shows slightly less repetition at the same cross-entropy than GPT-2 (117M); CTRL shows the same law with a model-dependent offset.
- **Trap avoidance.** In 900-token generations (10 samples averaged), small-$k$/$p$ runs' cross-entropy decays into the boredom trap and large-$k$/$p$ runs' climbs into the confusion trap, while mirostat's "converges to the target cross-entropy within a few tokens and maintains the desired value of cross-entropy for long texts."
- **Human evaluation.** 300-token GPT-2 continuations at $\tau \in \{2.5, 3, 4, 5\}$ via mirostat and top-$p$, plus a human-written continuation, rated blind by **43 participants** (University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign and IIT Kanpur) on 1–7 Likert scales for fluency, coherence and overall quality. Texts at **$\tau = 3$ received the best ratings on all three axes**, and at $\tau = 3$ more than half the raters mistook the machine text for human-written. Crucially, at a *given* observed cross-entropy, mirostat and top-$p$ texts scored similarly — the paper's own reading is that the win comes from being able to *hit and hold* the good perplexity value, not from any per-token magic. Its stated takeaway: "mirostat with target surprise around 3.0 produces varying lengths of high-quality texts with minimal repetitions."
- **Repetition penalty interaction (negative result, reported as such).** A repetition penalty does lower repetition at fixed cross-entropy, but repetition has "no good relation" with the penalty strength $\theta$, so the penalty cannot be used to *target* a repetition level — reinforcing the paper's thesis that cross-entropy is the controllable variable.

Context a modern reader must add: the target that won, $\tau \approx 3$, is a GPT-2-at-117M number. The runner-world default that descended from this work is $\tau = 5.0$ (§5) — practitioners re-centred the band for larger, better-calibrated models, exactly as the paper's "slightly dependent on the model used" caveat predicts. Treat 3.0 as evidence that an optimum band *exists and is findable*, not as a transferable constant.

## 5. What it changed in practice

- **It put a stateful controller into the standard sampler menu.** Mirostat ships in llama.cpp in both forms — `mirostat = 1` (the paper's Algorithm 1 lineage) and `mirostat = 2` (the appendix's Algorithm 2, "Mirostat 2.0", the recommended/stable one in current guides) — with parameters `--mirostat-ent` ($\tau$, default **5.00**) and `--mirostat-lr` ($\eta$, default **0.10**). Ollama exposes the same surface as `mirostat` (0 = off, 1, 2), `mirostat_tau` (5.0) and `mirostat_eta` (0.1). In the KoboldCpp canon documented by this corpus (`runners/koboldcpp.md` §4.1), mirostat is listed as the sampler that *"overrides the other samplers; dynamically adjusts selection to hold perplexity near a target (type / tau 5.0 / eta 0.1); targets the 'boredom trap' (repetitive) and 'perplexity trap' (incoherent) simultaneously"*, settable per generation through the API.
- **Its chain position is *replacement*, not *slot*.** This is the sharpest practical contrast with min-p (companion dossier, `papers/min-p-sampling.md`). llama.cpp's documented behaviour is that when mirostat is enabled, top-$k$, nucleus (top-$p$) and locally-typical samplers are **ignored** — mirostat *is* the truncation mechanism, ending the chain by selecting the token itself (in llama.cpp's implementation it replaces the final `dist` sampler rather than filtering before it). The documented guidance in sampler guides is correspondingly exclusive: enable mirostat *instead of* the filter stack (top-p = 1.0, top-k = 0, min-p = 0.0), not in addition to it. Where min-p composes, mirostat substitutes — because a feedback controller whose pool is pre-truncated by other filters is measuring a statistic it no longer controls.
- **Its honest adoption verdict is "available everywhere, default nowhere."** Unlike min-p, mirostat never became a default: it remains an opt-in mode for long-form generation in every runner that carries it. The reasons are visible in the paper's own frame — the optimum $\tau$ is model-dependent, the controller adds per-token state and a feedback transient ("within a few tokens" to converge), and its evidence base is a 117M model. Its durable legacy is arguably conceptual as much as algorithmic: the boredom/confusion-trap vocabulary and the idea that a sampler should hold an *output statistic* constant over length are now standard ways persona-chat users diagnose long-session degradation, and the Min-p paper itself benchmarks mirostat as a named baseline (scoring it poorly on modern-model creative writing — 16.69% / 14.23% win rates in `papers/min-p-sampling.md` §4 — a result about cross-era transfer, recorded there with its context).

## 6. Applicability

### (a) Ollama build — IDEA-LEDGER #37, the sampler pipeline

*Recommendations in this subsection are engineering judgment, argued from the paper and the corpus's canon dossier (`runners/koboldcpp.md` §§4, 9.2) and marked as such.*

- **Implement mirostat as an exclusive mode, not a chain stage.** The #37 pipeline should model it the way llama.cpp does: a request either runs the filter chain (penalties → DRY → truncators → XTC → temperature) *or* runs mirostat (penalties/DRY may still precede it, since they edit logits rather than truncate by statistic — judgment, and it must be tested, not assumed). An API that lets a user set `mirostat = 2` and `min_p = 0.1` simultaneously and silently honours only one is the exact silent-behaviour class this corpus repeatedly flags in Ollama's current surface; the fork should reject or loudly resolve conflicting sampler modes in the response metadata.
- **Ship Mirostat 2 (Algorithm 2) first.** It needs no Zipf fit, no vocabulary-size term, and its state is one float ($\mu$) plus the update rule — a small, exactly-testable Go implementation. Defaults (judgment, following runner practice rather than the paper's GPT-2 optimum): `mirostat_tau = 5.0`, `mirostat_eta = 0.1`, exposed per request; label $\tau$ in the UI as *target surprise* with the paper's band logic explained ("too low → repetition, too high → incoherence; the right value is model-dependent") instead of presenting 5.0 as a universal constant.
- **Scope it to where its evidence points: long persona sessions.** Mirostat's one demonstrated superpower is holding a statistic steady over hundreds of tokens where fixed knobs drift into the traps — precisely the 200-turn persona-chat failure mode #37 exists to fix. Offer it as a named preset ("long-session / anti-drift") rather than a general default; for short factual generation the stateless min-p chain is simpler, stateless across requests, and better evidenced on modern models.
- **State discipline is the implementation risk.** $\mu$ is per-generation state: it must live in the session/generation object, reset at generation start ($\mu = 2\tau$), never leak across requests sharing a resident runner, and be included in any seed-reproducibility story (#37's conformance habit: fixed seed + fixed prompt must reproduce a mirostat run exactly, which requires logging $\tau$, $\eta$ and the initial $\mu$ alongside the seed).

### (b) Project Aesir

At the level of the corpus's Aesir material (`aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`) and the programme's sampling-parity slice **QS-4** as referenced in the commission: mirostat is the parity item that cannot be graded statelessly. Min-p (companion dossier) can be verified distribution-by-distribution; mirostat's output at token $t$ depends on the entire surprise history through $\mu$, so QS-4 test vectors must be *full recorded generations* — fixed model, prompt, seed, $\tau$, $\eta$ — compared token-for-token over hundreds of steps, because a subtly wrong update (sign error in $e$, $\mu$ initialised at $\tau$ instead of $2\tau$, error computed from averaged rather than instantaneous surprise — the paper's Algorithm 3 shows that last variant performing measurably worse) produces fluent text that merely drifts, the hardest divergence class to catch. Two further Aesir-specific notes: (i) the controller's state interacts with resident sessions and prefix snapshots — a restored prefix must restore $\mu$ with it, or the resumed generation restarts the feedback transient mid-conversation; $\mu$ belongs in the session snapshot key/state alongside the KV material. (ii) Aesir's accelerator-first design should note mirostat's per-step cost honestly: Algorithm 2's sort-by-surprise is over the vocabulary each token — trivial beside a 7B forward pass on an accelerator, but it is a synchronising, distribution-wide operation, and Algorithm 1's top-100 Zipf fit exists precisely to bound that cost; the choice between them in Aesir should be measured, not inherited.
