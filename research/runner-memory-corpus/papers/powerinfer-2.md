## 2. The problem as framed

A smartphone is the most constrained serious inference target in the corpus: the paper's motivating contrast is that industry on-device offerings are small language models (its example: Gemini Nano at 3.25B parameters, <2 GB) precisely because nothing larger fits or runs acceptably — while the reasons to want the model *on* the phone (instant assistance with no network latency, privacy through local processing, offline reliability) argue for the largest model the device can be made to serve. PowerInfer-2's goal is stated as a capability claim: fast inference for LLMs **exceeding the device's memory capacity**, up to 47B parameters.

The paper refuses the obvious port. PowerInfer-1 and LLMFlash work on PCs; the paper measures what happens when PC-style systems meet phone silicon and names two hardware gaps (§2.3–2.4 of the paper):

- **The sparse-computing gap.** A phone SoC's fast unit is its **NPU** — on the test devices, Qualcomm Hexagon — which is excellent at *dense* matrix work and has no efficient path for *unstructured sparse* work: the authors' own sparse primitives on the NPU run **slower than on the CPU**, the exact reverse of the PC GPU situation. The phone's **CPU** is the flexible unit — big/mid cores with NEON handle sparse matrix–vector work well — but it is slow at dense work and, alone, under-uses the memory system. The mobile GPU is measured slowest of the three for matrix–vector work and is needed for rendering, so the design excludes it. **Neither fast-but-rigid nor flexible-but-slow serves an LLM alone**; and a sparsity-based system (PowerInfer's whole premise) naively ported forces all its sparse work onto the CPU and leaves the NPU — the device's dense engine and its widest memory client — idle. Measured consequence of the naive port, with 50% of FFN weights offloaded to flash: PowerInfer decode collapses from 12.4 to **1.4 tok/s**, with I/O at 81.9% of latency (LLMFlash: 12.9 → 2.3 tok/s, I/O 76.7%).
- **The storage gap.** Below DRAM sits **UFS flash**, a far worse disk than PC NVMe: UFS 4.0 sequential reads reach ~4 GB/s only at 512 KB blocks and fall to ~450 MB/s at 4 KB; random-read IOPS are ~12× below NVMe (100K vs 1,200K as reported); random performance degrades as the read range grows; throughput depends on which CPU core drives the I/O (4 KB random reads: big core 1,076 MB/s vs little core 762 MB/s); and UFS's **single command queue** means parallel I/O from multiple cores can *lose* up to 40%. Any design that treats flash as a paging backing store at matrix granularity will spend its life waiting on it — which is the measured fate above.
- **A third, dynamic complication: sparsity is not a constant of the model.** The paper's Figure 2 measurement (Bamboo-7B, layer 10) shows the highly-activated neuron share rising from **<1% at batch 1 to ~75% at batch 32**, because a neuron counts as activated if *any* token in the batch fires it — and modern phone workloads create internal batches dynamically (Best-of-N sampling, MCTS, sequences finishing at different times and shrinking the effective batch mid-generation). A static hot/cold split tuned for one batch size is silently wrong at every other. The split itself must be a runtime variable.

The framing in one sentence: the phone offers three mismatched resources — a dense-only accelerator, a sparse-capable but slow CPU, and a flash tier that punishes small, random, or parallel access — and an LLM whose sparsity pattern changes shape as the batch breathes; the system must be designed around all four facts at once.

## 3. Core mechanism in our own words

**The neuron cluster — the one abstraction everything else hangs on.** PowerInfer-2 decomposes matrix operations into **neuron clusters**: groups of neurons from a single FFN layer with similar activation behaviour, whose size is determined adaptively from both the activation statistics and the target compute unit. Large, frequently-activated ("hot") clusters are materialised as **dense matrices**; small, rarely-activated ("cold") clusters stay as individually managed sparse units. The cluster is simultaneously the unit of *computation scheduling* (what goes to NPU vs CPU), of *storage layout* (how weights sit in flash), of *caching* (what the in-DRAM cache holds and evicts), and of *I/O pipelining* (the granularity at which compute and loading overlap). The reason clusters — not neurons, not matrices — are the right grain is the NPU: a single neuron is a sparse object the NPU cannot execute, and a whole matrix is an I/O object UFS cannot feed; a cluster of co-behaving hot neurons, pre-packed, is a *dense* object the NPU executes natively, and a cluster-sized transfer is large enough to pipeline against compute. **The sparsity grain is matched to the accelerator's grain** — that matching, not any single kernel, is the paper's core move.

**The division of labour (the "polymorphic" / adaptive neuron engine).** The engine runs different strategies per phase, because the phases have opposite sparsity shapes:

- *Prefill — NPU-centric, dense.* A prompt's tokens jointly activate nearly everything (the paper reports activation probability approaching 99.99% at batch 128 for TurboSparse-Mixtral), so prediction is pointless and sparsity is absent: all neurons merge into effectively one large cluster, the NPU runs dense matrix work layer by layer, whole layers are pulled from flash with large **sequential** reads, and a dedicated **big CPU core prefetches the next layer** while the NPU computes the current one — I/O reported fully hidden behind compute in the measured layers.
- *Decode — hybrid, split per matrix.* **Attention heads are partitioned across CPU and NPU** and computed in parallel (attention is dense but small). Each **FFN matrix is split along the neuron dimension**: hot clusters to the NPU as dense matmul; cold neurons to the CPU, gated by the PowerInfer-lineage **predictor** (only predicted-active cold neurons are computed), executed with ARM NEON kernels in chunks (e.g. 512 rows) across cores; partial results are merged on the CPU side. In the paper's worked example (a 14,336×4,096 FFN matrix), batch 1 splits **50/50**; as batch grows and activation densifies, the NPU share rises to **~70% / CPU ~30%**.
- *Dynamic re-splitting under a static-graph NPU.* NPUs execute precompiled static graphs, so changing the split means loading a different graph. The offline planner pre-generates a family of NPU graphs (one per batch-size / hot-ratio point; each ~10 KB), and at runtime the engine **loads the next graph asynchronously during attention computation**, activating it immediately after — reconfiguration with no stall, and the paper's concrete answer to "static shapes vs dynamic sparsity": precompile the *set* of shapes the dynamics can take, and switch among compiled artefacts at runtime. (The corpus's NPU dossier, §7 below, is why this pattern matters beyond phones.)
- *Why run both units at once even when one could do the work:* unified memory. On the OnePlus 12 with a 7B model, measured matmul bandwidth is 43.9 GB/s CPU-only, 56 GB/s NPU-only, and **59.6 GB/s with both engaged** — heterogeneous execution literally widens the shared memory pipe, so the hybrid split buys bandwidth as well as compute.

**The models: TurboSparse lineage.** PowerInfer-2's sparsity is not found, it is *supplied* — by the same lab's TurboSparse work (Song et al. 2024, arXiv:2406.05955, the paper's reference [52] / models' source). TurboSparse re-trains Mistral-7B and Mixtral-8×7B with a **dReLU** activation (ReLU applied to both branches of the gated FFN unit, so a unit survives only if both its gate and up pre-activations are positive) plus a curated sparsification data mixture, reaching ~90% average FFN sparsity for TurboSparse-Mistral-7B and ~85% per-expert for TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B in the TurboSparse paper's own reporting (see `runners/powerinfer.md` §2.6 for the audit). PowerInfer-2's evaluation models are TurboSparse-Mistral-7B, TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B, Bamboo-7B, sparse LLaMA-13B, sparse Qwen2-7B, and a SiLU Mistral-7B as the control (§4). The dependency is load-bearing and must travel with every headline number: **the 47B-at-11.68 tok/s result is a result about a TurboSparse-re-trained Mixtral** (47B total, ~3B activated per token by PowerInfer-2's own accounting, compounding MoE routing sparsity with neuron sparsity), not about an arbitrary 47B checkpoint.

**Storage engine — cache, pipeline, loading.** Three mechanisms, all at cluster/neuron grain:

- *Segmented neuron cache (in DRAM).* Three regions: a **fixed region** (attention weights + KV cache, pinned for the session), a **hot region** (clusters held as dense matrices, LRU eviction at *cluster* granularity), and a **cold region** (individual neurons, LRU at *neuron* granularity). Eviction is free — weights are read-only, so an evicted entry is simply dropped, never written back. The hot/cold boundary itself moves with batch size. Reported cache hit rate in the ablation: ~95%.
- *Cluster-level I/O pipeline.* Matrix-level double-buffering still idles cores whenever any cluster of the current matrix is stuck in flash. PowerInfer-2 breaks the matrix barrier: each cluster's work is decomposed into five stages — predict → Gate I/O → Gate compute → Up/Down I/O → Up/Down compute — spread over multiple compute threads plus **one dedicated I/O thread** (a deliberate consequence of UFS's single queue: one issuer, big core). A core that finishes a cluster immediately takes an *in-memory cluster from the next matrix* while the I/O thread fetches the missing ones. In the paper's critical-path breakdown this drops I/O's share of decode time from 76.7% (LLMFlash) to **13.7%**.
- *Differentiated loading.* Attention and hot weights: sequential reads, hot weights asynchronously preloaded during the preceding attention block, with the planner sizing the hot set so the prefetch provably fits inside attention compute time at the device's measured I/O bandwidth. Cold weights: small random reads, but **bundled across matrices** — the Gate/Up/Down rows for the same neuron index co-activate ~80% of the time (while distinct cold neurons within one matrix co-activate <20% once hot neurons are removed), so flash layout is organised **by neuron index across the three matrices** rather than by matrix. For FP16 a bundle is one 24 KB read; for INT4 the bundle is ~7.5 KB, padded to 8 KB and split into **two 4 KB reads** (measured faster on UFS), with a two-phase refinement: load Gate first, fetch Up/Down only if the gate output is actually non-zero — declining to spend I/O on the ~20% of bundles that do not fully co-activate.
- *Quantisation for the NPU.* NPUs of this class do not support the group-wise quantisation llama.cpp uses; plain per-channel INT4 (QNN's approach) loses heavily on outlier weights in the paper's accuracy table. PowerInfer-2 uses a hybrid: outlier weights in INT8, the remainder INT4 per-channel (AWQ-inspired, as the paper describes it) — accuracy near llama.cpp's at NPU-compatible shapes (§4).

**Offline planner.** Before any inference: profile activation frequencies over **10M+ tokens** (Wikipedia + RefinedWeb) at batch sizes 1–4 and beyond; profile the specific device (NPU throughput by matrix size, CPU sparse speed, bandwidth under access patterns, UFS behaviour by block size and issuing core); then emit per-batch-size execution plans — cluster assignments, cache-region ratios, the pre-generated NPU graphs, thread/core assignments (including reserving the big core for I/O). Device-specificity is a designed feature: the plan is compiled *for this phone*, the way the corpus's NPU dossier describes compiled artefacts generally. Implementation: ~12,000 added lines of C++ on PowerInfer-1, running on **rooted Android** (it uses privileged APIs such as `mlock`); the paper notes no kernel modifications are required.

## 4. Reported results — as reported, with hardware context

All figures are the paper's own (arXiv v3 text, read 2026-11-15); nothing is re-derived. Baselines: llama.cpp, MLC-LLM, Qualcomm QNN (commercial NPU engine), and the authors' reimplementation of LLMFlash (not open-sourced; rebuilt from its paper inside llama.cpp with its sparsity prediction, row-column bundling, and neuron caching).

- **Testbeds (Table 3):** **OnePlus 12** — Snapdragon **8 Gen 3**, 24 GB DRAM / **19 GB** usable, **UFS 4.0**; **OnePlus Ace 2** — Snapdragon **8+ Gen 1**, 16 GB DRAM / **11 GB** usable, **UFS 3.1**. Models: Mistral(SiLU)-7B, sparse Qwen2-7B, Bamboo-7B, sparse LLaMA-13B, TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B. Default offload setting: **50% of FFN weights in flash** (75% for Mixtral-47B on the Ace 2). Workloads: multi-turn dialogue, code generation, math, role-play; decode measured with ≤64-token prompts and up to 1,024 output tokens, averaged over 10 runs.
- **Headline (abstract / §7.2.1):** **first system to serve a 47B LLM on a smartphone** — TurboSparse-Mixtral-47B at **11.68 tokens/s** on the OnePlus 12 (at full 19 GB availability; see the memory-scaling point below for the same model at other budgets, and §3 above for what "47B" activates per token). Decode speedups on OnePlus 12: average **24.6× (up to 27.8×) over llama.cpp**, average **3.84× (up to 4.63×) over LLMFlash**. On OnePlus Ace 2: **14.1×** over llama.cpp and **2.93×** over LLMFlash on average. In the 50%-offload figure configuration, Mixtral-47B decode is reported at **9.96 tok/s** — the 11.68 figure is the full-memory configuration; the two numbers describe different cache budgets and must not be merged.
- **Memory scaling (Mixtral-47B, OnePlus 12, §7.2.3):** at only **7 GB** available, **2.13 tok/s** — the budget there is instructive: ~1 GB non-FFN weights (embeddings, attention, KV, LM head), **2.6 GB predictor weights**, **2.7 GB INT4 quantisation scales** for the FFN, 0.3 GB runtime, leaving **0.4 GB of neuron cache (1.8% of FFN weights)**. Speed then scales roughly linearly with memory to 11.68 tok/s at 19 GB (3.12× LLMFlash, 21.2× llama.cpp at that point). The lesson inside the budget: at small memories the *auxiliary* state (predictors + scales) rivals the weights it serves — the same predictor-tax warning the runner dossier prices for PowerInfer-1.
- **Prefill (§7.2.2):** with 512-token prompts on OnePlus 12, up to **48.97× over LLMFlash, 44.23× over llama.cpp, 1.99× over QNN**; in-memory prefill exceeds **700 tok/s**, comparable to QNN (the paper's §2.3.1 NPU measurement for a 7B INT4 model: NPU 770 tok/s vs GPU 37 vs CPU 8 in prefill). With 50% FFN offloaded, prefill holds 404.6 tok/s. Prefill is where the NPU-centric design pays most — dense, batched, sequential-I/O-shaped work is exactly the NPU's home regime.
- **In-memory decode (Bamboo-7B, §7.3):** **2.24× over llama.cpp, 2.48× over MLC-LLM, 1.86× over QNN** with everything resident; and with 50% of FFN weights offloaded to flash it uses **1.5 GB (40%) less memory** than the in-memory frameworks at comparable speed — the paper's "≈40% memory reduction at matched speed" claim for models that fit.
- **Ablation (Bamboo-7B decode, OnePlus 12, 50% offloaded, §7.5):** CPU-only baseline **0.4 tok/s** → +bundled loading **1.1** → +neuron cache **4.181** (a 2.82× step, at a ~95% hit rate) → +cluster-level pipeline **9.60** → +XPU co-processing **11.07 tok/s**. I/O share of the critical path: **13.7%** for PowerInfer-2 vs **76.7%** for LLMFlash (Table 4).
- **The activation-family control (§7.2.5) — reported honestly by the paper and preserved here:** Mistral(**SiLU**)-7B decode **5.3 tok/s vs LLMFlash 2.18 (2.4×)**, against Bamboo-7B's **11.1 vs 2.4 (4.6×)** over the same baseline. The mechanism's gains halve when the model's sparsity is the thresholded ~50% kind rather than the trained-in ReLU-family kind — the phone reproduction of PowerInfer-1's central caveat.
- **Consistency and jitter (§7.2.4):** task-level decode for Mixtral-47B stays **≥11.4 tok/s** across role-play / dialogue / math / code at full memory; but token-level latency spreads: P99 is **40.9% slower than the mean** for Mixtral-47B, driven by cache-miss variance (average miss rate 3.5%, **P99 miss rate 18.9%**). Sparsity systems have jitter as a native property; averages flatter them.
- **Best-of-N (§7.4):** on Bamboo-7B, Best-of-4, all-resident: PowerInfer-2 beats QNN by 1.84× and its own CPU-only variant by 1.28× at N=4, and retains a 1.42× advantage as sequences finish and N shrinks — the dynamic re-split (§3) earning its keep on a workload whose batch size changes mid-generation.
- **Accuracy (§7.6, OpenCompass, INT4):** average over ARC-Challenge / ARC-Easy / MMLU / GSM8K — Qwen2-7B: llama.cpp 79.25, **PowerInfer-2 78.38**, QNN 56.93. Bamboo-7B: llama.cpp 70.12, **PowerInfer-2 68.35**, QNN 63.26. The paper's claim is "comparable to llama.cpp, significantly above QNN," attributed to its hybrid outlier-INT8 quantisation vs QNN's per-channel INT4 — as reported, on these two models.
- **Energy (§7.7):** **0.257 J/token** vs QNN 0.373 and llama.cpp 0.672, at similar peak power (~5.1 W) — the saving is finishing sooner, not drawing less.

## 5. Position in the corpus

- **The sequel that changes the question.** `runners/powerinfer.md` establishes PowerInfer-1's law: activation sparsity is skewed (a small hot set carries most activations), so place hot work on the fast unit, compute cold work where it sits, and predict what will fire. PowerInfer-2 keeps every part of that law and changes the *hardware algebra* underneath it: the fast unit can no longer do sparse work at all, a third tier (flash) is slow enough to dominate if mishandled, and the batch — hence the sparsity itself — moves at runtime. Its answer, the neuron cluster, is best read as a **granularity translation**: PowerInfer-1's per-neuron logic re-expressed at the coarsest grain the rigid accelerator can still exploit. The corpus's activation-sparsity law therefore gains a rider from this paper: *sparsity is an activation-family property* (PowerInfer-1) **whose exploitable grain is an accelerator property** (PowerInfer-2). A sparsity scheme designed at the wrong grain for its silicon is not a slower version of the right scheme; on the NPU it is a non-scheme — the paper's sparse-on-NPU primitives lose to the CPU outright.
- **Cross-link to the NPU memory dossier — the compiled regime, again.** `memory/npu-memory-and-dataflows.md` (§6, "the compiled-blob regime") documents how NPU stacks converge on weights frozen into compiled artefacts, static shapes, and compile-time memory planning, because the hardware underneath converged first. PowerInfer-2 is that dossier's pattern operating *inside* a research system rather than a vendor stack: pre-generated static NPU graphs as the unit of deployment, an offline planner that profiles the specific device and compiles per-device, per-batch-size plans, and dynamic behaviour achieved by **switching among precompiled artefacts** rather than by runtime generality. Its twist on the pattern is the sparsity rider: the compiled artefacts are parameterised by *hot-cluster ratio*, so the static-shape regime is made to serve a dynamic-sparsity workload by enumerating the dynamics in advance. For the corpus's Hailo thread the lesson cuts both ways, and §6 states it carefully.
- **Sibling of FlexGen, opposite regime.** The companion dossier's system and this one are the wave's two poles: FlexGen tiers *dense* tensors across GPU/CPU/disk for *throughput* at batch ~10²; PowerInfer-2 tiers *sparse* neuron clusters across NPU/CPU/flash for *latency* at batch ~1–4. They share the deep moves — tier fractions decided by offline analysis of measured hardware, compute delegated to where data sits, I/O overlapped at the finest grain available — and differ in everything the regime dictates. That the same moves reappear at both poles is the wave's evidence they are regime-independent engineering, while the numbers at each pole are regime-bound and non-transferable.
- **Relation to LLMFlash and the flash-offload line.** PowerInfer-2's named rival is Apple's LLMFlash (row-column bundling + neuron caching for flash-resident weights); its margin over that rival comes from the two things bundling alone lacks — heterogeneous NPU/CPU execution and sub-matrix pipelining — which is why the ablation's largest single steps are the cache and the pipeline rather than the bundling it shares with its baseline.

## 6. Applicability

**(a) Phones are not fleet targets — what transfers is the grain lesson.** No fleet machine is a phone, no fleet roadmap runs models on a Snapdragon NPU, and none of §4's tokens/s figures describe any hardware Volmarr owns. The dossier's transferable content, labelled as transfer, is threefold:

1. *Match the sparsity grain to the accelerator's grain.* Any future sparse-execution work — on the fleet's GPUs today, on any NPU-class part tomorrow — must choose its unit of skipping (neuron, block, cluster, expert) by what the target executes natively, not by what the mathematics prefers. PowerInfer-2's cluster is the worked example: re-graining hot neurons into dense blocks converted an unusable accelerator into the fastest unit on the device. The same question, asked of a GPU block-sparse kernel or a Hailo dataflow mapping, has the same shape.
2. *Static-shape regimes can serve dynamic workloads by enumeration.* Pre-generating the small set of compiled graphs the dynamics can visit, and swapping them asynchronously in a compute shadow (here, during attention), is a general pattern for compiled-blob targets — directly relevant to any future Hailo/NPU work in this programme, where the NPU dossier records that the binding constraint met first is the toolchain's compiled catalogue, not the silicon. PowerInfer-2 is evidence *for* the Possibility Law's discipline in both directions: the static-graph limit was real, and it was a software-shape fact that engineering routed around — not a hardware verdict that NPUs cannot serve dynamic-sparsity models, and not proof that any given catalogue part will serve any given model either.
3. *Batch size is a sparsity variable.* Any static hot/cold policy — including the fleet runner's tiered-residency ideas (#29) — is silently a policy for one batch size. On the fleet that batch is 1 and stable, so the risk is low; but the moment a local runner batches (speculative verification spans, Best-of-N, parallel sampling), PowerInfer-2's Figure 2 measurement is the warning label: the hot set densifies toward 75% by batch 32, and a residency plan that assumed batch-1 sparsity will thrash.

**(b) What does not transfer.** The UFS-specific I/O engineering (single-queue discipline, 4 KB split reads, big-core I/O pinning) is priced for a storage tier an order of magnitude worse than the fleet's NVMe; the fleet's analogue problem (expert/weight streaming over PCIe) is already covered by the MoE-Infinity and FlexGen dossiers at the right tier prices. The predictor machinery inherits PowerInfer-1's supply-chain problem in full (per-model trained predictors, 2.6 GB of them in the 7 GB Mixtral budget) — the runner dossier's rejection (ledger R-series) stands unchanged by anything in this paper. And TurboSparse dependence means the headline capability (47B on a phone) is a property of *re-trained checkpoints from one lab's ecosystem*, not a serving feature a runner can offer for arbitrary models — the same detect-and-honour posture the corpus takes toward LayerSkip/NSA-class trained-in properties.
