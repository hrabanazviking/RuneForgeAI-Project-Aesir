## 2. The problem as framed

QuIP# works the extreme end of the wave: weight-only PTQ at ≤4 bits per weight, with the real contest at 3 and especially 2 bits — the regime where GPTQ needs small groups and still loses visibly, AWQ's heuristics (in the paper's words) "fall apart at even 2.15 bits" in a cited comparison, and OmniQuant produces unusable 2-bit models. At 2 bits a scalar quantizer has four levels per weight; the question is no longer how to round cleverly within a bad representation, but how to change the representation so that four-level-class storage can carry the information at all.

The paper's diagnosis, inherited from QuIP and made precise, is that scalar PTQ error is dominated by *coherence*: outliers and concentrated structure in both the weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$ and the proxy Hessian $\mathbf{H} = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^\top]$. Formally, a matrix is $\mu$-incoherent when no single coordinate carries outsized magnitude — for weights, $\max_{i,j} |W_{ij}| \le \mu \|\mathbf{W}\|_F / \sqrt{mn}$; for Hessians, when the eigenvector matrix has no large entries. When $\mathbf{W}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ are incoherent, adaptive rounding has *provably bounded* error; when a few coordinates are huge, those coordinates' rounding errors dominate the layer output error $\ell(\hat{\mathbf{W}}) = \mathrm{tr}((\hat{\mathbf{W}}-\mathbf{W})\mathbf{H}(\hat{\mathbf{W}}-\mathbf{W})^\top)$ and no rounding order rescues them. Heuristic outlier handling — grouping (which spends real bits on per-group scales: 0.25 extra bits/weight for an FP16 scale per 64 weights, a fortune at 2 bits) or keeping outliers in FP16 (unstructured, slow) — pays for the pathology instead of removing it.

A second, independent diagnosis concerns the *shape* of the codebook. Scalar quantization represents groups of weights by points on a hypercube grid. If the weight distribution can be made ball-shaped (sub-Gaussian), a hypercube wastes its corners and starves its centre: the representable set is shaped wrong for the source. At 4 bits the waste is affordable; at 2 bits it is not.

## 3. Core mechanism in our own words

QuIP# is three techniques stacked on the same layer-wise, Hessian-weighted proxy-loss framework that GPTQ established (it uses the LDLQ family of adaptive rounding, shown in the QuIP line of work to be equivalent to GPTQ's OPTQ formulation and optimal among linear-feedback adaptive rounding methods).

**1. Incoherence processing with the Randomized Hadamard Transform (RHT).** Before quantizing a layer, conjugate both the weights and the Hessian by structured random orthogonal transforms built from Hadamard matrices and random sign flips: $\hat{\mathbf{W}} = U S_U \,\mathrm{Had}(\ldots \mathbf{W} \ldots)\, S_V V^\top$ in the paper's Algorithm 3 form, with $\hat{\mathbf{H}}$ transformed by the $V$ side alone. Orthogonal conjugation preserves the proxy loss exactly, so the *function* is unchanged — but with high probability the transformed matrices are $\tilde{O}(1)$-incoherent: energy is spread evenly across coordinates, outliers cease to exist as coordinates, and the transformed weights approach a ball-shaped sub-Gaussian distribution. Compared with original QuIP's Kronecker-product transforms, the RHT (a) improves the incoherence bound from a log-squared to a log dependence on matrix size, (b) costs $O(n \log n)$ via the Fast Walsh-Hadamard Transform instead of $\Theta(n\sqrt{n})$, and (c) needs no floating-point multiplies at all, since Hadamard entries are ±1. Non-power-of-two dimensions are handled by factoring $n = pq$ with known Hadamard matrices (e.g. Llama-2-70B's intermediate dimension $28672 = 1024 \times 28$), with a Randomized Fast Fourier Transform variant as fallback (reported within 0.08 perplexity of RHT at 2 bits). At inference, the transforms are applied to activations on the fly ($y \leftarrow \mathrm{Had}(S_V \odot x)$, quantize-multiply, then $\mathrm{Had}(S_U \odot y)$); the two sign vectors cost under 0.01 bits/weight on LLM-sized matrices. Crucially, incoherence processing spends essentially *no* bits on outlier management — the saving that makes 2 bits arithmetically possible.

**2. Vector quantization with the E8 lattice codebook (E8P), via BlockLDLQ.** Instead of rounding weights one scalar at a time, QuIP# rounds *blocks* of 8 weights jointly to points of a codebook shaped like the ball the RHT produces. The codebook is built from the $E_8$ lattice — the arrangement achieving the optimal (densest) packing of unit balls in 8 dimensions — intersected with a ball and padded ("E8P"). The engineering trick that makes this practical: by exploiting $E_8$'s sign/parity symmetries, a codebook of $2^{16}$ entries (8 dimensions × 2 bits) is decoded from a stored table of only $2^8 = 256$ absolute values (1 KiB — fits in GPU L1 cache even after 32× duplication for bank conflicts), with the remaining bits of each codeword carried as sign flips and a ±1/4 shift, decodable in under 5 instructions per weight in the paper's accounting. Rounding with a vector quantizer inside an LDLQ-style feedback loop required generalizing LDLQ to blocks: BlockLDLQ uses a block-LDL decomposition of the Hessian and rounds block by block, feeding the accumulated error of already-rounded blocks forward as linear feedback, with a provable error bound of the same improved form as scalar LDLQ (Theorem 4.1 of the paper). Higher bitrates (3–4 bits) use residual vector quantization on top of the same machinery in the paper's experiments.

**3. Fine-tuning to recover inter-layer fidelity.** Pure layer-wise PTQ ignores interactions between layers. QuIP# adds a comparatively cheap fine-tuning stage in the AQLM style: first, within each transformer block, still-unquantized parameters are tuned to compensate for already-quantized ones (parallelizable across blocks); then, after all layers are quantized, the remaining unquantized parameters — including the incoherence sign vectors, optimized as real vectors — are tuned end-to-end against activation error on a small development set. Reported cost: around 50 GPU-hours for a 70B model for the fine-tuning itself; end-to-end, Llama-2-70B quantizes in under 10 GPU-hours without fine-tuning and around 100 GPU-hours with it. The ablations (§4) show fine-tuning and the E8 codebook each matter most exactly where the method is aimed — at 2 bits their removal is catastrophic compared with their modest effect at 4 bits.

## 4. Reported results — as reported, with context

All figures are the paper's own. Perplexity is WikiText2 (and C4 where noted), lower is better; models are Llama 1 and Llama 2. Two context-length conventions appear in the paper (2048 and 4096) and are kept separate below, as the paper itself insists.

**Perplexity at context length 2048 (paper Table 2, selected rows).** FP16 references: Llama-2-7B 5.47, Llama-2-13B 4.88, Llama-2-70B 3.32.

| Bits | Method | Llama-2-7B | Llama-2-13B | Llama-2-70B |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | AWQ / OmniQuant / QuIP# | 6.15 / 5.74 / **5.56** | 5.12 / 5.02 / **4.95** | — / 3.47 / **3.38** |
| 3 | AWQ / OmniQuant / QuIP# | 24.0 / 6.58 / **5.79** | 10.5 / 5.58 / **5.10** | — / 3.92 / **3.56** |
| 2 | OmniQuant / QuIP# (no FT, no E8) / QuIP# | 37.4 / 12.3 / **6.66** | 17.2 / 7.60 / **5.74** | 7.81 / 4.87 / **4.16** |

Two structural findings the paper emphasizes: QuIP# *without* fine-tuning and *without* the lattice codebook already beats AWQ and OmniQuant at 2–3 bits — incoherence processing alone is doing heavy lifting — and full QuIP# at 2 bits (6.66 on Llama-2-7B) lands near OmniQuant's *3-bit* quality (6.58), a full bit of effective compression gained.

**Perplexity at context length 4096, with ablations (paper Table 4).** FP16: Llama-2-7B 5.12, 13B 4.57, 70B 3.12.
- 4-bit QuIP#: 5.19 / 4.63 / 3.18 — statistically at the FP16 line, comparable with AQLM (5.21 / 4.64 / 3.17 at ~4.0 bits).
- 3-bit QuIP#: 5.41 / 4.78 / 3.35, comparable to AQLM's 5.46 / 4.83 / 3.36 at ~3.0 bits.
- 2-bit QuIP#: **6.19 / 5.35 / 3.91**, versus AQLM 6.93 / 5.70 / 3.94 (at ~2.0 bits), QuIP (original) — / 13.5 / 5.90, and QuIP#'s own ablations: without fine-tuning 8.22 / 6.06 / 4.16; without the E8 codebook 11.2 / 7.04 / 4.58 (7B / 13B / 70B). At 2 bits, removing E8P costs ~5 perplexity points on the 7B model; removing fine-tuning costs ~2. At 4 bits both ablations cost only ~0.03–0.17. The machinery is priced for the extreme regime and is nearly free of benefit where scalar methods already work — read the method as a 2-bit technology with 3–4-bit competence, not the reverse.

**Zero-shot accuracy (paper Table 3, Llama-2, LM-Eval `acc`).** At 2 bits, QuIP# retains most of FP16 where predecessors collapse: Llama-2-70B ARC-Easy 77.3 (FP16 77.7) and PiQA 80.3 (81.1), versus OmniQuant-2bit 55.4 / 68.8; Llama-2-7B ARC-Easy 64.6 (FP16 69.3) versus OmniQuant 35.2. AQLM is the only comparable method and is generally at or slightly behind QuIP# at 2 bits, ahead nowhere decisively.

**The scaling claim.** The paper's headline conceptual result: QuIP# 3-bit models *scale better* (perplexity versus model size) than its own 4-bit models, and on Llama-2 outperform a "theoretical lossless 4-bit model" (an FP16 model costed at 4 bits/weight) — presented as the first time a 3-bit PTQ method has done this, directly contesting the earlier claim in the literature that 4 bits is the optimal PTQ operating point. QuIP# 2-bit models are reported to scale similarly to higher bitrates. In plain terms: if the scaling holds, spending a fixed memory budget on a *larger model at fewer bits* beats a smaller model at 4 bits — the opposite of the conservative advice the GPTQ-era results suggested for small models.

**Inference speed, in its original kernel context.** Proof-of-concept CUDA implementation on an NVIDIA RTX 4090: over 50% of peak memory bandwidth (>1 TB/s class) during generation for a 2-bit Llama-2-70B (56.84% of peak, 32.74 tokens/s with the FlashAttention-based harness; 2-bit 7B: 170.50 tokens/s at 29.6% of peak). In the Hugging Face-harness comparison (paper Table 6): Llama-2-7B at 2 bits — FP16 33.1 tok/s, AQLM 20.6 tok/s (slower than FP16, because its 1 MiB per-layer unstructured codebooks cannot fit L1 cache), QuIP# 106.3 tok/s; Llama-2-70B at 2 bits — FP16 OOM, AQLM 8.27 tok/s, QuIP# 25.9 tok/s. The speed claim is inseparable from the E8P codebook's 1 KiB footprint: the lattice structure is not an accuracy luxury, it is what makes vector quantization *fast enough to be worth doing*.

**Calibration, as reported:** proxy Hessians built from 6,144 sequences at the model's native context length from the RedPajama-1T dataset — a materially larger calibration investment than GPTQ's 128 segments or AWQ's small Pile sample, which is part of the method's true cost and must be quoted with its results.

## 5. What it changed in practice

QuIP# moved the credible frontier of weight-only PTQ from 3 bits to 2 bits, and — together with AQLM, its vector-quantization contemporary and closest competitor — established the design pattern for the extreme regime: *transform the weights into a distribution with no outliers and a known shape, then quantize vectors of weights against a codebook shaped to match, with Hessian feedback during rounding.* Scalar grids with per-group scales, the GPTQ/AWQ pattern, remain the right tool at 4 bits; below 3 bits, the literature's centre of gravity is incoherence processing plus codebooks.

Its practical lineage runs in two directions. In the research/serving world, QuIP# and AQLM define the sub-2-bit and 2-bit reference points against which newer extreme quantizers are graded, and QuIP#'s scaling result reopened the "bigger model, fewer bits" provisioning question. In the local-inference world, the *conceptual* descendants are the codebook-based extreme formats in GGUF's IQ family (IQ2/IQ1-class quantizers): learned/importance-informed codebooks over small weight vectors, paired with importance measures derived from activations, used to make ~2-bit and sub-2-bit local models usable at all. That connection should be stated carefully, as it is here: the IQ family is publicly documented as codebook-based vector quantization informed by the same body of work, but it is an independent implementation line (imatrix-based importance weighting, its own codebooks and kernels in llama.cpp), **not** a port of QuIP#, and no derivation of IQ results from QuIP#'s theorems should be claimed. What transfers is the design insight — at ~2 bits, codebook shape and outlier removal decide quality — not the specific transforms, codebooks, or guarantees.

## 6. Applicability

### (a) Ollama on a 6 GB card — which formats to prefer, and why

- **This is the wave's enabling paper for the 6 GB tier's most valuable trick: trading bits for model size.** A 13B-class model at ~2 bits occupies roughly the weight memory of a 7B at 4 bits. GPTQ-era evidence said don't — small models degrade disproportionately at low bits. QuIP#'s scaling evidence says a *properly built* 2–3-bit artifact of a larger model can beat the smaller 4-bit model outright. For Ollama provisioning on 6 GB, the actionable rule is conditional: prefer the larger-model/fewer-bits option **only when the artifact comes from the incoherence/codebook lineage** (QuIP#-class, AQLM-class, or GGUF IQ-class quantizers with activation-derived importance data) — never from scalar RTN/GPTQ-style quantization pushed below its regime. The method, not the bit count on the label, is the safety property.
- **At 3 bits, QuIP#-class artifacts are the quality leaders in this wave's evidence** (Llama-2-7B 5.79–5.41 depending on context convention, versus AWQ 24.0/6.24-class results in the respective tables and GPTQ 6.43) — but availability rules practice: 3-bit GGUF Q3/IQ3-class artifacts are what a local user can actually obtain, and they descend from mixed lineages. Grade the specific artifact (see §6b), don't buy the surname.
- **At 4 bits, QuIP# buys nothing worth its complexity for local use.** Its own ablations show the lattice/fine-tuning machinery contributing ~0.1 perplexity at 4 bits. GPTQ/AWQ/GGUF-Q4 artifacts with mature kernels are the right 4-bit choice on a 6 GB card.
- **Kernel cost is part of the format.** A QuIP# artifact requires Hadamard transforms on activations at inference and codebook lookups in the matmul; an IQ artifact requires its own dequantization path. On a small consumer GPU these overheads are proportionally larger than on the paper's RTX 4090. Any improved-Ollama design adopting codebook formats must budget the transform/lookup cost per token and measure end-to-end tokens/second against the Q4 baseline — the paper's own AQLM cautionary tale (more accurate on paper, slower than FP16 in practice, purely from codebook cache behaviour) is the standing warning.

### (b) Aesir's compression school — what a home-grown quantizer must implement or measure

QuIP# defines the compression school's graduation exercise — the techniques required to compete below 3 bits — and, just as usefully, the order in which to attempt them:

- **First, measure coherence.** For each target model, Aesir should compute and record simple incoherence diagnostics per layer (max-to-RMS ratios for weights and Hessian eigenspectra proxies) before and after any transform. If a home-grown quantizer cannot say how coherent its inputs are, it cannot predict where scalar quantization will fail — QuIP#'s theory makes coherence *the* explanatory variable, and it is cheap to measure.
- **Implement an orthogonal pre-transform before implementing a codebook.** The paper's ablation hierarchy is explicit: incoherence processing alone (no E8, no fine-tuning) already beats AWQ/OmniQuant at 2–3 bits. A randomized Hadamard transform is a small, well-specified piece of mathematics ($O(n \log n)$, ±1 entries, sign vectors as the only side data) with a large payoff — and it composes with the LDLQ/GPTQ compensation Aesir should already have from the GPTQ dossier. Codebooks without incoherence processing attack the harder half of the problem first.
- **If codebooks, then cache-shaped codebooks.** The E8P lesson is a *systems* constraint disguised as mathematics: the codebook must fit L1 (1 KiB, structured, symmetry-compressed) or inference speed collapses regardless of perplexity. Any Aesir vector quantizer must state its codebook's stored size, its decoded-lookup cost per weight, and its behaviour under cache duplication — and grade tokens/second alongside perplexity, in the paper's own two-table style.
- **Budget calibration and fine-tuning honestly.** QuIP#'s results rest on 6,144-sequence Hessians and, for the best numbers, ~50 GPU-hours of fine-tuning at 70B scale. An Aesir quantizer claiming QuIP#-class 2-bit quality with GPTQ-class calibration (128 segments, no fine-tuning) is claiming something the paper's ablations contradict. The school should publish, per artifact, its calibration set size and total quantization compute next to its faithfulness grades.
- **Grade the scaling claim locally before provisioning on it.** "Larger model at 2 bits beats smaller at 4 bits" is the single most consequential provisioning assertion in this wave for a 6 GB target — and it is a claim about Llama-family scaling measured by one paper. Aesir's faithfulness protocol (teacher-forced rank/logit agreement, packed versus original, per the corpus's Aesir grading discipline) should test it directly on the school's own target models at equal *total memory* (weights + KV + overhead), which is the comparison a 6 GB user actually faces.

- **KV wave (KIVI / KVQuant dossiers, `papers/kivi.md`, `papers/kvquant.md`):** the symmetry completes here — GPTQ/AWQ/SmoothQuant manage outliers by *rescaling* (diagonal transforms fitted to activation statistics); QuIP# manages them by *mixing* (orthogonal transforms that dissolve outlier coordinates entirely); KIVI manages the same pathology in the KV cache by *axis choice* (quantize along the outlier axis so it harms only itself). Three structural answers to one distribution problem, at three different points in the system — and a quantizer/cache designer who understands all three can mix them: incoherence-style thinking is already implicit in how the KV wave treats key-cache channels.
