## 2. The problem as framed

Llumnix starts from two properties of LLM requests that, together, break the assumption every conventional serving scheduler is built on — that a request can be placed well *once*, at arrival:

1. **Workload heterogeneity.** The same model serves chat, summarisation, coding, agents — requests whose input lengths, output lengths, and latency expectations differ by orders of magnitude. The paper's evaluation traces make the spread concrete: real datasets with input means of a few hundred tokens and P99s in the thousands, alongside generated long-tail (power-law) mixes of frequent short requests and rare very long ones.
2. **Execution unpredictability.** A request's output length — hence its runtime and its KV-cache growth — is *not known at arrival* and cannot be known: generation is autoregressive and stops when the model stops. Memory demand grows token by token, unpredictably, for the life of the request.

A dispatch decision made at arrival is therefore made with the least information the system will ever have, and it **rots within seconds**: an instance that was the right home for a request at dispatch becomes the wrong home as that request (and its neighbours) grow unpredictably. The paper names the three concrete diseases that follow, all of them *post-placement* diseases that queueing-only balancing cannot cure after the fact:

- **Isolation failure and preemption.** Unpredictable KV growth causes memory contention on an instance; the engine preempts running requests (evicting or recomputing them), producing unstable latencies and SLO violations. The cluster may have ample free memory *in aggregate* while one instance is preempting — the free space is simply on the wrong instances, and the requests that need it are nailed to the wrong ones.
- **Fragmentation.** Load-balancing dispatch spreads requests evenly, which spreads *free memory* evenly too — so a new long-input request can queue indefinitely while the cluster's total free blocks would fit it several times over, because no *single* instance holds a large enough contiguous share. The paper measures this directly: head-of-line queuing requests blocked while total free memory could satisfy at least three instances' worth of them. Load balancing and de-fragmentation are in direct tension, and a dispatch-only scheduler must choose its poison at arrival time.
- **Undifferentiated priorities.** Interactive and offline traffic share instances with no mechanism to protect the urgent from the patient — and static reservation (dedicated machines for high-priority work) wastes the fleet whenever the urgent class is quiet.

The paper's framing analogy is deliberate and load-bearing: LLM serving resembles **an operating system hosting processes on multiple cores** — dynamic working sets, differing priorities, unpredictable lifetimes — far more than it resembles traditional stateless DNN inference. Operating systems solved the analogous problem decades ago, and not by dispatching better: by **context-switching** — moving running work. The missing primitive in LLM serving is that the unit of scheduling is the *queued* request only. Llumnix's thesis: **the rescheduling unit must be the in-flight request.**

## 3. Core mechanism in our own words

**Runtime rescheduling via live migration.** Llumnix continuously reconsiders the placement of *running* requests across model instances and moves them — request state, KV cache and all — while they generate. Migration is the single primitive; load balancing, de-fragmentation, prioritisation, and auto-scaling drain/fill are all expressed as policies *over* it.

**The migration protocol — why the pause can be hidden.** Naively, moving a request means recomputing its context or stop-and-copying its KV: the paper measures naive approaches' stalls at up to **over 50× the cost of a decode step**, growing with sequence length — prohibitive, and worse exactly for the long requests that most need moving. Llumnix's protocol exploits one structural fact: **the KV cache is append-only.** Blocks written by earlier iterations are never modified again; only new blocks are appended. Migration therefore proceeds as a **multi-stage pipeline overlapped with continued decoding**:

- *Stage 0:* the source keeps decoding the request while copying the KV blocks of all completed iterations to the destination.
- *Stage k:* while decoding continues, copy the (few) blocks generated during stage k−1. Because copying is much faster than generation, each stage's new-block set is small and the stages converge rapidly — in the evaluation, migration completes in **two stages, the minimum**, across the sequence lengths tested.
- *Final stage:* exactly one iteration's worth of KV remains. The source drains the request from its batch, copies that last block, and the destination resumes it. **The downtime is the copy time of one iteration's KV — measured at roughly 20–30 ms, near-constant in sequence length and shorter than a single decode step.**

A **handshake protocol** keeps this correct under live conditions: before each stage the source asks the destination to pre-allocate and reserve the blocks about to arrive (failure aborts the migration and cleans up); after each stage the source checks whether the request finished (EOS) or was preempted mid-migration (either aborts cleanly on the destination side); on completion the source releases its blocks and the destination commits. The design borrows the shape of VM live migration but pointedly *not* its dirty-page tracking — append-only state has no dirty pages, which is what makes the LLM version simpler and its downtime constant.

**What the transfer costs the neighbours.** KV blocks are moved with the **Gloo** collective library (Send/Recv), deliberately not NCCL — concurrent NCCL invocations are unsafe alongside inference communication, and the pipelined design makes Gloo fast enough. Blocks are **fused** before sending: vLLM's KV lives in many small non-contiguous blocks (the paper's example: a 1K-token LLaMA-7B sequence is ~4,000 blocks of 128 KB), so the source gathers them into a contiguous CPU buffer on a separate CUDA stream and sends the buffer whole. Measured interference: per-step decode time on source and destination during migration differs from normal execution by **up to ~1%**, and instances have a migration in flight only ~10% of the time in the serving experiments — an effective overhead the paper fairly calls negligible, and the price that buys every policy below.

**Architecture: global scheduler + llumlets.** Continuous rescheduling of every running request would crush a centralised scheduler, so Llumnix splits it. A cluster-level **global scheduler** makes only *instance-level* decisions — dispatch, pairing migration sources with destinations, auto-scaling — from load figures the instances report; its complexity is independent of the number of running requests. Each instance runs a **llumlet** (local scheduler + migration coordinator) that tracks its own requests, computes its load, chooses *which* requests to migrate when the global scheduler marks it as a source, and executes the handshakes. Requests are dispatched to the instance with the highest **freeness**, F = (M − ΣV)/B — free memory (in virtual-usage terms) divided by batch size, i.e. roughly *how many more decode iterations this batch can run* — a load metric denominated in time, not bytes.

**Virtual usage — the policy unification.** The elegant move: every rescheduling scenario is reduced to load balancing by lying, carefully, about load. Each request carries a **virtual usage** in place of its physical KV footprint:

- *Normal case:* virtual usage = physical usage (routine load balancing).
- *Head-of-line queuing request:* virtual usage = its (currently zero-footprint) real demand. The instance now looks overloaded on paper, load balancing fires, and running requests migrate away — which *is* de-fragmentation, achieved without a defragmentation code path.
- *High-priority request:* virtual usage = physical usage + a **headroom** (profiled as the spare memory needed to preserve near-ideal decode speed, divided among the instance's high-priority requests). As the headroom is consumed, neighbours are migrated away — priority isolation by eviction of everyone else, with no static reservation. Two priority classes (high/normal) are implemented, for both *scheduling* priority (queue order) and *execution* priority (headroom); the design generalises to more.
- *Instance draining for auto-scaling:* add a fake request with virtual usage **∞**. Load balancing evacuates the instance completely; symmetrically, a fresh instance is saturated automatically by the same balancing flow. Auto-scaling itself keys off average freeness, kept within a configured band.

Migration rounds run periodically: instances below/above freeness thresholds become sources/destinations, are paired freeness-extreme-first, and sources migrate their **lowest-priority, shortest-sequence** requests first (cheapest to move, least harmed by moving) until they leave the source state.

**Implementation shape.** ~3,300 lines of Python as a standalone scheduling library over vLLM (continuous batching and PagedAttention inherited), with instances and frontends as Ray actors and an OpenAI-style API at the front — migrated requests keep streaming to the same frontend, so movement is invisible to clients. If the global scheduler fails, frontends fall back to direct simple dispatch with migration disabled: the service degrades to a conventional system rather than stopping.

## 4. Reported results — as reported, with hardware and scale context

All figures are the paper's own, on a datacentre testbed, against vLLM as the common engine so that only scheduling differs. **Fleet results; §5 separates mechanism from magnitude.**

**Setup (§6.1).** Testbed: **16 GPUs** — 4 Alibaba Cloud VMs (ecs.gn7i-c32g1.32xlarge), each with **4× NVIDIA A10 (24 GB)**, PCIe 4.0, 128 vCPUs, 752 GB RAM, 64 Gb/s network. Models: **LLaMA-7B** (one GPU per instance; 16 instances) and **LLaMA-30B** (4-GPU tensor-parallel instances), FP16; the vLLM version used supports the original LLaMA at up to 2K context (see §7). Traces: 10,000 requests each; Poisson and Gamma (bursty, varied coefficient of variation) arrivals; lengths from **ShareGPT** and **BurstGPT** real conversation data plus generated power-law distributions (Short/Medium/Long means 128/256/512, tails to ~6K) in five input/output combinations. Baselines: **round-robin** dispatch, **INFaaS++** (the authors' strengthened version of INFaaS with memory-load-aware dispatch and auto-scaling), and **Llumnix-base** (Llumnix without priorities) for the priority ablation.

**Migration efficiency (§6.2):**

- Downtime **~20–30 ms, near-constant** as sequence length grows — shorter than one decode step — versus baselines (recompute; blocking KV copy) whose downtime grows with length to up to **111×** Llumnix's; recomputing an 8K LLaMA-30B sequence takes ~3.5 s, a stall of ~54 decode steps.
- Migration completes in **two stages** at all lengths tested; overhead to co-running requests **≤ ~1%** per-step decode time during migration; migration in flight ~10% of the time per instance.

**Serving performance (§6.3), 16× LLaMA-7B instances:**

- *Headline (abstract):* tail latencies improved **by an order of magnitude**; high-priority requests accelerated **up to 1.5×**; **up to 36% cost savings** at similar tail latencies (auto-scaling, §6.5).
- *Prefill latency via de-fragmentation:* up to **15× (P99) / 7.7× (mean)** over INFaaS++ across the generated traces (the paper's §6 summary figures); per-experiment, P99 prefill improves up to 14.8× and mean up to 7.7× on generated distributions, and up to 5.5× (P99) / 2.2× (mean) on the real datasets. Against round-robin the prefill gains reach 34.4× (P99) — round-robin is included mainly to show that count-based balancing is not load balancing.
- *Decode latency via fewer preemptions:* P99 decode latency improved up to **2×**; preemption loss (extra queuing + recompute time) cut by **70.4% on average** across experiments (≈1.3 s average end-to-end saving) and by 84% on average versus round-robin.
- *End-to-end:* up to 2× (mean) / 2.9× (P99) versus round-robin on real traces; up to 1.5× / 1.6× versus INFaaS++ on generated traces.
- *Fragmentation case study:* during a busy period, average fragmented share of cluster memory **0.7% (Llumnix) vs 7.9% (INFaaS++)** — a 92% reduction; INFaaS++ sits above 10% fragmentation for much of the period while Llumnix is at ~0 for most of it.

**Priorities (§6.4):** with 10% of requests marked high-priority under bursty (Gamma) arrivals: high-priority end-to-end latency improves **1.2–1.5×** over Llumnix-base, prefill latency 2.9–8.6× (mean) / 3.6–10× (P99), decode 1.2–1.5× (mean) / 1.3–2.2× (P99) — while normal requests' latencies worsen by at most **4.5% (end-to-end), 13% (prefill), 2% (decode)**. The protection is bought by moving *other* requests, and the bill presented to them is small and stated.

**Auto-scaling (§6.5):** using freeness-band scaling, Llumnix saturates new instances and drains terminating ones through the same migration path, delivering the abstract's **up to 36% cost saving at similar P99 latencies** versus the INFaaS++ auto-scaling baseline.

**Scale caveat.** The cluster is 16 mid-range datacentre GPUs and the models are 7B/30B at ≤2K native context — modest by 2024 fleet standards and tiny beside Mooncake's production (§ sibling dossier). The *ratios* are also conditioned on multi-instance slack existing at all: migration fixes bad placements only when somewhere better exists. That condition is the whole subject of §5.

## 5. What transfers DOWN to a single machine

Judge honestly, as the corpus requires: **on a one-GPU runner, Llumnix's headline mechanism has nowhere to go.** Migration needs a destination instance; a single 6 GB card running one model has none, and no scheduling cleverness manufactures one. The P99 multipliers of §4 do not transfer, the llumlet/global-scheduler architecture does not transfer, and a local re-implementation of "live migration" between two processes sharing one GPU would pay real copy costs to buy movement within a memory pool that a block allocator already manages in place. What survives the scale collapse is not the mechanism but three of its ideas — and they survive in recognisable, buildable forms:

1. **Placement decisions should be revisable — so state must be built movable from the start.** This is the residual lesson, and it is the one the corpus has already independently arrived at from the single-machine side. Llumnix could only revise placements because request state was *designed* as a transferable object (paged blocks, a defined handshake, a commit/abort protocol). The single-machine analogues are exactly the corpus's residency line: a session whose KV is a first-class, serialisable, restorable object (IDEA-LEDGER #1, #10) can be moved between *models and backends on one machine* — chat model to summariser sidecar and back, GPU residency to RAM to disk and back (IDEA-LEDGER #11: spill idle, never running) — while a session smeared through one process's heap can only be destroyed and recomputed. Llumnix is the fleet-scale argument for the design rule: **whatever the future scheduler might want to move, build movable now.** Project Aesir's hybrid-model work is the same rule under harder physics: recurrent state cannot be paged, so Aesir snapshots whole state at boundaries, bit-exact, in a bounded store (IDEA-LEDGER #23) — a snapshot is precisely "state built movable" for a model class whose state does not come in blocks.
2. **The append-only pipeline is the local spill/restore pattern.** Llumnix hides migration cost by copying the immutable bulk *while compute continues* and pausing only for the final iteration's residue — downtime constant in total state size. The single-machine transfers that do exist (session spill to RAM/disk between turns, restore on resume, snapshot capture in Aesir) should be built the same way: move the bulk in the background during work that does not depend on it; make the user-visible pause proportional to the *last increment*, not the whole history. The corpus's restore figure (2.14 s vs 160.7 s recompute for a 7.3K-token session) is the prize; Llumnix's staging is how such transfers stay invisible when they happen mid-conversation rather than between turns.
3. **De-fragmentation and virtual usage, miniaturised into the block pool.** Llumnix's fragmentation is *across instances*; the 6 GB runner has the same disease *within* one KV arena: free blocks scattered so that a long prompt cannot be admitted while aggregate free memory looks sufficient — compounded by the display-attached reserve arithmetic that can leave only ~0–250 MiB of KV budget on the target card (IDEA-LEDGER #3). Two Llumnix ideas apply directly. First, *measure fragmentation as a first-class metric* (Llumnix's "free memory that could satisfy the blocked head-of-line request if unfragmented" is a better local health number than raw free bytes; the corpus's five-number VRAM ledger, IDEA-LEDGER #6, should carry it). Second, *virtual usage as policy language*: the local scheduler's real decisions — protect the live conversation, admit the sidecar only into genuine slack, evict the idle session first — can be expressed exactly as Llumnix expresses them, by inflating the accounting weight of what must be protected until the ordinary allocator moves everything else. Priority-by-headroom becomes, locally, *the active persona session carries headroom; background work is the thing that gets spilled* — preemption-by-migration with the disk as the destination instance.
4. **What the local version of "migration" actually is.** Between backends on one machine — Ollama to a llama.cpp sidecar, GPU layers to CPU layers, one model to another — a session handoff is a Llumnix migration with the network replaced by memcpy and the destination's "pre-allocation handshake" replaced by a fit check. The protocol shape (reserve → bulk copy overlapped with continued work → final-increment pause → commit/abort) is worth copying literally; it is short, it is proven, and it makes failed handoffs abort-clean instead of half-moved.

**For Aesir:** the serve's resident sessions face Llumnix's exact problem at n=1 machine but n>1 *device* (NPU/GPU/CPU under unified memory): a session placed on the wrong device for its current phase is a bad placement that a dispatch-only design must endure. Aesir cannot migrate KV between instances — but with snapshot-movable state it can *re-place sessions between devices at turn boundaries*, which is Llumnix's revisability principle at the only granularity unified memory allows. The lesson to bank is the principle and the protocol shape, not the fleet machinery.

## 7. Risks and limits of the source itself

- **Small, dated testbed by design.** 16× A10-24GB, LLaMA-7B/30B, and a vLLM baseline limited to the original LLaMA's 2K context. The paper argues the results are representative of longer-context variants "from a systems perspective" — plausible for the mechanism (append-only staging only gets *more* valuable as KV grows) but the *measured ratios* are 2K-context ratios, and the constant-downtime claim is demonstrated, not proven, at 8K total sequence length in the migration microbenchmark.
- **Synthetic arrivals, partly synthetic lengths.** Arrivals are Poisson/Gamma by construction and half the length distributions are generated power laws; the real datasets (ShareGPT/BurstGPT) anchor the claims but the largest prefill gains appear on the generated long-tail traces, which were designed to stress exactly the fragmentation Llumnix fixes. That is legitimate stress-testing, and it is also a selection effect: workloads without long tails will see smaller wins.
- **The strongest baseline is the authors' own.** INFaaS++ is INFaaS as improved by the Llumnix team ("we improve it by making it focus on GPU memory load"). The strengthening cuts in the reader's favour — the baseline is not a strawman — but its tuning is still the proponents', and round-robin, the only wholly independent baseline, is weak enough that its 26–34× prefill ratios should be discounted entirely.
- **Two priority classes, profiled headroom.** The prioritisation results rest on 10% high-priority load and a headroom target (1,600 tokens) chosen empirically for the testbed model. Production priority mixes, and headroom profiling for other models, are asserted to generalise rather than shown.
- **Migration is not free at the cluster level, only at the request level.** The ≤1% interference and ~10% duty-cycle figures are from the paper's own traces; a scheduler that migrates pathologically often (thrash under rapidly oscillating load) would pay more, and the paper's threshold-based trigger is a heuristic defence, not a bound.
- **No production deployment is reported.** Unlike Mooncake, Llumnix is an evaluated research system on a cloud testbed, from a team at Alibaba; the paper reports no production fleet running it. Its claims are *evidence* of the reproducible kind — arguably stronger per-claim than Mooncake's production testimony — but they are evidence about a 16-GPU cluster in 2024, and §4's caveat stands.
