## 2. The problem as framed

Standard attention is the long-context bottleneck in both phases, but for different reasons in each — and the paper is explicit about the split, in arithmetic-intensity terms:

- **Prefill and training** are compute-bound: batched attention over the whole sequence costs quadratically in sequence length in FLOPs, and that arithmetic dominates.
- **Decode** is memory-bandwidth-bound: each step generates one token while loading the entire KV cache, so its arithmetic intensity is low and its cost is bytes read. The paper's motivating estimate (§1): attention accounts for **70–80% of total latency when decoding 64k-length contexts**.

Against that background the paper makes two charges against the prior sparse-attention literature — its §2 is titled, pointedly, "The Illusion of Efficient Inference" and "The Myth of Trainable Sparsity":

1. **Phase-restricted, hardware-unfriendly sparsity.** Inference-only methods typically accelerate one phase only (H2O-style eviction targets decode; MInference targets prefill — the paper names both), use token-granular or per-head selection that scatters memory access, and collide with grouped-query / multi-query attention (GQA/MQA): under GQA the KV actually loaded is the *union* of the selections of all query heads sharing a KV head, so per-head sparsity does not translate into proportionally fewer bytes read. Theoretical FLOP savings therefore fail to become wall-clock savings.
2. **Sparsity imposed on a model trained dense.** Applying sparsity post-hoc forces the model off its pretrained trajectory — the paper cites the finding that the top 20% of attention covers only ~70% of total attention mass, so retrieval heads are vulnerable to pruning — and most selection schemes are either non-differentiable (clustering, hashing) or too irregular for FlashAttention-style blockwise kernels, so they cannot be trained through anyway.

The problem as framed is therefore not "make attention sparse" but: **design a sparse attention the model is pretrained with, whose sparsity pattern is block-structured and GQA-aware enough that the theoretical saving survives contact with Tensor Cores and HBM** — efficient in training, prefill, and decode alike.

## 3. Core mechanism in our own words

For each query, NSA replaces the single dense attention over all preceding keys/values with **three parallel branches**, each attending to a different, much smaller remapping of the same past, whose outputs are combined by a learned gate. Formally the output is a sum over branches $c \in \{\text{cmp}, \text{slc}, \text{win}\}$ of $g_t^c \cdot \mathrm{Attn}(\mathbf{q}_t, \tilde{K}_t^c, \tilde{V}_t^c)$, where each gate $g_t^c \in [0,1]$ comes from an MLP with sigmoid over the input features, and the three branches are given **independent keys and values** so no branch's learning can be shortcut by another's (in particular, so fast-adapting local patterns cannot starve the long-range branches of gradient).

**Branch 1 — Compression (coarse, global).** Contiguous blocks of keys/values are aggregated into one compressed token per block by a learnable MLP $\varphi$ with intra-block position encoding. In the paper's experimental configuration: compression block length $l = 32$, stride $d = 16$ (blocks overlap, $d < l$, to mitigate fragmentation). Attending to these gives the query a cheap, whole-past summary scan — roughly one token per 16 positions — and, crucially, its attention scores are reused by Branch 2.

**Branch 2 — Selection (fine, sparse).** The past is also divided into selection blocks ($l' = 64$ in the configuration). A selection block's importance score is *derived from the compression branch's scores for free*: the softmax scores $\mathbf{p}_t^{\text{cmp}}$ over compressed tokens are summed over the compressed blocks overlapping each selection block (Eq. 9 in the paper; when the two blockings coincide the scores transfer directly). For GQA/MQA, scores are additionally **summed across all query heads in the group** (Eq. 10), so the whole group selects the *same* blocks — this is the fix for the union-of-selections problem of §2: one shared selection means one shared, contiguous KV load. The top-$n$ blocks by score ($n = 16$ in the configuration, including a fixed initial block and two local blocks) are then attended at full, uncompressed resolution.

**Branch 3 — Sliding window (local).** The most recent $w$ tokens ($w = 512$ in the configuration) are always attended, in their own branch, so local context — the part of attention that is dense and non-negotiable in practice — is handled explicitly rather than competing with the long-range branches.

The total keys a query touches stays far below the sequence length ($N_t \ll t$ in the paper's notation): at 64k, the paper's decode accounting (its Table 4) counts 5,632 token-equivalents of KV load per step against 65,536 for full attention.

**Kernel design.** Compression and sliding-window attention are already compatible with FlashAttention-2-style kernels; the new work is the selection kernel, written in Triton. Its inversion of the usual loop order is the point: FlashAttention loads a block of *queries* and streams KV past it, but neighbouring queries select disjoint KV blocks, which would scatter the loads. NSA's kernel instead loads, per grid position, **all query heads of one GQA group at one query position** together with that group's shared selected-block index set, then streams the selected KV blocks — contiguous, coalesced, Tensor-Core-friendly — through SRAM in the inner loop. Group-centric loading eliminates redundant KV transfers (the group fetches each selected block once, not once per head) and keeps inner-loop lengths near-uniform across grid positions, balancing work across streaming multiprocessors. The paper frames the whole design as balancing arithmetic intensity per phase: cut FLOPs where compute binds (training/prefill), cut bytes where bandwidth binds (decode).

**Training integration.** Nothing in the three branches is a discrete, gradient-blocking operation of the clustering/hashing kind: compression is a learned MLP, gating is a learned MLP, and selection scores come from the compression attention itself. NSA is therefore pretrained end-to-end *as* the model's attention. The paper's experiment pretrains a 27B-total / 3B-active MoE backbone (30 layers, GQA with 4 groups and 64 heads, DeepSeekMoE with 72 routed + 2 shared experts) with NSA from scratch — 270B tokens at 8k length per the §4.1 setup (the introduction rounds the same run as 260B tokens) — followed by continued training and SFT at 32k with YaRN for long-context adaptation, with a full-attention twin trained identically as the baseline.

## 4. Reported results — as reported, with hardware/model context

All figures are the paper's own, on the 27B MoE model above unless stated otherwise. Kernel speeds are Triton implementations compared against a Triton-based FlashAttention-2, measured on A100 GPUs (the efficiency section specifies an 8-GPU A100 system; GQA group $g = 4$, 16 heads per group, $d_k = 192$, $d_v = 128$).

- **Speed at 64k context (paper §5, Figure 6):** up to **9.0× forward** and **6.0× backward** speedup over FlashAttention-2, with the ratio growing with sequence length. These are kernel measurements.
- **Decode at 64k (paper §5.2, Table 4):** KV-load volume per step falls from 65,536 to 5,632 token-equivalents, from which the paper derives an **expected ~11.6× decoding speedup**, on the stated reasoning that decode is memory-bound so speedup is approximately linear in bytes loaded. Read this number as the paper presents it — a volume-derived expectation (its Table 4 column is literally headed "Expected Speedup"), not a measured end-to-end decode timing of the same kind as the forward/backward figures. The intermediate points: 8k → 2,048 tokens loaded (4× expected), 16k → 2,560 (6.4×), 32k → 3,584 (9.1×).
- **General benchmarks (paper Table 1):** NSA average **0.456 vs 0.443** for the full-attention twin across nine benchmarks (MMLU, MMLU-PRO, CMMLU, BBH, GSM8K, MATH, DROP, MBPP, HumanEval), ahead on 7 of 9; the named gains are reasoning tasks — DROP +0.042, GSM8K +0.034. Pretraining loss is reported as consistently *below* the full-attention baseline throughout training (Figure 4).
- **Long context (paper Table 2, Figure 5):** **perfect retrieval accuracy at all positions** in the 64k needle-in-a-haystack test; LongBench average **0.469 vs 0.437** for full attention and 0.423 for an Exact-Top sparse baseline, at matched sparsity (all sparse baselines budgeted to 2,560 activated tokens per query, NSA's average at 32k) — with the largest margins on multi-hop QA (HotpotQA +0.087, 2Wiki +0.051 over full attention), code (LCC +0.069), and passage retrieval (PassR-en +0.075). Inference-only sparse baselines (H2O 0.303, InfLLM 0.383, Quest 0.392) trail by wide margins in this comparison.
- **Reasoning after SFT (paper Table 3):** after identical distillation SFT from DeepSeek-R1 (10B tokens of 32k reasoning traces), NSA-R scores **0.121 vs 0.046** (Full Attention-R) on AIME 24 at an 8k generation limit, and **0.146 vs 0.092** at 16k.

As-reported caveats that belong with the numbers: the quality parity-and-better claims rest on *one* model scale (27B/3B-active), trained by the authors, against their own full-attention twin; and the "beats full attention" direction — sparsity as a regulariser that filters attention noise — is the authors' interpretation of their result, not an independently replicated finding.

## 5. Position vs the corpus's KV-selection line

This is the distinction the wave exists to state precisely, because NSA is routinely shelved next to SnapKV/H2O and the two families save different things:

- **SnapKV / H2O / StreamingLLM (`papers/snapkv.md`, `papers/h2o.md`, `papers/streamingllm.md`) shrink the cache.** They decide which KV entries *exist*: evicted or never-retained tokens are gone from memory, so the cache's byte footprint shrinks, decode reads a physically smaller cache, and the saving is **memory capacity** (and, secondarily, decode bytes, because there is less cache to read). The risk is irreversibility — a discarded token cannot be recovered if generation later needs it.
- **NSA shrinks the read, not the cache.** The full KV cache is still computed and retained; what changes is how much of it each query's attention *loads and computes over* — compressed tokens for the global scan, a handful of full-resolution blocks, a local window. The saving is **compute in prefill/training and bytes-read in decode**, not cache capacity: an NSA model at 64k still stores a 64k cache (in its three branch representations). The corresponding risk is different, too — not irreversible loss, but *approximation per query*: a block the compression scores rank low is skipped *for that query*, yet remains available to later queries whose scores rank it higher. Selection here is per-query and recoverable; eviction is per-cache and final.

The two compose in principle — nothing stops an NSA-style sparse read operating over a cache that a selection policy has also trimmed — but they are not substitutes, and their headline numbers must never be compared as if they measured the same resource.

NSA's second, sharper difference: **it is trained-in.** The compression MLP, the gates, and the model's adaptation to seeing the past only through these three branches are all products of pretraining. An inference runner **cannot retrofit NSA onto an existing dense model** — applying the three-branch pattern post-hoc to a full-attention checkpoint is exactly the §2.2 failure mode the paper demonstrates against (the model never learned to route information through compressed tokens, and its retrieval heads were shaped by dense attention). NSA is an architecture a model *ships with*, not a feature a runner *turns on*. MInference (§ sibling dossier) is the opposite pole on precisely this axis: same sparse-computation ambition, zero training, pattern imposed at inference time on a dense model that tolerates it because prefill attention is empirically sparse enough.

## 6. Applicability

### (a) A personal runner on existing GGUF models

- **NSA is out — plainly.** Every GGUF model a personal runner serves today (Llama, Qwen, Mistral, Gemma families and their kin) is a dense-attention (or GQA/MLA) checkpoint. There is no runner-side switch, kernel flag, or cache policy that makes such a model an NSA model; the sparsity lives in trained weights (compression and gating MLPs) and in representations that only exist if the model was built with them. Any "NSA mode" offered for an existing GGUF would be a different, untested post-hoc sparse method wearing NSA's name and none of its quality evidence.
- **The lesson is forward-looking, and it is a detection duty.** If open-weight models begin shipping sparse-native attention — the design is published, award-winning, and from a lab that releases weights — a runner's job is to *recognise and honour the architecture*: parse the branch structure and its hyperparameters (block sizes, stride, top-$n$, window) from model metadata, allocate the branch-specific KV representations, and route attention to kernels that implement the trained pattern exactly. Serving an NSA checkpoint through a dense-attention path (or an approximation of the branches) would silently change the model's computation — the same class of offence as serving a sliding-window model as full attention. Architecture fidelity, declared loudly when unsupported ("this runner cannot serve this attention type yet"), is the actionable takeaway for a runner today.
- **One transferable engineering idea, labelled as ours:** the *group-shared selection* principle. Any future block-sparse work in a ggml-class backend over GQA models must select per KV-group, not per query head, or the bytes-read saving evaporates into the union problem (§2.1). That is a constraint to design by now, borrowed from NSA's analysis, not an NSA implementation.

### (b) Decode relevance

Unlike a prefill-only method, NSA claims decode as a first-class beneficiary — decode is where its bytes-read argument bites hardest, since a dense decode step must load the whole cache for one token and NSA loads ~5.6k token-equivalents at 64k. Two honest qualifications: (i) the 11.6× is the paper's volume-derived *expected* speedup (§4 above), not a measured token-generation rate; real decode also loads weights every step, which dilutes the attention-only figure end-to-end, increasingly so at small model sizes and short contexts. (ii) The benefit scales with context: at 8k the expected factor is 4× on attention traffic, and at the ≤32k contexts of typical personal use the absolute KV traffic is itself modest next to weight traffic for batch-1 decode. NSA's decode story is real but it is a *long-context* story, and it belongs to models that ship the architecture — for the existing GGUF fleet it changes nothing.
