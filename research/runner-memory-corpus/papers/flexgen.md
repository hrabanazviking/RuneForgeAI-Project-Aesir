## 2. The problem as framed

- **The capacity wall, stated in bytes.** An OPT-175B-class model needs ~325 GB just for its FP16 weights — at least five 80 GB A100s by the paper's arithmetic — before any KV cache exists. The systems that existed for running such a model on less hardware (DeepSpeed ZeRO-Inference, Hugging Face Accelerate) inherited their offloading strategies from *training*, and the paper's charge is precise: those strategies do excessive I/O and strand at batch sizes of one or two for OPT-175B, achieving throughput far below what the hardware could in principle move.
- **The workload that makes the trade legal: throughput-oriented generative inference.** FlexGen explicitly does *not* target interactive chat. Its named workloads are "back-of-house" batch jobs — benchmarking (HELM), information extraction, data wrangling, form processing — that run inference over a large corpus of prompts and are **latency-insensitive**: nobody is waiting on any single token. In that regime latency is a currency that can be spent, in bulk, to buy throughput: process prompts in very large batches so that the expensive act of loading a layer's weights from CPU or disk is amortised over hundreds of sequences at once, and overlap the loading with computation. The paper's Figure 1 is the whole framing in one plot: a latency–throughput Pareto frontier on a single 16 GB card, on which FlexGen claims a new frontier with up to 100× higher maximum throughput for OPT-175B than the baselines, which simply run out of memory before they can trade any further.
- **Why the KV cache, not the weights, is the real sizing problem at large batch.** The paper's memory analysis (§3) is the part a modern reader should not skip: at batch 512, prompt 512, output 32, OPT-175B's KV cache is **1.2 TB — 3.8× the weights**. Any offloading scheme that places only weights and leaves cache/activations pinned to the GPU (as both baselines did) caps its batch size on cache, not weights, and therefore caps its throughput. FlexGen's placement problem is irreducibly three-way — weights, activations, *and* KV cache, each split across GPU, CPU, and disk — because the tensor that dominates depends on the batch size the schedule is trying to reach.
- **The second challenge: compression that serves I/O, not compute.** Prior quantisation work optimised integer arithmetic; FlexGen quantises to shrink *bytes stored and bytes moved*, dequantising back to FP16 before computing (§3). That inversion — compression as a transport technology — is the same idea the corpus's CacheGen dossier (`papers/cachegen.md`) later applies to KV in flight, and it is why FlexGen's 4-bit result belongs in an offloading paper rather than a quantisation paper.

## 3. Core mechanism in our own words

FlexGen is a PyTorch-based engine with three separable contributions: a **schedule**, a **placement search**, and a pair of **approximations** that plug into the search. Nothing in the core offloading design (§4 of the paper) relaxes the computation at all — it is exact inference under a searched memory policy.

**The three-tier placement problem.** The machine is modelled as three devices forming a hierarchy: GPU (smallest, fastest, can compute), CPU with its DRAM (middle, can compute slowly), disk (largest, slowest, cannot compute). A policy assigns, for each of the three tensor kinds, the fraction resident on each tier: weights $(w_g, w_c, w_d)$, activations $(h_g, h_c, h_d)$, KV cache $(c_g, c_c, c_d)$, each triple summing to 1. Granularity is chosen per kind for analysability: weights are partitioned at *layer* granularity, activations and KV cache at *tensor* granularity. Two further policy variables set the schedule's shape: the GPU batch size ($gbs$ — sequences computed together per GPU pass) and the block size ($bls$ — the *effective* batch, $gbs$ × number of GPU batches per block).

**Computation delegation.** Placement is not only about storage: where a tensor lives decides where its computation can cheaply happen. The paper's worked case is attention during decode. If the KV cache sits on the CPU, computing attention on the GPU means moving the whole cache — $b \times s \times h_1 \times 4$ bytes — while computing it on the CPU means moving only the activation, $b \times h_1 \times 4$ bytes: an **$s\times$ reduction in I/O**, so for sequences of length ≥512 CPU-side attention wins despite the CPU's slower arithmetic. Delegation is therefore a first-class policy dimension, not an implementation detail. (It interacts with compression: the paper turns CPU delegation *off* when 4-bit quantisation is enabled, because CPU dequantisation overhead eats the win — §4 below.)

**The zig-zag block schedule.** Inference's computation graph is a grid: rows are sequences, columns are token steps, each cell is one layer of one step. Traversing row-by-row (finish one batch completely before starting the next — what the baselines do) reloads every layer's weights for every batch: maximal weight I/O. Traversing column-by-column (hold one layer's weights on the GPU and run *all* sequences through it) reuses weights perfectly but requires storing activations and KV for the entire effective batch, which exhausts CPU and disk memory part-way down the column. FlexGen's schedule is the compromise the paper calls **zig-zag / block scheduling**: proceed column-by-column inside a *block* of sequences (weights loaded once per block, reused across all its GPU batches), then move to the next block. The paper proves (Theorem 4.1) this schedule's I/O complexity is **within 2× of the optimal** traversal, and notes it implements the block schedule rather than a more advanced provably-optimal one for practical reasons. On top of the schedule sits **overlapping**: loading the next layer's weights, loading the next batch's cache/activations, and storing the previous batch's outputs are launched concurrently with the current batch's compute across CUDA streams and CPU threads, synchronising once per cell.

**The LP policy search.** Given $(bls, gbs)$ — enumerated over a small grid, since $gbs$ is a multiple of 4 and blocks are small — finding the best placement is a **linear program in the 9 fraction variables**: minimise $T / bls$ (per-sequence latency, i.e. maximise throughput), where $T$ comes from an analytical cost model, subject to peak-memory constraints on each of the three tiers and the three sum-to-1 constraints. The cost model prices one layer's prefill time $T_{pre}$ and average decode time $T_{gen}$ each as the **max** of five overlapped terms — CPU→GPU read, GPU→CPU write, disk→CPU read, CPU→disk write, and compute — under a perfect-overlap assumption, with each I/O term computed from tensor sizes × the placed fractions ÷ measured tier bandwidths. Hardware parameters are fitted by profiling the actual machine, so the same search retargets to different GPU/CPU/disk combinations; the formulation also accepts added latency constraints and compression variants. The paper is candid about the seams: fractions are relaxed to reals although tensors cannot split arbitrarily, peak-memory modelling is imperfect (fragmentation), a searched policy occasionally OOMs and is hand-adjusted, and "it is common that a better policy can be obtained by tuning manually." The LP is a strong starting point with a human in the loop, not an oracle — a calibration worth keeping when §6 borrows the idea.

**Approximation 1 — 4-bit group-wise compression of weights *and* KV cache.** Both large tensor kinds are quantised to 4 bits with **fine-grained group-wise asymmetric quantisation** (Shen et al. 2020 lineage): groups of **64 contiguous elements**, each group carrying its own min/max, elements stored as 4-bit integers and dequantised to FP16 before compute. Grouping dimension is chosen empirically per kind: weights along the **output-channel** dimension, KV cache along the **hidden** dimension. No retraining and no calibration. The stated purpose is compression for storage and I/O reduction — it is what lets the 175B configuration hold everything in CPU memory and drop the disk tier entirely (§4). The paper reports 3-bit was tried and does **not** preserve accuracy.

**Approximation 2 — sparse attention (top-10% value cache).** After computing attention scores, each query keeps the indices of its top-K tokens and only the corresponding **10% of the V cache** is loaded and used. Presented as a preliminary, pluggable demonstration that the framework absorbs approximations, with quality held on the paper's two accuracy tasks (§4) — the direct ancestor, in spirit, of the corpus's later KV-selection line (SnapKV, H2O, InfiniGen), with the difference that FlexGen's selection is a throughput accessory inside an offloading engine, not the contribution itself.

**What it sacrifices, deliberately.** Latency — by design, and in quantities that must be quoted whenever the throughput numbers are: the headline configurations run at total batch latencies of **4,000–12,000 seconds**. A single user's next token is not 1 second away in any useful interactive sense; the 1 token/s figure is an *aggregate* rate across an effective batch of 144 sequences. FlexGen also sacrifices the disk tier's dignity: with compression off at 175B, disk offloading is load-bearing (the ablation OOMs without it) at the testbed's ~2 GB/s SSD read bandwidth. And multi-GPU extension is by **pipeline parallelism** (layers partitioned equally across GPUs, the single-GPU search reused per shard), chosen because the target is throughput and pipeline's communication is cheap — tensor parallelism, the latency tool, is explicitly not the choice.

## 4. Reported results — as reported, with hardware context

All figures are the paper's own (ICML 2023 / arXiv v2 text, read 2026-11-15); nothing is re-derived. Throughput benchmarks use dummy weights; accuracy runs use real weights.

- **Testbed (Table 1):** single **NVIDIA T4, 16 GB** (Google Cloud instance); Intel Xeon @ 2.00 GHz host with **208 GB CPU DRAM**; **1.5 TB** cloud NVMe SSD, measured ~**2 GB/s read / 1 GB/s write**. Workload: synthetic prompts padded to uniform length, **32 output tokens** per prompt, prompt lengths 512 and 1024. Metric: generation throughput = generated tokens ÷ (prefill + decode time). Models: OPT 6.7B / 30B / 175B. Baselines: DeepSpeed ZeRO-Inference and Hugging Face Accelerate (the only offloading systems then available; both row-by-row, cache/activations GPU-only, no quantisation in these runs), plus Petals for the collaborative-inference comparison.
- **Maximum generation throughput (Table 2), prompt 512 — tokens/s:** OPT-6.7B: Accelerate 25.12, DeepSpeed 9.28, FlexGen **25.26**, FlexGen (4-bit) **29.12**. OPT-30B: 0.62 / 0.60 / **7.32** / **8.70**. OPT-175B: 0.01 / 0.01 / **0.69** / **1.12**. At prompt 1024 the pattern holds at lower rates (OPT-175B: FlexGen 0.35, with compression 0.42; baselines 0.01 / OOM). In words: at 175B the uncompressed system is ~69× the baselines; with compression, **112×** at prompt 512 — the abstract's rounded claim is "100× higher maximum throughput," reached at effective batch size **144** with total latency 4,000 s, the configuration that first reaches **1 token/s generation throughput for OPT-175B on a single 16 GB GPU**.
- **The latency price, in the paper's own trade-off points:** at a matched latency of 5,000 s, FlexGen (effective batch 64) achieves >40× DeepSpeed's throughput while Accelerate cannot complete a batch; allowing 12,000 s latency, the uncompressed system reaches effective batch **256** (8,192 tokens generated per batch) for 69× maximum throughput, where both baselines cap at batch size 2 by OOM.
- **Searched policies (ablation, Table 4):** the OPT-30B optimum places 20% of weights on GPU / 80% on CPU, all activations and KV on CPU, GPU batch 48 × 3 batches per block → 7.32 tok/s. The OPT-175B optimum places **0% of weights on GPU**, 50% on CPU (rest on disk), GPU batch 32 × 8 → 0.69 tok/s. That a throughput-optimal policy can put *no weights at all* on the GPU — the card becomes a compute engine fed entirely from below — is the cleanest single exhibit of placement-as-search beating placement-by-intuition.
- **Ablations (same table):** removing policy search costs little at 30B (7.26) but collapses 175B (0.69 → 0.27); removing overlap: 5.86 / 0.59; removing CPU compute: 4.03 / 0.62; removing disk at 175B: **OOM**; running the DeepSpeed policy inside the FlexGen runtime: 1.57 / 0.01 — the paper's evidence that the *policy*, not merely the runtime, carries the result. Runtime breakdown: GPU compute utilisation 82% in prefill, **13% in decode** — decode remains I/O-bound even in the winning system.
- **Accuracy of the approximations (Table 5):** LAMBADA accuracy / WikiText perplexity — OPT-30B: FP16 0.725 / 12.72; 4-bit 0.724 / 12.90; 4-bit + sparse attention 0.718 / 12.90. OPT-175B: FP16 0.758 / 10.82; 4-bit 0.756 / 10.94; 4-bit-S 0.756 / 10.94. The paper's characterisation — negligible loss at 4 bits for both weights and KV — is what these deltas support, on these two tasks, for OPT models; 3-bit does not preserve accuracy.
- **HELM demonstration:** FlexGen benchmarks **OPT-IML-30B** on 7 representative HELM sub-scenarios in **21 hours** on the Table 1 setup — the paper's proof that the target workload (offline evaluation of a model that does not fit) is actually completable on one commodity card.
- **Scaling (Table 3, 4 GPUs, pipeline parallel):** generation throughput 201.12 / 23.61 / 2.33 tok/s (6.7B / 30B / 175B) vs single-GPU 25.26 / 7.32 / 0.69 — sub-linear for generation overall (prefill pipeline bubbles, only 32 output tokens), but **super-linear for decode-only throughput** (764.65 / 48.94 / 3.86), because sharding relieves per-device memory pressure enough to move up a batch-size / tier regime.
- **Against collaborative inference (§6.3):** a single-T4 FlexGen beats the *per-GPU* throughput of a Petals cluster under all tested network conditions, and can show lower latency on slow networks with short generations; the paper's own balance sentence is that collaborative inference remains the more viable option for latency-sensitive scenarios.

## 5. Position in the corpus

- **The ancestor, literally.** `papers/infinigen.md` (Wave 15) records that InfiniGen is *implemented on top of FlexGen* and takes FlexGen (full-fetch and INT4 variants) as its principal baseline; its motivating measurement — data transfer at 96.9% of a Transformer block's time — is a measurement *of FlexGen*. Until this wave, the corpus cited that host framework in every InfiniGen claim without having dossiered it. This dossier closes that hole: the reader can now see what InfiniGen inherited (the three-tier placement, the CPU-resident KV pool, the block schedule) and what it added (per-layer rehearsal-selected prefetch *within* the regime FlexGen defined).
- **The regime split, stated by its author.** FlexGen is the corpus's clearest primary-source statement that "running a model bigger than the GPU" is **two different problems wearing one name**. In the throughput regime, the correct moves are: giant effective batches, weights reused across a block, latency spent freely, placement searched globally. In the latency regime — a person in a chat, batch 1, next-token time is the product — nearly every one of those moves inverts: batching is unavailable, reuse collapses, and the binding metric is the single stream's decode rate. A personal interactive runner (the corpus's target: Ollama-class, one user, one conversation) lives entirely in the second regime. FlexGen's *numbers* therefore do not transfer to it at all — quoting "1 token/s for 175B" beside an interactive runner's tokens/s is a category error this dossier exists to prevent — while its *method* transfers completely (§6).
- **The placement-search idea vs hand placement.** Every mainstream local runner places tensors by rule of thumb: whole layers to the GPU until VRAM fills (`-ngl`-style), KV where the engine's default puts it, activations wherever the graph runs. FlexGen's durable claim is that placement is an **optimisation problem with an objective, constraints, and measurable hardware inputs** — and its DeepSpeed-policy ablation (same runtime, hand-inherited policy: 0.01 vs 0.69 tok/s at 175B) is the corpus's starkest measurement of what un-searched placement can cost inside the regime where placement dominates. PowerInfer's ILP placement (`runners/powerinfer.md` §3.2) is the same conviction at neuron granularity in the latency regime; the two papers are the corpus's pair of witnesses that *where tensors live should be solved, not set*.
- **Compression's job description.** FlexGen's 4-bit weights+KV is compression *for movement and capacity* — dequantised before compute, chosen for ratio over kernel convenience — which places it on the transport side of the corpus's compression map, with CacheGen (`papers/cachegen.md`), and against the compute-side quantisation line (GPTQ/AWQ and the GGUF formats) whose formats exist to be *computed in*. Its group-size-64, per-kind grouping-dimension choices are also an early data point for the corpus's KV-quantisation dossiers (`papers/kivi.md`, `papers/kvquant.md`): KV at 4 bits with fine-grained groups and negligible measured loss, on OPT, in 2023.

## 6. Applicability

**(a) The fleet — a FlexGen-shaped machine running the opposite workload.** purplelongship (RTX 2060-class 6 GB card, ~39 GB system RAM, NVMe below) is, in tier shape, exactly FlexGen's machine scaled down: a small fast tier, a middle tier several times larger, a large slow tier. What does *not* survive the move is the workload assumption — the fleet's runner serves one interactive user, so effective batch is 1, the zig-zag block schedule has nothing to zig across, and FlexGen's throughput arithmetic (which needs batch in the hundreds to amortise a weight load) collapses to its latency column, where the paper itself reports decode GPU utilisation of 13%: the honest reading is that full offloading of a far-too-big model on this machine yields *a capability* (the model runs at all) at *batch-job speeds*, and presenting it as interactive serving would be a mis-sale. **Judgement (ours, labelled):** the correct fleet use of a FlexGen-style mode is explicitly as a **batch mode** — overnight summarisation of a document pile, bulk extraction, evaluation sweeps over a corpus (the HELM use case is literally this) — priced per use case against the interactive mode, never blended into one "tokens/s" figure.

What survives unconditionally is **placement-as-optimisation**:

1. *Measure the tiers, then solve.* The LP's inputs are four bandwidths and three capacities — all measurable on the actual machine in minutes. Even a greedy/knapsack solver over per-layer (or per-tensor-kind) fractions, driven by measured bandwidths rather than defaults, is strictly more honest than layer-count offload knobs, and FlexGen's ablation is the evidence that the policy layer is where the performance lives.
2. *Place the three tensor kinds independently.* The fleet-relevant corollary of the 1.2 TB KV example: weights, KV, and activations have different size curves (KV grows with context × batch; weights are fixed), so a single "offload amount" knob conflates three decisions. A runner that exposes — or at least internally searches — the three fractions separately is implementing FlexGen's actual lesson.
3. *Delegation is placement too.* The $s\times$ attention argument applies verbatim to a 6 GB card with CPU-resident KV at long context: at some sequence length, computing attention where the cache lives beats shipping the cache. The crossover is computable from the same measured bandwidths.

**(b) What not to import.** The 4-bit group-wise scheme as a *format* — the fleet's quantisation formats (GGUF lineage) are compute-side formats with their own dossiers, and FlexGen's transport quantisation would need dequantise-on-arrival machinery whose only payoff is at batch sizes the interactive workload never reaches. And the disk tier as a *live* tier for interactive decode: FlexGen needs it only because its models are 10–20× its GPU; on the fleet, a model that requires live disk reads per token is a batch-mode model, and the runner should say so rather than stream it hopefully.

- **`papers/infinigen.md` (Wave 15) — the descendant.** InfiniGen builds on FlexGen, inherits its CPU-pool KV design, and attacks the transfer bottleneck FlexGen's own regime creates (96.9% of block time in transfer, per InfiniGen's breakdown of the FlexGen baseline). Read together: FlexGen decides *where everything lives*; InfiniGen decides *what crosses, per layer, per token*.
