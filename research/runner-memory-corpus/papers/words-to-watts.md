# From Words to Watts: Benchmarking the Energy Costs of LLM Inference

## 1. Full citation

- **Title:** From Words to Watts: Benchmarking the Energy Costs of Large Language Model Inference
- **Authors:** Siddharth Samsi, Dan Zhao, Joseph McDonald, Baolin Li, Adam Michaleas, Michael Jones, William Bergeron, Jeremy Kepner, Devesh Tiwari, Vijay Gadepally — MIT (incl. MIT Lincoln Laboratory / MIT SuperCloud) with co-authors at NYU and Northeastern University, as printed in the paper
- **Venue / year:** 2023 IEEE High Performance Extreme Computing Conference (HPEC 2023), pp. 1–9. DOI: 10.1109/HPEC58863.2023.10363447
- **arXiv:** arXiv:2310.03003 [cs.CL] — https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03003 (submitted 4 Oct 2023; v1, the version read for this dossier)
- **Standing:** the authors describe the work as a *preliminary* analysis and, to their knowledge, one of the first studies of LLM inference performance from the computational-and-energy perspective at multi-node, multi-GPU scale. That self-description is part of the citation: the numbers below are a first map, drawn by its makers as one.

## 2. The problem as framed

The paper opens where its sibling in this wave (`papers/power-hungry-processing.md`) opens — training had an energy literature; inference, which in deployment happens incomparably more often, did not — and then narrows to the question a *systems* group can answer with instruments: for one representative open LLM family, run properly at scale, what does inference actually cost in time, throughput, and joules, and how do those three move against each other when the operator's real knobs turn — model size, GPU generation, number of GPUs the model is sharded across, batch size, generation length, and GPU power cap?

The framing matters for this corpus because it is the inverse of the usual runner benchmark. A runner benchmark typically reports a rate (tokens/second) on one GPU and stops. Samsi et al. treat the rate as one of three coupled outputs of a configuration, the other two being average power draw and energy per unit of work. Their implicit claim — made explicit by their power-capping experiment (§4) — is that the configuration that maximises the rate is in general *not* the configuration that minimises the cost of the work, and you cannot see the difference until you measure energy as a first-class output. Operators were optimising tok/s while the binding constraint — at datacentre scale a power/cooling envelope, at laptop scale heat and battery (§6a) — was watts.

Scope, stated by the paper itself: it does **not** control for output correctness/quality, and does **not** evaluate quantisation, continuous batching, or query-arrival-rate modelling — all named as future work. Its serving model is offline batch inference over a fixed prompt set, not an interactive server. Those absences bound every transfer in §5–§6.

## 3. Core method in our own words

- **Models:** Meta's LLaMA (first generation), four sizes defined — 7B, 13B, 33B, 65B — with the analysis centred on **LLaMA 65B** (80 layers, model dimension 8,192, 64 attention heads, pre-trained on 1.4T tokens) and **7B / 13B as bare-minimum-hardware baselines**. Open-source PyTorch implementation with the FairScale library for **model sharding** (tensor-parallel splitting of one model across GPUs/nodes). Decoding settings held constant: temperature τ = 0.8, top-p = 0.95. No compression, quantisation, or distillation anywhere in the study — full models, sharded to fit.
- **Workloads:** two datasets, chosen to differ in kind — **Alpaca** (52,000 instruction-following examples; natural-language instructions, some with inputs) and **GSM8K** (8,500 human-written grade-school maths problems; shorter, differently-structured contexts and outputs). **4,096 prompts sampled** from each per experiment. A single experiment = one pass over the 4,096 prompts at a stated batch size, shard count, and maximum generation length (256 / 512 / 1,024; 2,048 in the utilisation study).
- **Hardware:** the **MIT SuperCloud** HPC system. Two node types (paper Table I), each with 2 CPUs + 2 GPUs: (i) Intel Xeon Gold 6248, 384 GB RAM, **NVIDIA V100 32 GB** (GPU TDP 250 W); (ii) Intel Xeon Platinum 8358, 503 GB RAM, **NVIDIA A100 80 GB** (GPU TDP 300 W). All experiments on GPUs, over the 25 Gb/s Ethernet interconnect (not the 100 Gb/s Omni-Path fabric). Large distributed runs (8/16/32 shards) mainly on V100s for availability; smaller-scale runs on A100s.
- **Bare-minimum configurations (paper Table II):** the fewest GPUs that hold each model, and the maximum batch they then allow at max generation length 256 — 7B: 1× V100 or 1× A100, max batch 64; 13B: 2× V100 or 1× A100, max batch 64; 65B: 8× V100 or 4× A100, max batch 64 (V100) / 128 (A100). The 65B baseline comparisons run at batch 64, max length 256.
- **Instruments:** **nvidia-smi** sampling GPU state every **100 ms**, and NVIDIA **DCGM** for aggregate GPU energy in joules on the rank-0 node; for multi-node runs, total energy = rank-0 energy × number of nodes (an extrapolation the paper states plainly — per-node symmetry is assumed, not measured). **GPU power draw capped at 250 W maximum unless otherwise stated** — note this is below the A100's 300 W TDP in Table I; the cap is part of every result's conditions. CPU/host energy is *not* in the ledger: this is GPU energy.
- **Metrics (the paper's definitions, §III-C):** throughput as **words/second, tokens/second, and responses/second** (words by whitespace split of the output string; tokens by LLaMA's own tokenizer; responses = completed generations). Energy as three derived quantities, each = total aggregate GPU energy ÷ denominator: **energy per second** (i.e. mean power, in watts), **energy per decoded output token** (joules/token), and **energy per response** (joules/response). Every energy figure in §4 is one of these three, under the conditions stated with it.

## 4. Reported results — as reported, with full context

All figures are the paper's own, under §3's conditions (GPU-only energy via DCGM, 250 W cap unless stated, 4,096 prompts, τ=0.8 / top-p 0.95, unquantised LLaMA, offline batch inference). Following Wave 10's rule — *no number without its conditions* (`papers/mlperf-inference.md`) — shard count, batch, dataset, and generation length travel with each figure below, because the paper's central finding is that they change the answer.

**Hardware generation: faster costs more per second (7B/13B/65B baselines; batch 64, max length 256; bare-minimum GPU counts per Table II):**

- A100 beats V100 on throughput for all three models on both datasets — by ~**2× for LLaMA 7B** and ~**1.25× for 13B** (words, tokens, and responses per second alike); for **65B the A100 advantage is much smaller**, which the paper attributes to multi-node sharding latency on every forward pass (65B needs 4× A100 across 2 nodes or 8× V100 across 4 nodes at minimum).
- The throughput gain is **not free**: energy per second (mean watts) is *higher* on A100 than V100 for all sizes on both datasets, with the largest increase again at 7B. For 65B the paper explicitly questions whether the small throughput gain is worth the higher power draw. Dataset matters too: 7B's A100 improvement is considerably larger on Alpaca than on GSM8K — input complexity moves both rate and energy at fixed configuration.

**Sharding: more GPUs, more watts, more joules per token (LLaMA 65B, V100s, 8/16/32 shards):**

- Mean power for 65B inference sits **on the order of 300 W to 1 kW**, rising from the 8-GPU to the 32-GPU configuration. Within a fixed batch size, **increasing shard count always increases energy per second**; batch size correlates only slightly by comparison. Raising max generation length 512 → 1,024 raises watts somewhat, inconsistently.
- **Energy per decoded token rises with shard count** on both datasets at both generation lengths studied. At max length 512 the figure is **~3–4 joules per output token** (65B, across the shard/batch grid); length 1,024 gives approximately the same joules/token — generation length has negligible effect on the per-token price in this range.
- **Energy per response** follows the same pattern: shard count is the dominant term; batch size nudges it up slightly and inconsistently within a shard configuration; generation length 512 → 1,024 shows no clear effect.
- One exception, reported as tentative by the paper itself: on **GSM8K at 16 shards and max length 512**, increasing batch size *reduced* energy per token (and per response) — a possible "sweet spot." It **disappears at length 1,024**, and the paper states plainly that more experimentation is required to establish whether such sweet spots are real. It is recorded here as an unconfirmed anomaly, not a batching law.

**Power capping: the wave's most transferable experiment (LLaMA 65B, GSM8K, batch 64, 4× A100 80GB; caps 250 W baseline vs 175 W vs 150 W; paper Table III):**

| Cap | Output length | Time change | Total energy change | Token-rate change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 175 W | 256 | +6.23% | −21.82% | −5.87% |
| 175 W | 512 | +6.51% | −23.95% | −6.11% |
| 175 W | 1,024 | +7.40% | −23.87% | −6.89% |
| 150 W | 256 | +15.33% | −32.76% | −13.15% |
| 150 W | 512 | +21.70% | −34.66% | −17.83% |
| 150 W | 1,024 | +21.65% | −34.59% | −17.80% |

- Headline, as the paper averages it: a **30% power-cap reduction (250 → 175 W) costs +6.7% inference time and buys −23.21% total energy**. The exchange rate then deteriorates: at 150 W the time penalty roughly triples (+19.49% average) for roughly one-and-a-half times the energy saving. There is a knee, and it is at the moderate cap — the paper cautions that a single static cap for all workloads will not show the same effectiveness, and that broader recommendation needs more experiment.

**Utilisation: the GPUs are busy, the memory is not (65B, batch 64):**

- Streaming-multiprocessor utilisation is **94–95%** across shardings (V100, length 256), rising to ~98% on A100 at longer outputs. But **GPU memory utilisation is only ~23–27%** on the 4× A100 configuration and falls per-GPU as shards multiply (max ~24% at 8 V100 shards → ~7% at 32). Sharding to *fit* a model over-provisions memory several-fold — the paper's own conclusion is that co-locating multiple models per GPU (MPS/MIG sharing) is the unexplored efficiency frontier in its setup, i.e. its joules/token figures are measured on deliberately under-shared hardware.

## 5. The metric lesson

*[Paper-supported statements are unmarked; our inference for runner practice is labelled.]*

The paper hands the corpus its LLM-native energy unit — **joules per decoded token** (equivalently, its reciprocal, tokens/joule) — and then demonstrates, three ways, why it must sit *alongside* tokens/second rather than be derived from it:

1. **The rankings genuinely disagree.** A100 out-ranks V100 on tok/s for every model tested and is *out-ranked by it* on watts drawn; for 65B the paper cannot say the trade is worth it. Shard count raises aggregate throughput capacity while monotonically worsening joules/token. If tok/s and J/token always agreed, one metric would do; in this data they repeatedly do not.
2. **Mean power is not the price of work.** Energy/second (watts) rises with batch size only slightly and with shards strongly, while energy/*token* is near-flat across generation lengths and batch sizes in most of the grid — because a configuration that draws more watts for proportionally less time can cost the same per token. The laptop translation (§6a): a runner's *peak* or *average* wattage is a thermal statement, not an efficiency statement; efficiency is watts ÷ tok/s at the same instant, accumulated per token.
3. **The frontier is movable by fiat.** Power capping does not trade along a fixed speed–energy curve so much as reveal that the default operating point was chosen for speed alone: −23% energy for +7% time at 175 W is a trade most deployments, shown the numbers, would take for most workloads. No tok/s-only benchmark can even *display* this option.

Where the two rankings *do* agree is equally instructive: within a fixed shard count and model, the paper finds no large batching dividend in joules/token (the one GSM8K sweet spot aside, §4) — because its workload is offline batches already at batch 64+, far past the point where fixed costs amortise. *[Our inference, labelled:]* the familiar serving claim "batching raises throughput *and* efficiency" is a claim about the low-batch regime (batch 1 → small batch, where per-step fixed costs — kernel launches, weight reads per step shared across sequences — dominate); Samsi et al. measure the plateau beyond it, where additional batch buys throughput head-room but little per-token energy. Both can be true; the paper disciplines *where* the claim stops.

*[Our inference, labelled:]* the personal-scale corollary the papers support only indirectly — neither measures a single-stream laptop — is that **idle and baseline power dominate at personal scale**: a single user issues requests sparsely, so the energy ledger of a local runner is mostly the cost of keeping a multi-GB model resident and a GPU awake between requests, divided by very few tokens. Samsi's rank-0 × nodes extrapolation and Luccioni's sibling-GPU idle footnote are the datacentre shadows of the same fact: *unattributed baseline draw is part of every honest energy number*, and at n=1 user it is most of it.

## 6. Applicability

### (a) The personal fleet

*[Engineering judgement, labelled as such — the paper measured V100/A100 nodes in an HPC centre; everything here is transfer, with the mechanism named so the transfer can be judged.]*

- **Power capping is the single most transferable finding in this wave for a laptop runner** (see the report summary). A laptop GPU already lives under a power cap — the Max-Q variant in Volmarr's 6 GB RTX 2060 machine *is* a capped part — and Samsi's knee at −30% cap says the capped operating point is not merely a thermal compromise: it is close to the energy-optimal one, buying ~23% energy for ~7% time on the only workload measured. The runner-level consequence: an "efficiency mode" that lowers the GPU power limit (or, on the Strix Halo APU, the package power limit) and accepts single-digit-percent slower decode is a real feature with a measured exchange rate, not a placebo — and on battery it converts directly into minutes.
- **Tokens/joule is the battery-range metric.** A laptop user asking "how long can I run this model on battery?" is asking for Wh per 1,000 tokens at their settings — Samsi's joules/token × token count, plus the machine's baseline draw × wall-clock time (§5's idle corollary). A runner can surface both terms honestly: per-request joules for the generation itself, and a session Wh total that includes residency. Reporting only the first is the laptop form of reporting only tok/s.
- **What an energy-aware runner could expose:** (i) live tokens/joule next to tok/s in the status line — same instruments, one division; (ii) a per-request energy estimate in the response metadata (joules, at the measured mean power for that request); (iii) an efficiency mode = power cap + (per the sibling dossier) output-length discipline + smallest-adequate model, presented with its expected exchange rate ("~7% slower, ~20% less energy" as a *measured-on-other-hardware* prior, to be re-measured locally before it is quoted as local fact); (iv) a warning, not a default, when a configuration shards/spills across devices — the laptop analogue of shard count — since §4 shows distribution itself carries a joules/token tax.
- **What does not transfer:** the absolute numbers. 3–4 J/token is a 65B model sharded over datacentre GPUs at a 250 W cap, GPU-energy-only, unquantised. A quantised 7B on one laptop GPU will sit far below it; quoting Samsi's figure as a laptop expectation would violate the very disclosure rule this wave exists to teach. The *shape* transfers (cap knee, shard tax, baseline dominance); the magnitudes must be re-measured per machine — which is precisely what Wave 10's disclosure set, plus the energy line proposed in the sibling dossier §6b, is for.

### (b) The corpus

Adopt **joules/token (and its reciprocal, tokens/joule) as a first-class column** in future benchmark reporting in this corpus, with the same standing as decode tok/s:

- Reported **per phase where the instrument allows** (prefill is compute-dense and short; decode is the long, memory-bound tail — a single blended J/token hides which phase a change helped), and always with the Wave 10 minimal disclosure set (model+quant in bytes; prompt/output profile; machine state; repetitions/spread) **plus** the energy line proposed in `papers/power-hungry-processing.md` §6b (instrument named; total Wh/J; mean W; idle/baseline stated or labelled unmeasured).
- **Scope label mandatory:** GPU-only (DCGM/RAPL-package/nvidia-smi class) vs whole-system (wall meter) — Samsi is GPU-only, Luccioni is Code Carbon's GPU+CPU+RAM estimate including sibling idle; pooling the two scopes in one table without the label recreates, inside our own corpus, the incomparability both papers were written to end.
- For Aesir-side grading specifically: an energy figure, like a speed figure, is only bankable next to its faithfulness grade (raw-oracle rank/logit-gap band) — a quantisation or kernel change that wins tokens/joule by diverging is a different model, under the same constitution as Wave 10's quality floor.

## 7. Cross-links

- `papers/power-hungry-processing.md` (this wave, sibling) — the breadth counterpart: Luccioni et al. vary task and model class across all of ML and price the *task*; Samsi et al. fix one LLM family and price the *token* across serving configurations. The sibling supplies the energy disclosure line (§6b) this dossier's §6b adopts; this dossier supplies the per-token unit the sibling's per-task unit cannot express for open-ended generation.
- `papers/mlperf-inference.md` (Wave 10) — the measurement constitution both energy dossiers cite for the no-number-without-conditions rule. MLPerf's Server scenario is the formal version of this paper's missing piece: Samsi measures offline batches at fixed arrival (none), so his joules/token are Offline-scenario figures; an interactive deployment's energy at a latency SLO is unmeasured by either — the corpus should say so when citing them for serving claims.
- `papers/vidur.md` (Wave 10) — Vidur predicts latency and QPS-per-dollar for unrun configurations from profiled operators; energy is the natural third output for the same machinery (the operators that take the time draw the power), and Samsi's grid (shards × batch × length, with measured joules) is exactly the validation shape a Vidur-style energy model would need. Neither paper does this; flagged as a programme direction, not a result.
- `memory/memory-technologies-dram-cxl.md` — the microscopic ledger beneath this dossier's system-level one. Horowitz's per-access ladder (45 nm: ~640 pJ per 32-bit DRAM access vs ~5 pJ for a small SRAM read — see the NPU dossier for the sourced table) explains *why* decode energy behaves as Samsi measures it: decode streams the full weight set from DRAM per token, so joules/token tracks bytes-moved-per-token, and sharding's joules/token tax is the ladder paid again at every device boundary (weights and activations crossing interconnects instead of staying on one die's cheap rungs). The two dossiers are one argument at two magnifications.
- `memory/npu-memory-and-dataflows.md` — energy per access as the NPU argument, stated architecturally there and confirmed at system scale here: the dataflow programme (minimise DRAM touches per MAC; keep reuse on-chip) and Samsi's results (fewer devices touched per token → fewer joules per token; memory utilisation ~25% while SMs sit at ~95% — the machine is movement-bound, not compute-bound) are the same diagnosis. For the fleet: the Strix Halo's unified-memory APU is, in this light, an anti-sharding machine — one memory, no per-token interconnect tax — which is an energy argument for it quite apart from its bandwidth figures.
