## 2. The problem as framed

FastServe's target is not execution speed but *waiting*. Its framing (§§1–2 of the paper text):

- **Run-to-completion + FCFS = head-of-line blocking.** Orca introduced iteration-level scheduling — the batch is re-decided after every token — and vLLM added PagedAttention, but both, as the paper stresses, still schedule first-come-first-served and, once a job is admitted, run it to completion. GPU memory caps the batch, so a long job occupying the batch blocks every later job behind it. The paper's Figure 1 quantifies the stakes on real length distributions: with fixed-length jobs there is almost no queuing delay even at load ≈ 1, but under ShareGPT/Alpaca's skewed lengths, **queuing delay rises to up to 90% of total latency**. Optimising execution time then optimises the small term.
- **Output length is unknown at arrival — and that kills the textbook answer.** The optimal preemptive policy for mean latency, Shortest Remaining Processing Time (SRPT), needs each job's remaining size. For an LLM job the per-iteration time is predictable but the *number of iterations* — the output length — is set by the semantics of the generation and is not known a priori. The paper names this the **semi-information-agnostic** setting: half the size information exists (input length, hence first-token time, is known exactly), half does not (output length). Any scheduler must be designed for that asymmetry, not despite it.
- **Preemption is not free — it is a memory problem.** A preempted job's KV cache must be kept for its resumption. Under FCFS only the running batch's KV exists; under preemption, *every started-but-unfinished job's* KV exists. The paper prices it: one OPT-175B job with a 512-token input needs **at least 2.3 GB** of KV cache ($4 \cdot l \cdot h \cdot (s{+}t)$ bytes in FP16), and in its synthetic-workload measurement the skip-join MLFQ's peak KV demand reaches **7× FCFS's** (OPT-2.7B, output capped at 20 tokens). A preemptive scheduler without a memory mechanism re-creates blocking in a new form: defer new jobs when KV is full (MLFQ degenerates to FCFS) or kill-and-recompute low-priority jobs (wasted work, and a promotion/kill deadlock the paper spells out).

In one sentence: **the queue, not the kernel, was the latency — but reordering the queue is only possible if you can preempt per token, guess job size from the half you know, and pay the KV bill for every job you have started and not finished.**

## 3. Core mechanism in our own words

Three mechanisms, designed as a set: a scheduler that reorders, a rule that places arrivals, and a memory system that makes reordering affordable.

**Token-granularity preemption.** Because generation is autoregressive and the engine already runs iteration-level scheduling, the natural preemption point is *every output token*: after each iteration the scheduler may continue a job or preempt it for another. Nothing about the model changes; the decision cadence is the mechanism. On top of that cadence FastServe runs a priority scheduler instead of FCFS.

**Skip-join Multi-Level Feedback Queue (MLFQ).** The classic MLFQ — the standard approximation to SRPT when job sizes are unknown (a practical form of Least-Attained Service) — keeps $n$ queues $Q_1 \dots Q_n$ with strictly increasing quanta $q_1 < q_2 < \dots < q_n$ (in FastServe, each quantum doubles the previous; $q_1$ is set to the minimum iteration time). A job that exhausts its quantum unfinished is *demoted* to a lower queue — in FastServe's Algorithm 1, demoted by $\eta$ levels, keyed to its next iteration's time — so jobs reveal their length by living: short jobs finish in the high queues, long jobs sink. Three FastServe-specific elements sit on top of the classic design:

1. **Skip-join entry (the title mechanism).** A naive MLFQ arrival always enters $Q_1$ — which LLM inference breaks, because the *first* iteration (prefill over the whole prompt) can alone exceed the top quanta: preempting mid-prefill discards the activations; not preempting betrays the discipline. FastServe instead *profiles* iteration time as a function of input length (per model/hardware, in advance — per-iteration time is predictable like any one-shot DNN inference), predicts the arrival's initialisation time $t_{init}$ from its known input length, and joins it directly at the highest-priority queue whose quantum covers it: the smallest $i$ with $q_i \ge t_{init}$. Higher queues are *skipped* — hence the name — which both respects the atomicity of prefill and removes pointless demotions (a long-prompt job was never a "short job" candidate, and the scheduler now knows it on arrival). This is the semi-information-agnostic idea made operational: use the half of the size you know to place the job; let demotion discover the half you don't.
2. **Demotion by attained service.** Each further quantum consumed without finishing demotes the job again, so priority is a running, evidence-based estimate of remaining size — SRPT approximated by behaviour rather than by prediction.
3. **Starvation guard.** Demotion can strand long jobs indefinitely under load. FastServe periodically promotes any job whose starvation time reaches a threshold $\alpha$ (tuned to the user's SLO; **300 ms by default** in the paper) back to $Q_1$ and resets its clock. The guard is evaluated, not just asserted: the paper's tail-latency results (§4) are its evidence.

**Proactive KV-cache swapping.** To pay §2's memory bill, FastServe extends the KV cache into host memory: KV of jobs not currently scheduled is offloaded to host RAM and uploaded on return — *proactively*, overlapped with the running jobs' GPU execution, so the swap is off the critical path. (The scale of the problem: for OPT-175B on 16× A100, one job's KV is ~2.3 GB; a reactive swap over PCIe 4.0×16 takes ~36 ms against a ~60 ms decode iteration — sequential swapping would eat the schedule.) Swap order is decided by an **Estimated Next Scheduled Time (ENST)** per job — the minimum of its starvation-promotion time and the summed quanta of higher-priority work ahead of it, divided by batch size — swapping *out* the largest-ENST job first and *in* the smallest. A reserve of idle KV slots, sized from historical arrival patterns, is held for bursts of new arrivals so a high-priority arrival never waits on a reactive eviction. The prototype also integrates PagedAttention, so the swapping works over paged blocks.

**Distributed elements.** The same design is carried across GPUs: tensor and pipeline parallelism for models that fit nowhere singly (OPT-175B evaluated on 16× A100), with the KV cache partitioned as the parallelism dictates, per-stage offload/upload decisions propagated with the intermediate results so swapping overlaps inter-stage transmission, and a pipeline-aware scheduling rule (a job in flight through the pipeline stays in its queue while the scheduler fills stage bubbles with the highest-priority pending job). Implementation, as reported: RESTful frontend + scheduler in ~2.9K lines of Python, distributed execution engine in ~8.1K lines of C++/CUDA, Ray actors as GPU workers, custom kernels for iteration-level scheduling and PagedAttention.

## 4. Reported results — as reported, with context

All figures are the paper's own. **Nothing below was measured on consumer hardware, at batch size 1, or on a single-user workload.**

**Setup (paper §6.1).** Testbed: two AWS EC2 p4d.24xlarge instances, each 8× NVIDIA **A100-40GB** (NVLink), 1,152 GB host memory, PCIe 4.0×16; design-choice ablations on a single A100-40GB. Models: **OPT-13B (1 GPU), OPT-66B (4 GPUs), OPT-175B (16 GPUs)**, all FP16. Workloads: ShareGPT and Alpaca length distributions with **Poisson arrivals** (neither dataset carries arrival times; the paper follows vLLM in synthesising them — the assumption BurstGPT later measured against reality; see §5). Metric: average per-token latency (job end-to-end latency ÷ output length) and P95 tail latency; headline comparisons are *maximum throughput under a latency SLO*, the SLO set to 10× the single decode-iteration latency (0.3 s in their profiling). Baselines: FasterTransformer v5.3, **vLLM v0.1.7**, and FastServe-FCFS (FastServe's own engine with its scheduling/memory techniques removed — the control that separates scheduler gains from implementation gains).

**Headline results, by version (see the §1 warning):**

- **v3 abstract (current):** vs vLLM, throughput improved by up to **31.4×** under the same average-latency requirement and **17.9×** under the same tail-latency requirement.
- **v1 text (§6.2 detail):** vs vLLM, throughput under the SLO improved by **2.3–18.3× on ShareGPT** and **3–31.4× on Alpaca** across the three model sizes; vs FasterTransformer by 31.5–74.9× (ShareGPT) and 9.5–15.8× (Alpaca). The earlier v1 abstract's Orca comparison — average JCT up to **5.1×** and tail JCT up to **6.4×** better — belongs to that version's framing and baseline.
- **The scheduler's isolated share:** FastServe-FCFS already beats vLLM (the paper credits its C++ implementation and kernel fusion), but full FastServe beats FastServe-FCFS by **2–4×** (ShareGPT average latency) and 1.6–2× (Alpaca) — the defensible attribution of the gain to skip-join MLFQ + proactive swapping rather than to engineering. On tail latency (ShareGPT), full FastServe beats its FCFS control by up to 1.5× (OPT-175B) and 2–2.8× (OPT-13B/66B), and vLLM by up to 17.9×.
- **Goodput check:** at P95 goodput under 5×/10×/20× phase SLOs (OPT-13B), FastServe beats vLLM by **4.1–4.7×** and its FCFS control by 1.46–1.64×, consistently across SLO tightness.
- **Worked example (paper Fig. 7):** three simultaneous jobs, quanta 1/2/4/8 — average latency: FCFS 4.23, naive MLFQ 5.0 (worse than FCFS: the mid-prefill preemption dilemma, §3), **skip-join MLFQ 3.3**, SRPT oracle 3.0. The toy example carries the paper's thesis in miniature: the skip-join entry rule, not MLFQ alone, is what closes most of the gap to the oracle.

Read the multipliers as the paper's own framing demands: they are *throughput-at-fixed-SLO* ratios in a regime where the baseline's throughput collapses because its queue is blocked — a statement about how bad FCFS queuing gets under skewed lengths at load, as much as about how good MLFQ is. At light load, with no queue, there is nothing for the mechanism to win.

## 5. Position in the corpus

**Two answers to head-of-line blocking.** The corpus holds both, and they attack different halves of the phenomenon:

- **FastServe (2023): reorder the queue.** Keep the step size as it is (one token per job per iteration), but decide *which* jobs occupy the batch by revealed length — demote generators as they generate, admit short jobs past long ones, preempt at token boundaries. The blocking it removes is *queuing* blocking: a short request no longer waits for a long generation ahead of it in line.
- **Sarathi-Serve (2024): bound the step.** Keep the admission order essentially FCFS-with-a-budget, but cut the other unbounded unit — the whole-prompt prefill — into token-budgeted chunks mixed decode-first into every step (`papers/sarathi-serve.md`). The blocking it removes is *stall* blocking: no single iteration can run long, so no decode stream can be frozen by a neighbour's prompt.

**Which won in practice, from the corpus's record:** chunked prefill won, decisively and visibly. It is enabled by default in vLLM V1 (whose scheduler is a unified per-step token budget with no separate prefill/decode phases), shipped in SGLang as a first-class knob, and treated by DistServe as the strongest colocated baseline (see the banked `runners/vllm.md`, `runners/sglang.md`, and `papers/sarathi-serve.md` §5). MLFQ-for-inference did not become the standard shape: no mainstream engine in the corpus's runner dossiers exposes a multi-level feedback queue as its scheduler, and vLLM's own evolution went the other way — FCFS/priority admission plus preemption-by-recompute/swap as a *memory* mechanism, with the step bounded by budget rather than the queue stratified by attained service. The reasons are legible from the two papers themselves: chunking is a *local* change to batch assembly with a bounded, measurable cost (Sarathi-Serve prices it at ≤ ~25% prefill overhead at its smallest budget, negligible at its largest) and no new state to keep; MLFQ is a *global* scheduling discipline whose precondition is holding every started job's KV somewhere — FastServe's own 7× peak-KV measurement is the price tag, and its proactive swapping machinery is most of the system's complexity. On memory-starved GPUs, the technique that spends no memory beat the technique that spends memory cleverly. There is also an workload-historical reason: BurstGPT (sibling dossier, this wave) showed real scheduling rankings flip between service types — a global queueing discipline is a bigger bet on workload shape than a step bound, which helps under every arrival process because it constrains the scheduler's own worst case rather than predicting the workload's.

**What MLFQ still offers that chunking does not — stated fairly.** Chunked prefill bounds the damage a *prompt* can do to neighbours; it does nothing about the damage a *long generation* does. A 4,000-token generation admitted under FCFS still occupies its batch slot, its KV, and its share of every step for its whole life, and a short interactive request arriving behind several such generations waits — chunking or no chunking — because decode slots, not prefill steps, are the contended resource. FastServe's demotion is precisely the protection for that case: priority decays with attained service, so the system's favour flows continuously toward jobs that have not yet consumed much — which, in a mixed workload, are disproportionately the short, interactive, latency-sensitive ones. The protection matters most exactly where chunking is silent: **when output lengths vary wildly** (the long tail both papers document) **and when preemption is expensive** — where a job's KV is large, host-memory swapping is slow, or recompute is prohibitive, a scheduler that *avoids* starting the wrong job at the wrong priority (skip-join placement) and *gracefully deprioritises* rather than evicts (demotion instead of preemption-by-recompute) preserves work that a cruder discipline discards. Modern engines re-derive fragments of this under other names — priority queues, SLO-aware admission, preemption modes — which is the usual afterlife of a good queueing idea: absorbed as instincts, not adopted as architecture.

**Lineage placement.** FastServe sits directly on Orca (`papers/orca.md`): Orca made the batch re-decidable per iteration; FastServe asked what policy should do the deciding, and answered with operating-systems queueing theory a decade older than the field. BurstGPT's Table 1 returns the compliment from the measurement side, using FastServe's synthetic workload as its example of lengths-without-concurrency. And its semi-information-agnostic framing — *input known, output unknown* — is the durable conceptual export: every later output-length-prediction and speculative-scheduling design in the serving literature is an attempt to buy back, with a predictor, the half of SRPT that FastServe routed around with feedback.

## 6. Applicability

### (a) A personal runner: queueing mostly evaporates — per-session fairness survives

A single user issuing one turn at a time has no queue, no head of line, and nothing to demote; FastServe's throughput-at-SLO multipliers are fleet numbers under Poisson load on A100 clusters and transfer not at all. The moment the local picture gains a second concurrent obligation, however, a miniature of FastServe's problem appears — and the local workload supplies it routinely: a long generation from one persona is still streaming when the user addresses a second persona (or interrupts with a short interactive turn — a correction, a stop-and-ask, a quick factual query); sidecars (memory extraction, summarisation) contend with the visible turn; a swipe/regenerate races the generation it replaces.

For that multi-persona case, FastServe's transferable content is an *intuition with a mechanism attached* (labelled **judgement**, the local form being this dossier's translation, not the paper's claim):

- **Demote by attained service, per session.** Priority for the next step should decay with how much generation a session has already consumed in this contention episode, so a persona mid-way through a 2,000-token monologue progressively yields step-share to a persona asked a 30-token question — instead of FCFS locking the short turn behind the long one until it finishes or is cancelled. The local currency is not throughput but *responsiveness symmetry*: no session's inter-token gap should be set by another session's verbosity.
- **Skip-join's local form is admission honesty:** a request whose *known* cost is already large (a long prompt's prefill, priced exactly as FastServe prices $t_{init}$) should not be admitted as if it were a lightweight arrival ahead of interactive work; place it in the schedule at the priority its known cost earns, and let its unknown output length be discovered by demotion.
- **The starvation guard is not optional in miniature either:** demotion without promotion is how a background persona's long turn never finishes in a busy group scene. FastServe's periodic promotion (its $\alpha$ tuned to an SLO; locally, to a human-scale "no session waits more than a few seconds for its next token") is the difference between fairness and a different unfairness.
- **What does *not* miniaturise:** proactive KV swapping. On a 6 GB-class card with host RAM behind PCIe, swapping a persona's KV out to make room is the same arithmetic FastServe faced (tens of ms per GB-scale cache against tens-of-ms token steps) with none of the fleet headroom; the local answers to KV pressure are the corpus's residency entries (IDEA-LEDGER #31's ordered shedding, §7), not a swap tier maintained for scheduling elegance. If sessions must alternate, alternating at *turn* boundaries with resident prefixes (ledger #1/#5) beats preempting mid-generation and paying swap or recompute per switch.

### (b) The design lesson that survives at every scale

FastServe's deepest export is not MLFQ; it is the demonstration that **the scheduler's policy is a product decision separable from the engine's speed** — FastServe-FCFS, its own control, is the proof: same engine, same kernels, 2–4× worse under load purely from policy. A personal runner makes policy decisions too (which session steps next, what a sidecar may preempt, what gets shed under pressure); FastServe argues those decisions deserve names, knobs, and a starvation bound, rather than emerging from whatever order the event loop happened to fire. That is a **proposal** for local design discipline, labelled as such; the paper supplies the evidence that policy is worth that much, at fleet scale, as-reported.
