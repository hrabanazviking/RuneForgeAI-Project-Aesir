# SkipDecode — SkipDecode: Autoregressive Skip Decoding with Batching and Caching for Efficient LLM Inference

*Ollama Research Forge, OR-3 Paper Sweep, Wave 24 (early exit). Companion dossier: `papers/layerskip.md`. This wave covers conditional compute in **depth**. LayerSkip (companion) builds the exit capability into the weights with a training recipe; SkipDecode is the serving-shaped member of the pair — it starts from the observation that the early-exit methods of its day were undeployable in real serving, and designs the exit policy around the batch and the KV cache instead of around the individual token.*

## 2. The problem as framed

SkipDecode accepts the standard early-exit premise — uniform depth wastes compute, because hidden states saturate: past some layer, further computation barely changes many tokens' predictions — and then argues that the existing token-level early-exit literature (its named foil is CALM, Schuster et al. 2022) solves the wrong problem. Per-token, confidence-driven exit works in single-sequence ("online") demos and collapses on contact with how LLMs are actually served. The paper names four practical blockers:

- **Batching.** In batched inference, sequences are processed in lockstep. If each token exits at its own layer, computation cannot stop until the *deepest* token in the batch at that position finishes — the batch's cost is set by its most expensive member, and the savings of every early exit in the batch evaporate. CALM as published supports batch size 1 only.
- **KV caching.** Autoregressive serving caches each token's keys and values per layer. If a later token exits *deeper* than an earlier token, the earlier token's KV entries for the layers in between were never computed — they must be recomputed (or back-filled by projecting a hidden state, CALM-style), taxing or voiding the saving. Exit depth that can *increase* along a sequence is structurally hostile to the cache.
- **Wasted context.** Under early *termination*, a token that exits at layer $k$ contributes nothing above layer $k$ for later tokens to attend to. Later tokens cannot benefit from computation earlier tokens performed — the deeper the earlier tokens went, the more of it is stranded below their own exit.
- **Cost uncertainty.** A learned per-token exit classifier gives no budget guarantee: the worst case (tokens exiting near the top) is the full network, so throughput and latency are unpredictable — a serving system cannot provision against a policy whose cost is data-dependent per token.

The design goal follows directly: an exit policy whose cost is **fixed in advance**, which is **uniform across a batch at each position**, **never deepens along a sequence**, and still captures most of the saturation saving. Note what this framing concedes up front: the policy will *not* be per-token adaptive. SkipDecode trades the oracle's per-token optimality for serving regularity — deliberately, and as its main claim to novelty.

## 3. Core mechanism in our own words

**One exit point per position, shared by the whole batch.** Batches are organised column-wise: a batch is the set of tokens at sequence position $n$ across all sequences. SkipDecode assigns a single exit layer $L(n)$ per position, with $L(t_{b,n})$ identical for every batch member $b$. Every sequence in the batch does exactly the same amount of work at each step, so the batch finishes together and the theoretical saving is the realised saving. This is the paper's "batch-uniform exit budget": the budget is a property of the *position*, not of any token's content or confidence.

**Monotonically decreasing exit along the sequence.** $L(n)$ never increases with $n$. The stated rationale is empirical: average loss per token position falls monotonically as a sequence progresses (the paper's Figure 1, OPT-350M on OpenWebText and on fine-tuned Reddit-TLDR) — early tokens, predicted from little context, are hard and error-prone, and their errors cascade autoregressively; later tokens, rich in context, are easier and their hidden states saturate sooner. So the policy spends the most depth first and decays. The serving payoff is exact: because every earlier token was computed at least as deeply as every later token, a later token's attention always finds the KV entries it needs already cached. **No KV recomputation, ever, by construction.**

**A static, budgeted decay schedule — not a learned policy.** Exit points are assigned by a pre-specified function bounded by two hyperparameters, a maximum exit layer (assigned to the first generated token) and a minimum exit layer (assigned at maximum sequence length), with the paper's evaluation using **linear decay** between them (a power-law variant was tried preliminarily and, as reported, did not improve on linear). The prompt is always processed with the **full network**; decay applies only to generated positions. Because the schedule is fixed before generation starts, the total compute budget is known in advance and strictly bounded — blocker four is answered by making the policy boring. There is no exit classifier, no confidence threshold, and no per-token decision anywhere in the method; that absence *is* the method.

**Skipping, not terminating — and where the budget is spent.** The subtlest part of the design: a token assigned $k$ layers of compute does not run the *bottom* $k$ layers and stop (early termination). It runs **1 warmup layer** at the bottom (the paper reports 1 warmup layer worked best across all settings — it bridges the representation gap between the raw embedding and the upper layers), **skips the lower-to-middle layers**, and spends the rest of its budget on the **top layers**. Two reasons, both stated in the paper: (i) attending later tokens can then read this token's *top-layer* representations — the full available context is usable by every subsequent token, answering blocker three; (ii) the upper layers are where hidden states saturate, so compute spent there is spent where the prediction is actually settled. "Exit point" in SkipDecode therefore means something slightly different from the rest of the early-exit literature: the token exits *into* the head from the top of the network as usual; what it skipped was the middle.

**Training requirement, as stated.** The paper is explicit that, beyond the skipping policy, it "does not necessitate any additional modifications to the transformer architecture, either during training or generation" — no new heads, no classifiers, no losses. But the evaluated models are **not** untouched pretrained checkpoints: each dataset's model is **fine-tuned with the skipping policy in force** (per-dataset training runs with stated epochs — E2E 8, Reddit-TLDR 3, CNN/DM 2 — learning rates swept over 2e-4 to 8e-6, hyperparameters including max/min/warmup layers tuned per target speedup on the E2E validation set by perplexity, and training sequences constructed so all layers are processed for the prompt portion, mimicking the generation-time behaviour). LayerSkip's related-work summary describes SkipDecode in exactly these terms: it "fine-tuned a decoder-only model to skip more layers for later tokens within a sequence." The weights, in other words, are adapted to the skip pattern before the skip pattern is served.

## 4. Reported results — as reported, with context

All numbers are the paper's own (arXiv v1 text, read 2026-11-15), in the paper's frame. Models: **OPT 1.3B (24 layers) and OPT 6.7B (32 layers)**, decoder-only. Datasets: **E2E** (structured-data-to-text), **Reddit-TLDR** (summarisation), **CNN/DM** (summarisation). Generation: beam 1, top-sampling 0.7, temperature 0.3. Crucially, speedups are measured **against the full OPT baseline that itself supports batching and KV caching** — the paper explicitly contrasts this with prior early-exit work that reported speedups against a batch-size-1, no-KV-cache baseline. "Speedup" here is a *compute* target realised by configuration (average active layers), in the paper's own construction: configurations for 2×/3×/4×/5× correspond to average generation depths of e.g. 16/11/8/6.5 layers (6.7B) and 12/8/6/5 layers (1.3B), with 1 warmup layer and min/max bounds per Table 2 of the paper. The paper does not report wall-clock hardware timings in the text consulted; the honest reading of its headline is "2×–5× less layer-compute at these quality deltas, in a serving-compatible shape."

**Headline claim (abstract): 2× to 5× inference speedups with negligible regression.** The detailed table supports a narrower version of that claim, and the paper's own §3.3 says so: **at 2×, there is "almost no degradation in all cases"; beyond 2×, quality declines steadily**, fastest on the hardest dataset. Selected cells (BLEU / ROUGE-L / BERT-F, 1× → 2× → 5×):

- *E2E, 1.3B:* 65.8 / 67.6 / 70.3 → 66.3 / 67.9 / 67.8 → 64.2 / 66.3 / 65.2. E2E barely degrades even at 5× (some 2×–3× cells sit *above* baseline on BLEU/ROUGE-L as reported).
- *E2E, 6.7B:* 64.2 / 66.6 / 70.8 → 65.3 / 68.2 / 67.6 → 64.0 / 65.7 / 65.2.
- *Reddit-TLDR, 1.3B:* 9.0 / 27.3 / 31.9 → 8.9 / 27.5 / 32.1 → 3.0 / 19.6 / 7.1. The 2× point is intact; by 4×–5× BERT-F collapses (11.5, then 7.1).
- *Reddit-TLDR, 6.7B:* 9.6 / 28.3 / 33.7 → 9.3 / 27.7 / 32.3 → 4.0 / 19.3 / 7.4.
- *CNN/DM, 1.3B:* 15.8 / 29.5 / 35.9 → 15.0 / 28.9 / 34.8 → 4.0 / 18.1 / 2.5.
- *CNN/DM, 6.7B:* 16.3 / 30.2 / 37.1 → 15.2 / 29.6 / 35.9 → 4.6 / 18.5 / 2.7.

The paper's interpretation: quality holds until the average depth crosses the **hidden-state saturation point**, then falls; E2E saturates earliest (most forgiving), CNN/DM latest. Against the adapted baselines (OPT 1.3B, ROUGE-L): at 5× on E2E, SkipDecode 66.3 vs a fixed-policy multi-layer-exit network 46.7 and CALM-DEC 22.8; on Reddit-TLDR at 2×, SkipDecode 27.5 vs multi-layer 17.2, with CALM-DEC degrading sharply as speedup rises — the paper attributes CALM-DEC's decoder-only collapse to KV back-filling corrupting the prompt encoding, which in a decoder-only model *is* the context.

**Stated limitations (the paper's own §5):** the position-based decay policy breaks **continuous batching** — when a sequence in a batch finishes, a new sequence can only join if its current position matches the rest of the batch's positions, so the method "does not naturally support the 'infinite loop' inference mode"; the prompt is always run at full depth (extending the decay to the prompt is named as untested future work); alternative decay functions are largely unexplored.

## 5. Position in the family

- **The batch-uniform answer to the adaptivity question.** The early-exit family's founding gesture is per-token adaptivity — CALM's confidence measures, learned exit classifiers. SkipDecode's position is that per-token adaptivity is precisely what makes early exit undeployable, and it gives the adaptivity up: adaptivity survives only at the granularity of *sequence position* (a statistical regularity of autoregressive text), never of the individual token. In the vocabulary of this corpus's serving papers, SkipDecode is to early exit what a static batching schedule is to dynamic batching — less optimal per unit, actually schedulable. LayerSkip (companion) makes the complementary trade from the other side: a fixed exit layer $E$ per deployment for plain exit (also not per-token), with per-token correctness recovered not by a policy but by *verification*.
- **Self-speculation vs external drafting (Waves 2/13) — the contrast that locates SkipDecode.** Speculative decoding's verify math guarantees the full model's distribution whatever the draft; SkipDecode has **no verification stage at all** — skipped layers are simply never run, and the quality cost is real, measured, and accepted (§4). It therefore sits *outside* the speculation family despite sharing the "spend less per token" goal: it is conditional compute, not draft-and-verify. Its relation to LayerSkip's self-speculation is the instructive one: both use the model's own shallower computation as the cheap path, but LayerSkip pays a verification pass to buy exactness back, while SkipDecode pays fine-tuning and a conservative schedule to keep the unverified error small. Same shallow path; one verifies, the other budgets.
- **Early exit vs MoE (Wave 6).** Both are conditional compute: MoE conditions in **width** (per layer, per token, a router picks a subset of experts — fully per-token adaptive, and it pays for it in routing and load-balancing machinery), SkipDecode conditions in **depth** (per position, a static schedule picks how many layers, favouring the top). The difference that matters for serving: MoE's conditionality is *inside* each layer's matmuls and preserves the uniform layer count, so batching shapes stay regular almost for free; depth-conditional compute changes the *number of sequential steps* per token, which is exactly the dimension batching synchronises on — hence SkipDecode's whole design effort. Its static schedule is the price of making depth-conditional compute as batch-regular as width-conditional compute already is.

## 6. Applicability

**(a) Which side of the retrofit line? — the trained-in side, but by fine-tuning rather than by architecture.** Read strictly, the paper's method has two components with different retrofit status, and honesty requires separating them. The **policy** — batch-uniform exit points, monotone linear decay between a max and a min layer, 1 warmup layer + top-layer spending — is pure inference-time structure: it needs no new parameters, no heads, no loss, and could in principle be imposed by a runner on any decoder-only checkpoint tomorrow. The **evaluated results**, however, were all obtained on models **fine-tuned under that policy** (§3): the weights were adapted, per dataset and per target speedup, to a network whose middle layers are routinely skipped. The paper reports no zero-shot experiment — no table in which an off-the-shelf OPT checkpoint is skip-served without fine-tuning and holds its quality — and its comparison method that comes closest (the fixed-policy multi-layer exit network) is itself a *trained* head. **Judgment (ours, labelled as such):** SkipDecode falls on the **trained-in side of the line, one step closer to the line than LayerSkip**. LayerSkip's capability is a pretraining-scale property (layer dropout + early-exit loss woven through training); SkipDecode's is a fine-tuning-scale property (ordinary task fine-tuning with the skip schedule active, no architectural change). A runner serving a stock GGUF with a SkipDecode schedule would be running an experiment the paper never ran, at depths (down to ~5 of 24 layers) where the companion dossier's untrained-baseline numbers suggest the cost would be severe. As with LayerSkip: for existing checkpoints this is a **detect-and-honour property of suitably fine-tuned future checkpoints**, not a switch a runner can honestly flip on arbitrary weights — though the artefact a future checkpoint would need to declare is smaller (the schedule it was fine-tuned under: max/min layers, warmup count, decay shape), and a runner could honour such a declaration with a comparatively simple static scheduler.

**(b) What a runner can do today.** The scheduler itself is legitimately runner-side *machinery*: position-indexed, batch-uniform layer budgets with monotone decay are implementable in any batched engine, and are the correct shape for serving *if* a checkpoint arrives that was fine-tuned for them. What a runner cannot honestly do today is apply that machinery to an arbitrary existing model and advertise the paper's 2×-with-negligible-regression figure — that figure belongs to fine-tuned OPT models on three specific datasets. And the standing warning from the companion dossier applies unchanged: **confidence-based *stopping* is not early exit.** Terminating a *sequence* early (EOS, stop conditions) saves whole tokens; SkipDecode shortens the *per-token* forward pass. A runner with good stopping behaviour has implemented neither SkipDecode nor LayerSkip, and naming it otherwise conflates the two axes this wave exists to separate. One further serving caveat a runner would inherit, from the paper's own limitations: the position-locked schedule conflicts with continuous batching (new sequences can only join a batch at matching positions) — on a modern continuous-batching engine, honouring SkipDecode means constraining the batcher, a real throughput trade that must be counted against the layer savings, not discovered after the fact.

## 7. Cross-links

- **`papers/specinfer.md` and `papers/eagle-2.md` (Waves 2/13, external speculation).** SkipDecode is the corpus's clearest non-speculative speed method in the depth family: no draft, no verify, no distribution guarantee — included in this wave precisely to mark the boundary of the speculation family from outside. The contrast sharpens both sides: speculation (external or LayerSkip's self-) buys speed *with an exactness proof* and pays in verification compute and machinery; SkipDecode buys speed *with a budget proof* (cost fixed in advance) and pays in measured quality. A serving system chooses which proof it needs.
- **`papers/native-sparse-attention.md` (NSA).** NSA and the two Wave 24 papers form the corpus's **"architecture properties a runner must honour"** class — capabilities conferred by training, which a runner can recognise and serve but cannot retrofit. Within the class, SkipDecode is the lightest member: NSA is an architectural property of the checkpoint (its attention pattern is the model), LayerSkip is a training-recipe property (its prefixes are competent because of how it was trained), SkipDecode is a fine-tuning property (its middle layers are skippable because it was fine-tuned with them skipped). The runner-side work scales accordingly: honour NSA by implementing its attention, honour LayerSkip by implementing KVQ-resumed self-speculation, honour SkipDecode by implementing a declared static schedule — and in all three cases, *detect first*: none of the three may be presumed of a checkpoint that does not declare it.
- **`papers/dola.md` (Wave 23).** DoLa reads early layers; SkipDecode skips *to* the late ones. The kinship is the shared empirical foundation — hidden-state saturation: DoLa's motivating observation (for fact-demanding tokens the distribution keeps changing into the late layers; for easy tokens it settles early) and SkipDecode's (loss per position falls monotonically; past saturation, extra layers buy nothing) are the same phenomenon read from opposite ends. The difference is the direction of trust: DoLa never trusts an early layer's output — it *contrasts* it against the final layer's, on an ordinary untrained-for-exit model, at decode time, with no fine-tuning; SkipDecode trusts the *top* layers' output after skipping the middle, and pays fine-tuning for that trust. DoLa is what a runner can do with early layers on today's checkpoints; SkipDecode is what becomes possible when the checkpoint was prepared for it.
