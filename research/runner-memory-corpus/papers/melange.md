## 2. The problem as framed

LLM serving is cost-prohibitive, and the paper's opening move is to locate the cost where prior work mostly did not look. A large literature optimises the *inference engine* — kernels, batching, KV management — taking the GPU it runs on as given. Mélange asks the provisioning question instead: **given a service to run, which GPU types should it run on?** The motivating price datum: a single on-demand NVIDIA A100 rents for over $2,600 per month from a major cloud provider, and real services need many.

The opportunity is heterogeneity, and the paper's central empirical claim is that it is *unpriced* by uniform provisioning:

- The GPU market offers a wide spread of types differing in price, memory, bandwidth, and compute — and **cost and performance are not linearly related** across that spread. Cost efficiency, defined as tokens served per dollar (T/$), therefore varies by GPU type — and, crucially, the *ranking* of GPU types by T/$ is not fixed.
- Three service characteristics move the ranking, each demonstrated experimentally in the paper's §4 (serving Llama2-7B, chiefly on A100 vs A10G, then generalised to H100/L4 and Llama2-70B):
  1. **Request size** (input + output tokens). Small requests are served most cost-efficiently by cheaper, weaker GPUs; large requests need — and reward — high-end GPUs. Request latency itself varies enormously with size (the paper measures a 110× latency increase as input/output lengths grow from 25 to 2,000 tokens for Llama2-7B on an A100), so "the workload" is a *distribution* of sizes, never a size.
  2. **Request rate.** Provisioned capacity should track volume. An expensive GPU at low utilisation is T/$-poor however fast it is; at low rates the right answer is a cheaper GPU, and at higher rates a *mix* of types permits finer-grained capacity steps than any single type's instance size allows.
  3. **Latency SLO.** The paper uses average time-per-output-token (TPOT) as its SLO. Low-end GPUs are slower per token; under a tight SLO they can only sustain a low output rate (or cannot comply at all), which collapses their T/$. Loose SLOs let cheap GPUs compete; tight SLOs push the optimum toward high-end hardware.

It follows — and this is the framing the title names — that **the most cost-efficient allocation for a given service is typically a mixture**: high-end GPUs for the large requests and the tight deadlines, low-end GPUs for the small requests and the quiet hours. (The paper's worked intuition: A100s for large requests within SLO, A10Gs at higher T/$ for small ones, and the still cheaper L4 to hold availability at low activity.) Uniform allocation — pick the one "right" GPU and replicate it — wastes money in both directions at once: overpowered for most requests, and coarsely grained against the request rate. The problem, restated: *navigate (request-size distribution × request rate × SLO × GPU option space) to the minimal-cost allocation that still attains the SLO* — automatically, because no human intuition prices a four-dimensional trade-off reliably.

## 3. Core mechanism in our own words

Mélange is an **offline allocator**, and its mechanism is a chain: profile → express the workload as packable items → solve a bin-packing optimisation → provision what the solver names. It does not schedule individual requests at runtime, and it does not move running work.

**a) The profiling matrix.** Everything rests on measured, per-(GPU, request-size, SLO) capacity figures. For each GPU type G, each request size s, and the service's SLO, offline profiling with the actual serving engine (vLLM in the paper) establishes **MaxTput(G, s, SLO)** — the maximum requests per second G can sustain for requests of size s while staying under the latency deadline. Cost efficiency per cell follows from the GPU's hourly price. This matrix is the paper's real asset: the §4 analysis (which GPU wins T/$ where, and by how much) is simply the matrix read aloud, and the allocator is the matrix put to work. Note what a cell *is*: a measurement of one (model, engine, GPU, size, SLO) combination — not a spec-sheet derivation. The paper is explicit that results can differ across engine versions (its analysis uses vLLM-0.2.7 throughout), which is the matrix discipline's honesty clause: cells expire when their conditions change.

**b) Workload as histogram, histogram as slices.** A workload is characterised by its overall request rate and a two-dimensional histogram over (input length, output length): each bucket holds the aggregate request rate of requests in its size range. (In the evaluation: 10 input ranges × 6 output ranges = 60 buckets.) For load purposes each bucket's requests are conservatively treated as the bucket's largest size. Buckets are then subdivided into **slices** — a slice is a (request size, request rate) item; with the evaluation's slice factor of 8, a bucket at 4 requests/s becomes 8 slices of 0.5 requests/s, 480 slices in total. Slicing exists for one reason: a whole bucket may exceed one GPU, and may be *most cheaply split across different GPU types* — slices are the granularity at which a mixture becomes expressible.

**c) Load, defined against the SLO.** The load a slice i (rate r_i) imposes on GPU type j is **r_i / MaxTput(G_j, s_i, SLO)** — the fraction of that GPU's SLO-compliant capacity the slice consumes, with a GPU's total capacity normalised to 1. Loads from slices of different sizes add. This simple additive model is acknowledged in the paper as an approximation (alternative request cost models exist; theirs "can be easily replaced"), and its virtue is that it makes the SLO a *property of the capacity figures* rather than a separate runtime check: if the summed load on every provisioned GPU is ≤ its count, the allocation attains the SLO by construction — a claim the paper then tests live (§4).

**d) The allocation optimisation, as formulated.** A cost-aware bin-packing problem, solved as an integer linear program with an off-the-shelf solver. Bins are GPUs (each type j with hourly cost c_j); items are slices. Decision variables: A_ij ∈ {0,1} (slice i is assigned to GPU type j) and B_j ∈ ℤ≥0 (how many GPUs of type j to provision). Then:

- **Objective:** minimise Σ_j B_j · c_j — total hourly deployment cost.
- **Every slice placed exactly once:** for each i, Σ_j A_ij = 1.
- **Capacity respected per type:** for each j, Σ_i A_ij · L_ij · r_i ≤ B_j, where L_ij is the profiled per-(1 req/s) load of slice i's size on type j.

The solver's B vector *is* the deliverable: the minimal-cost fleet composition for that workload and SLO. New GPU types, other accelerators, or an alternative SLO definition (e.g. time-to-first-token instead of TPOT) enter by adding profiled columns/rows, not by changing the formulation.

**e) Reallocation cadence, as described — and its stated limit.** Mélange is run **before deployment, and re-run when the workload changes significantly**: the paper's solver section states that execution "is a one-time event; users are required to run the solver only prior to deployment or when there is a significant change in the distribution of request sizes or rates." It is a provisioning decision, not a control loop. The paper's conclusion is candid about the complement it does *not* provide: Mélange "does not address other challenges of deploying a live LLM service such as handling GPU unavailability or responding to dynamically changing request rate and request size distribution." Runtime load balancing across the provisioned mixture is likewise treated as a separate, unsolved-in-this-paper problem — the evaluation uses a simple Join-Shortest-Queue variant, assumes a balancer that knows output lengths for its main SLO measurements, and names heterogeneity-aware load balancing under unknown output length as future work (the cost of that assumption is measured; see §4).

## 4. Reported results — as reported, with hardware context

All figures are the paper's own, as reported. **Cloud-fleet results; §5–§6 separate the discipline from the magnitudes.**

**Setup (§6.1).** Four NVIDIA GPU types, priced at the lowest on-demand rate found across AWS/Azure/GCP (H100, not offered on-demand by the majors at the time, priced from RunPod and normalised against A100 pricing — the method is stated in the paper):

| GPU | Price ($/h) | Memory | Mem. bandwidth | FP16 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L4 | 0.70 | 24 GB | 300 GB/s | 242 TFLOPS |
| A10G (PCIe) | 1.01 | 24 GB | 600 GB/s | 125 TFLOPS |
| A100-80G (SXM) | 3.67 | 80 GB | 1,935 GB/s | 312 TFLOPS |
| H100 (SXM) | 7.516 (normalised) | 80 GB | 3,350 GB/s | 1,979 TFLOPS |

Model and engine: **Llama2-7B** (Llama-2-7b-hf) served by **vLLM 0.2.7** with default parameters for the allocation evaluation (the §4 analysis additionally examines Llama2-70B on H100×2 vs A100×2). Datasets: **Chatbot Arena** (lmsys-chat-1m; short-context/conversational), **PubMed** summarisation (long-context/document), and a synthetic **Mixed** set (80% Arena / 20% PubMed). Request rates swept 1–32 requests/s. SLOs: TPOT **120 ms (loose)** and **40 ms (tight)**. Baselines: the same ILP restricted to a single GPU type. One structural constraint surfaces in the results: a single large PubMed request (~12,000+ tokens) generates KV exceeding the 24 GB of an L4/A10G, so low-end-only baselines are excluded on PubMed/Mixed, and within Mélange's mixtures L4/A10G are restricted to requests under 12,000 tokens.

**Cost savings (§6.2)** — mixed allocation vs the best single-type strategies, across 1–32 requests/s:

- **Loose SLO (120 ms):** up to **77%** (Arena), **33%** (PubMed), **51%** (Mixed). Ranges: Arena 15–77%, PubMed 15–33%, Mixed 13–51%.
- **Tight SLO (40 ms):** up to **68%** (Arena), **22%** (PubMed), **51%** (Mixed). Ranges: Arena 9–68%, PubMed 2–22%, Mixed 4–51%.
- Headline ranges (abstract/introduction): **9–77%** short-context, **2–33%** long-context, **4–51%** mixed.
- Pattern, as reported: at low request rates the savings are largest (an H100 at 1–2 requests/s is "egregiously" costly — the load cannot saturate even one GPU); as rate rises, low-end GPUs' advantage narrows but persists on small request sizes. Under the tight SLO, low-end GPUs need many more instances to comply, so mixtures shift toward A100/H100 and savings shrink — the allocator adapts the mixture to the SLO rather than merely to the workload.

**SLO attainment (§6.3)** — live check of the solver's by-construction claim: provision actual cloud instances per Mélange's allocation at 4 requests/s, serve 2,000 sampled requests with Llama2-7B/vLLM: **>99.95% of requests met the 120 ms TPOT SLO; >99.5% met the 40 ms SLO.** Of the violations, 84% are attributed to request-rate bursts (Poisson arrivals) or co-location with large requests; the paper notes the standard remedy — over-provision by inflating the solver's request-rate input (e.g. +20%).

**Unknown output length (§6.4)** — the honesty experiment: with a load balancer that sees only input lengths and estimates output length from the running average, SLO attainment at 40 ms falls to **97.2%** (a 5.6× increase in violations vs the known-output-length balancer; 91% of the additional violations are large requests landing on low-end GPUs). Inflating the solver's rate input by just **5%** restores attainment to **>99.5%**. The allocation survives its balancer assumption being removed; the tail latency does not, for free.

**Solver time (§6.5):** under **1.2 seconds in every setting** (table range 0.137–1.14 s across datasets, SLOs, and rates 1–32), growing sub-linearly with request rate — negligible, as the paper puts it, against workload execution time, and consistent with the run-it-at-provisioning-time cadence of §3e.

## 5. Position in the corpus

Mélange and ServerlessLLM (sibling dossier, this wave) are the corpus's two model-zoo papers, and they divide the zoo problem cleanly: ServerlessLLM prices **time** (what a cold model costs to start, and where it should start), Mélange prices **money** (what a running service costs per hour, and on which hardware it should run). Both stand opposite the corpus's resident-session doctrine (IDEA-LEDGER **#1**) in the same instructive way: the doctrine optimises the experience of the one session that matters; these papers optimise fleets in which no single session matters and the aggregate bill is the product.

The honest boundary, stated for Mélange specifically: **its optimisation has almost nothing to operate on at personal scale.** An ILP that chooses how many L4s vs H100s to rent presupposes (i) a menu of rentable, substitutable hardware, (ii) a workload large enough that instance granularity is a real degree of freedom, (iii) a dollar meter running by the hour, and (iv) many concurrent requests whose size distribution is stable enough to profile. Volmarr's fleet satisfies none of these in the paper's terms: the hardware is owned, sunk-cost, and non-substitutable; concurrency is one user; the "bill" is electricity, heat, and battery, not an hourly invoice. Quoting the 77% figure as if it promised anything locally would be exactly the context-stripping the corpus's measurement law forbids.

What transfers — and §6b develops it — is not the optimiser but the **profiling matrix as a record-keeping discipline**. Mélange's one non-negotiable premise is that allocation decisions must rest on measured per-(model, GPU) performance under stated workload and SLO conditions, because intuition and spec sheets rank hardware wrongly (the paper's T/$ rankings invert across request sizes on the *same* hardware pair). That premise is the corpus's own measurement law wearing an allocator's clothes: IDEA-LEDGER **#44** (a number is real only with scenario, model, workload, and machine disclosed — no context-free figures) and **#33** (measure per (model, workload)) state it as a *reporting* rule; Mélange extends the key from (model, workload) to **(model, workload, device)** and states it as a *decision* rule. On a heterogeneous personal fleet — where "which machine should run this model" is a real, recurring question currently answered from memory and feel — the matrix is the difference between an allocation practice and an allocation folklore. The solver is optional; the measurements are not.

A second, quieter position point: Mélange's allocator is deliberately *offline and static*, re-run on significant workload change, and the paper says so without apology. Set beside Llumnix (runtime migration, placements continuously revised) and ServerlessLLM (startup-time scheduling at request time), the corpus now holds all three cadences — provision-time, start-time, and run-time placement — each with its price stated by its own authors. For a personal fleet, provision-time is the only cadence that exists: models are placed when they are installed and configured, not when a request arrives. Mélange is thus, paradoxically, the fleet paper whose *temporal* shape fits the local problem best, even though its *economic* shape fits worst.

## 6. Applicability

### (a) The load-path half of the wave

Mélange has no load path — it assumes a fixed engine and prices placement above it — so its contribution to Ollama model switching is indirect but real: **it tells the switcher what a switch decision would need to know.** A runner (or a user) choosing between running a model on the laptop GPU, the APU, or CPU-offloaded is making a one-instance, one-request version of Mélange's allocation, and the paper's finding applies in miniature: the right answer varies with request size and latency expectation, not just with the model. The practical consequence belongs to §6b's record-keeping: unless load and generation performance are recorded per (model, device), every switch is also an unmeasured allocation. ServerlessLLM's dossier (§6a of `papers/serverlessllm.md`) carries this wave's direct load-path analysis; nothing in Mélange contradicts it, and Mélange's engine-version caveat (its matrix was profiled on vLLM 0.2.7; "results can differ across versions") applies to load-path measurements with equal force.

### (b) Mélange at fleet scale-of-three-machines *(labelled judgement)*

*The following is this dossier's judgement, applying the paper's discipline to Volmarr's fleet. It is not a claim the paper makes, and no figure from §4 is carried over.*

Volmarr's zoo already lives across three profoundly heterogeneous "GPU types," in Mélange's sense:

- **purplelongship** — the older laptop, RTX 2060 Max-Q class, **6 GB VRAM**, display-attached in normal use: the fleet's L4 — cheap to run, capacity-constrained, the right home for small models and short work, and (per the corpus's reserve decomposition, IDEA-LEDGER #3) unable to hold an 8B-class model with a useful context at any cache precision in its desk configuration.
- **purpleraider** — the ASUS ROG Flow Z13, **Strix Halo APU** with unified memory: the fleet's A100-analogue in capacity terms (a large single pool, no discrete VRAM wall) but with an entirely different cost profile — package power and shared bandwidth instead of a PCIe-fed card — and, per standing orders, currently paused for heavy work; an allocator that ignores *operational state* would misplace work here today, which is itself a Mélange-flavoured lesson: the matrix needs a column for "available at all."
- **Mimir** — the Raspberry Pi: the fleet's extreme low end, CPU/ARM-only, where the question is never throughput but *feasibility and patience* — embeddings, small models, always-on light duties. Mélange's framework accommodates it honestly: some (model, device) cells are not slow, they are *empty* — as with L4/A10G and 12,000-token requests in §4, capacity is a hard constraint that no T/$ figure softens.

Allocation across these three is manual today: Volmarr and Yrsa decide, per model and per project, what lives where, from experience. The paper's transferable offer is to make that practice *legible* without mechanising it:

1. **Keep the matrix, skip the solver.** A small, durable table — per (model + quant, device): load time (cold/warm, per the ServerlessLLM dossier's §6a), tok/s at a stated chat workload (prompt/output profile named), maximum usable context under the device's real reserve arithmetic, and a feasibility flag — is Mélange's profiling step preserved as record-keeping. With three devices and a zoo in the dozens, the table is a page, not a pipeline; it is filled incrementally, one measured cell at a time, exactly as #44's disclosure law would require of any number quoted from it.
2. **Let the table, not recollection, answer placement questions.** "Where should the 27B live?", "can the Pi hold the embeddings model *and* answer in reasonable time?", "is the summariser worth evicting the chat model for on 6 GB?" — each is a row lookup plus the §2 lesson that the answer depends on request size and latency expectation, so the table's workload column is not decoration. Where a cell is empty, the honest entry is *unmeasured* — the fleet-scale version of the paper refusing to allocate from spec sheets.
3. **Re-profile on change, which is the paper's cadence translated.** Mélange re-runs on significant workload change; the local triggers are a driver/backend update, a quant change, a new model family, or a machine's role change. Cells carry their date and engine version — the paper's vLLM-0.2.7 caveat, kept as a column.
4. **Do not import the objective.** The ILP minimises dollars per hour. The local objective is a compound the paper never prices: user-visible latency for one person, memory headroom for the resident session (IDEA-LEDGER #1 outranks allocation efficiency on this fleet, always), heat and battery, and the standing operational constraints on each machine. A local "solver" would be a person reading the matrix with those weights in mind — which is the current practice, upgraded from memory to record. That is the whole proposal, and it is deliberately small: **Mélange's discipline, at n = 3, is a notebook, not a system.**
