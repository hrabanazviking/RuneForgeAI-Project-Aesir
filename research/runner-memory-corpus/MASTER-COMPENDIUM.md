**The three theses of this book, stated up front.**

1. **Inference is memory movement.** At the batch size a personal runner lives at, generating a token means streaming essentially all of the model's bytes past the arithmetic units once. Compute is abundant; bandwidth and capacity are scarce. Almost every technique in this book — quantization, paging, prefix reuse, speculation, IO-aware kernels — is, underneath its own vocabulary, a way of moving fewer bytes, moving them once instead of repeatedly, or refusing to move bytes that carry no information. *(Chapter 1; dossiers: `memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md`, `papers/splitwise.md`.)*
2. **The KV cache is the product.** Weights are shared, static, and known at load time; the cache is per-conversation, growing, and where sessions, privacy, persistence, and failure all live. On a small card the cache arithmetic — bytes per token, precision, what is pinned and what is evicted — decides what the user can actually do, more than any kernel does. *(Chapter 4; dossier: `memory/kv-cache-design.md`.)*
3. **A personal runner is not a small datacentre.** The datacentre literature optimises aggregate throughput across many strangers under latency service-level objectives. A personal runner optimises one person's continuity: a persona that cannot be silently truncated away, a history that is never reprocessed, a memory that never quietly lies. The corpus's highest-ranked ideas are therefore residency, anchoring, and disclosure — correctness properties — ahead of every speed technique. Speed matters enormously; it is ranked after the properties that make speed worth having. *(Chapters 8–9; dossiers: `runners/ollama.md`, `aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`, `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`.)*

## Chapter 1 — The machine truth: inference is memory movement

### 1.1 The wall was predicted, then measured

The founding observation of memory-centric design is thirty years old and has only become more true. Wulf and McKee argued in 1995 that processor performance and DRAM performance were improving as *diverging exponentials* — they assumed roughly 80% per year for processors against 7% per year for DRAM — and that the difference between diverging exponentials itself grows exponentially, so a gap that begins as an annoyance ends as the whole story. Under deliberately generous assumptions (a perfect cache, only compulsory misses), their arithmetic put the average cost of a memory access at 1.52 cycles in 2000, 8.25 cycles in 2005, and 98.8 cycles in 2010, and defined the wall as the point where average access cost exceeds five instruction times: beyond it, making the processor faster does not change wall-clock time at all *(dossier: `memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md`, §1.1, from Wulf & McKee 1995)*.

McKee's 2004 retrospective confirmed the wall arrives *per workload*, not all at once, and grounded the trend in McCalpin's STREAM measurements: peak floating-point rates had been growing at roughly 50% per year while *sustained* bandwidth grew at roughly 35% per year *(dossier: `memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md`, §1.2)*. McCalpin's contribution is the measurement culture this book inherits: judge machines by sustained bandwidth, not vendor peaks, and never conflate **latency** (how long one access takes; the binding resource for dependent, unpredictable access) with **bandwidth** (how many bytes per second a streaming pattern can sustain). They need different fixes, and confusing them wastes engineering *(dossier: `memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md`, §1.3)*.

### 1.2 The roofline: the working method

Williams, Waterman and Patterson's roofline model (2009) turns the wall into a per-kernel method. A kernel's **operational intensity** is its operations per byte of DRAM traffic — bytes counted *after* the cache hierarchy has filtered what it can, so locality improvements raise intensity. Attainable performance is the minimum of two ceilings:

> Attainable performance = min( peak compute, peak bandwidth × operational intensity )

Plotted on log–log axes the two ceilings form a roof. The **ridge point** is the intensity where they meet. Left of it a kernel is memory-bound: the only ways to go faster are to use bandwidth better or to move fewer bytes. Right of it a kernel is compute-bound: more bandwidth buys nothing. The model is an upper bound, not a prediction; the distance a real kernel sits below its roof is diagnostic information *(dossier: `memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md`, §2)*.

The modern scale of the problem is worth fixing in mind. An NVIDIA H100 SXM offers roughly 989 TFLOP/s in BF16 against 3.35 TB/s of memory bandwidth (vendor peaks, as reported in the dossier's references), giving a ridge point near **295 FLOP/byte**. A kernel must do nearly three hundred floating-point operations for every byte it fetches before the H100's compute is even the constraint *(dossier: `memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md`, §3.2)*.

### 1.3 The two phases: GEMM and GEMV

Applied to Transformer inference — the application is Yuan et al.'s 2024 roofline survey, as reported in the dossier — the same layer has opposite character in the two phases of a request. Measured on LLaMA-2-7B on an NVIDIA A6000, the query projection runs at roughly **1,024 operations/byte in prefill** against roughly **1 operation/byte in decode**; the feed-forward gate projection measures roughly 1,215 against roughly 1. The survey's conclusion, quoted in the dossier, is that in the decode stage all computations are memory-bound *(dossier: `memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md`, §3.1; per-layer figures as reported from Yuan et al.'s Table 1 via secondary coverage — the dossier flags this provenance honestly, and this volume carries the flag with the numbers)*.

The reason is shape, not implementation. **Prefill** processes the whole prompt in parallel: each weight matrix is read once and used for all *P* prompt positions — a matrix–matrix product (GEMM) whose intensity scales with *P*, comfortably right of any modern ridge. Prefill is **compute-bound**, and its user-visible metric is time-to-first-token (TTFT). **Decode** generates one token per step: each weight matrix is multiplied by a single vector (GEMV), read, used for roughly one multiply–add, and discarded — roughly 2 FLOPs per parameter against 2 bytes per FP16 parameter, i.e. about **1 FLOP/byte at batch 1**, two-plus orders of magnitude left of the H100 ridge. Decode is **bandwidth-bound**, and its ceiling has a closed form worth memorising:

> tokens/sec per sequence ≲ sustained bandwidth ÷ bytes that must be read per token,
> where bytes-per-token ≈ active weight bytes + KV-cache bytes read that step.

The dossier's worked example: a 70B model in FP16 (≈140 GB of weights) on a 3.35 TB/s H100 tops out near **~20 tokens/sec per sequence** before any optimisation — not for lack of FLOPs, but because 140 GB must stream through the chip for every token *(dossier: `memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md`, §3.2)*.

Two corollaries govern everything that follows.

**First, the KV cache is part of the per-token traffic, and it grows.** Every decode step reads the keys and values of all previous tokens. KV bytes per token are fixed by architecture (Chapter 4 gives the arithmetic), so the KV term is small at short context and increasingly material at long context — taxing, as the dossier puts it, the phase that is already starved for bandwidth. Long-context decode is *more* bandwidth-bound, not less *(dossier: `memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md`, §3.2)*.

**Second, batching is powerful precisely because it moves decode's intensity.** With *B* sequences decoded together, weights are read once per step and used for *B* tokens, so intensity scales with *B* and aggregate throughput climbs near-linearly while decode stays on the bandwidth slope — until the ridge is reached (batch on the order of 150–300 on H100-class parts, per the reports the dossier read), KV capacity runs out, or latency objectives forbid larger batches. What batching does **not** change is per-sequence latency at batch 1, or the bytes-per-token floor for a single stream. A single-user workload lives at *B* ≈ 1 by definition — which is why the datacentre's central lever is unavailable to the personal runner, and why this book's later chapters are dominated by the levers that remain: fewer bytes (quantization), bytes moved once (residency and prefix reuse), and bytes not moved at all (speculation's verification trick, IO-aware kernels) *(dossier: `memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md`, §3.3)*.

A final distinction from McCalpin survives intact: at very small scale a decode step can be dominated by fixed costs — kernel launches, synchronisation, host-side scheduling — rather than by streaming bytes. Such a step sits *below* the bandwidth slope and will not speed up proportionally with quantization. The roofline tells you this too, but only if you plot the measured point instead of assuming it lies on the slope **[synthesis, following `memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md`, §3.4, which labels the same move as its own synthesis]**.

### 1.4 The bandwidth ledger: how this book keeps accounts

The roofline dossier distils a benchmarking law that the whole corpus obeys, and that Chapter 9 installs as a build requirement (ledger idea #33). Every speed claim must carry: the machine's roofs *measured on the machine* (sustained bandwidth per memory tier, not vendor peaks); the workload's bytes (model file size *and* active bytes — they differ under spill — plus KV bytes/token × context length, batch, and prompt length); the phases reported separately (prefill and decode are different regimes with different roofs); and distributions under recorded machine state, not single runs. A tokens/sec figure **does** prove that a specific binary, model build, context, batch, and machine state produced that rate; converted to implied bandwidth and compared with the measured roof, it can prove a run is *at* the wall or *below it by a stated gap*. It does **not** prove an engine is fast in general, that a change caused an improvement (without an A/B under protocol), or anything at all about correctness. The one-line discipline: *never report a speed without its bytes, its phase, its machine state, and its distance from a measured roof* *(dossier: `memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md`, §4.3)*.

Two fleet facts illustrate why the law matters. Our own MimirAlloc campaigns measured the AMD Strix Halo host (purpleraider) directly on the CPU side: a single-CCD bandwidth plateau of 61.8 GB/s at four threads, 38.2 GB/s on one core, a peak of 112.76 GB/s, and cross-CCD latency of 103 ns against ~32 ns intra-CCD — numbers no vendor sheet would have given, and the only honest basis for a roofline on that machine *(dossier: `memory/memory-wall-and-roofline.md`, §4.2, citing the MimirAlloc Z13 campaign records)*. Conversely, the corpus contains **no** sustained-bandwidth measurement for purplelongship's RTX 2060 Max-Q — the 6 GB card at the centre of the build campaign — and therefore asserts no tokens/sec figure for that card anywhere. That absence is Open Question 2 in the ledger, and it is flagged again in Chapter 10 *(dossiers: `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, "What could not be verified"; `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, §6)*. **[Corpus gap — flagged, not filled.]**

### 1.5 Phase characterisation as an industry premise

The two-phase character of every request — prefill compute-bound, decode memory-bound — was characterised most influentially by Splitwise, whose measurements became the field's shared opening paragraph. Its sharpest single datum: an H100 offers **3.43×** an A100's compute yet buys only about **0.70×** its time-between-tokens on decode — the newest GPU's FLOPs are largely wasted on the memory-bound phase. Splitwise's phase-split clusters (separate prompt and token machine pools) reached up to **1.4× higher throughput at 20% lower cost** than homogeneous baselines on production traces, or 2.35× more throughput under the same cost and power budgets; its KV-transfer mechanism cost under 7% of prompt computation and 0.8% end-to-end when overlapped. The paper is equally honest about its boundary: under saturated batch-job load with no latency objectives worth the name, phase splitting devolves to the baselines — it is a latency-regime technique *(dossier: `papers/splitwise.md`, §§4–5; cluster results from a validated simulator replaying production traces on BLOOM-176B and Llama2-70B, transfer measured on real DGX-A100/H100 hardware)*. DistServe and Sarathi-Serve (Chapter 3) are best read as two different engineering answers to Splitwise's characterisation: separate the phases onto different machines, or bound their interference inside one machine.

### 1.6 Below the runner: the layers that price the roof (v2)

V1 drew the roofline and stopped at the runner's API. Four B-side dossiers banked since then systematise what sits underneath, and three of their results change how this book's numbers must be read.

**The reserve is decomposed, and it has two cases.** A card advertised as 6 GB never offers 6 GiB to a runner: CUDA context (~300 MiB, order-of-magnitude from the public decomposition the dossier read), display/compositor residency when the card drives a screen (200–400 MiB), backend scratch (capped ~256 MiB in the planning decomposition), and fragmentation headroom (~10% of the KV arena) take their cuts first. The dossier's planning range is **~512 MiB headless** against **0.9–1.1 GiB display-attached** — and v1's single 512 MiB planning figure was, in those terms, exactly the optimistic headless end *(dossier: `memory/gpu-memory-internals.md`, §§2.3, 7.3)*. Every 6 GB worked figure in this volume is therefore labelled with its case from this draft onward; the fleet measurement that retires the range is still owed (Chapter 10, Q17). The same dossier supplies the accounting discipline Chapter 9 installs with idea #6: total / reserved / allocated / largest single free block, because an out-of-memory error can be true of the card, the allocator, and the request in three different worlds at once. It also names the one documented escape from segment-boundary fragmentation — CUDA's virtual-memory API (reserve address space once, map physical pages on demand), the mechanism behind PyTorch's expandable segments and the preferred shape for a growable KV arena where the backend offers it *(dossier: `memory/gpu-memory-internals.md`, §§3, 5.4, 7.1)*.

**A model load is a paging event before it is a compute event.** Weights arrive page by page, by fault, in the order the loader touches them; cold and warm time-to-first-token are different metrics and must never be averaged. The runner's levers are classical and specific: weight arenas requested 2 MiB-aligned and sized in 2 MiB multiples, `MADV_HUGEPAGE` under a system THP mode of `madvise` (the runner opts its known-dense ranges in; it does not set `always` for the machine), the result *verified* per arena via `AnonHugePages` — THP is a request the kernel may decline — and deliberate prefaulting (`MADV_WILLNEED`, or `MAP_POPULATE` at map time) issued ahead of first compute. One warning is stated plainly in the dossier because it is the most common `madvise` error in inference code: weights are **not** `MADV_SEQUENTIAL` — they are read once *per token, forever*, and sequential advice invites the kernel to drop pages the next token needs. Our own MimirAlloc Z-049 measurements price the huge-page half: 2 MiB **ADOPT** (bimodal on PMD alignment — an unaligned request silently gets base pages), 20 MiB **ADOPT**, 200 MiB **ADOPT on fault behaviour with bandwidth TIE** — the expected signature is faults and translation, not warm bandwidth *(dossiers: `memory/virtual-memory-and-caches.md`, §§2–5, 7.1; `memory/mimiralloc-our-own-proven-work.md`, §4.3; ledger #41)*.

**Geography is real, and honestly scoped.** Under Linux's first-touch policy, the loader thread that faults a weight page chooses its home node as a side effect — a single-threaded load can sentence every later decode thread to remote reads for the life of the process. The fleet's measured gradient is intra-package: ~32 ns intra-CCD against ~103 ns cross-CCD on Strix Halo. The dossier deliberately covers only this seam — parallel first-touch as a placement API, interleave as the honest default for shared read-only weights, measure topology at startup — and flags the full NUMA treatment (topology-aware KV placement, per-CCD arenas) as a separate, still-owed commission, with MimirAlloc's ARENA_PER_CCD decision as the corpus's current frontier marker. This book claims the seam, not the continent **[flagged scope, carried from the dossier]** *(dossier: `memory/virtual-memory-and-caches.md`, §6)*.

**Every bandwidth figure is a datasheet figure.** The memory-technologies dossier prices the branches: DDR/LPDDR for host and unified machines (Strix Halo's 256-bit LPDDR5X-8000 = 256 GB/s theoretical), GDDR on discrete cards (the RTX 2060's branch), HBM in the datacentre (width instead of speed — the branch that will not reach this fleet's desks), SRAM as the on-chip tier above the datasheet, and CXL as DRAM at the end of a fabric — expansion today, pooling and sharing as its later generations, at a measured latency and link-bandwidth price. Its portable discipline is three variables, never one: bandwidth, latency, capacity economics — a generation change moves them independently *(dossier: `memory/memory-technologies-dram-cxl.md`, §§2–8)*. Chapter 10 carries CXL as a frontier, not a feature.

## Chapter 2 — The anatomy of a runner

### 2.1 The parts every runner has

Strip away language, vendor, and scale, and every system in the corpus is assembled from the same six parts. Naming them once, precisely, lets the rest of the book compare like with like.

1. **Loader and formats.** Reads a checkpoint — GGUF, safetensors, an engine artefact — verifies and maps it, decides weight residency (which bytes live where, in what precision), and answers the fit question: does this model, at this context, on this machine, fit, and what spills if it does not? The loader owns the quantization *provisioning* decision (Chapter 5): whether precision was chosen when the file was authored, when it was converted, or while it loads.
2. **Scheduler.** Decides, step by step, which sequences advance, how much work each step contains, and what happens when memory runs out mid-flight. The scheduler owns the step loop (Chapter 3) and, with it, the latency character of the whole system.
3. **KV manager.** Allocates, shares, retains, evicts, and accounts for the key/value cache (Chapter 4). In modern systems this is also the prefix store: the same blocks, indexed by content, serve both the live cache and the reuse cache.
4. **Kernel layer.** The attention, GEMM/GEMV, dequantization, and sampling kernels that actually move the bytes (Chapter 7). The kernel layer is where format and hardware meet: a quantization format is only as good as the kernel that consumes it without unpacking it first.
5. **Sampler.** Turns logits into tokens — temperature, top-*k*/top-*p*, penalties, grammar constraints, and, in speculative systems, the verification rule that decides which drafted tokens stand (Chapter 6). The sampler is a correctness surface, not a detail: two of the corpus's speculation dossiers turn on whether the sampling rule preserves the target distribution exactly.
6. **Server surface.** The API, session model, and disclosure behaviour the user actually meets: whether requests are stateless or sessions are first-class, whether truncation and eviction are reported or silent, whether the surface is safe beyond loopback. Chapter 8 argues this part is where personal runners are won and lost.

**[Synthesis]** The six parts are not equally deep in every runner, and the *distribution of depth* is the fastest way to characterise a system. llama.cpp is kernel- and format-deep with a deliberately thin server. vLLM is scheduler- and KV-deep. TensorRT-LLM was, for most of its life, loader-deep to an extreme (the model was compiled into an engine artefact). Ollama is server- and packaging-deep over borrowed kernels. ExLlamaV2 is kernel- and format-deep for exactly one user. Reading any new runner, ask first where its depth sits; its weaknesses will sit in the shallow parts.

### 2.2 The design axes

The corpus's runners differ along three axes that matter more than any feature checklist.

**Axis 1 — Compile-first vs load-anything.** At one pole, a runner decides everything in advance: convert the checkpoint, build a specialised artefact for one model, one precision, one GPU architecture, one envelope of shapes, then serve the artefact. At the other pole, a runner loads an arbitrary community checkpoint at run time and pays for generality in per-step overhead. TensorRT-LLM's classic path is the compile-first extreme; llama.cpp/GGUF and mistral.rs's in-situ quantization are load-anything designs (Chapter 5). The axis matters because of what happened at the extreme: NVIDIA itself retired it (§2.4).

**Axis 2 — Datacentre vs personal.** Datacentre engines (vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM, LMDeploy) optimise aggregate throughput across many concurrent strangers, under per-request latency objectives, on large NVIDIA (and secondarily AMD/TPU) hardware. Personal runners (Ollama, llama.cpp, ExLlamaV2, MLX-LM, mistral.rs in its embedded form, PowerInfer) optimise one user's latency, residency, and continuity on one modest machine. The axis is not about size but about *objective function*: almost every datacentre win in this book is bought with an assumption — concurrency, VRAM headroom, a homogeneous fleet — that the personal setting violates (ledger §5, R11).

**Axis 3 — Single-engine vs ecosystem.** Some projects are one engine with one format (ExLlamaV2/EXL2). Some are substrates that other products embed or launch (ggml/llama.cpp under Ollama, LM Studio, Jan, KoboldCpp; FlashInfer as a kernel library inside SGLang, vLLM, and MLC-Engine; Candle under mistral.rs). Some are platforms that route among engines (LocalAI; LMDeploy's two-engine package). **(v2)** The sweep completed the ecosystem taxonomy with three more positions a builder must recognise: the **orchestrator/router** — Lemonade, which is not an engine at all but a vendor daemon that detects silicon, downloads and launches specialist engines as subprocesses (llama.cpp for GGUF, FastFlowLM for the NPU, ONNX Runtime GenAI for ONNX), and presents them behind one port in three API dialects *(dossier: `runners/lemonade.md`)*; the **container/distribution form** — RamaLama (models as OCI container artefacts; studied here as a packaging philosophy — this fleet's no-Docker law means it is a study source, never a deployment route) and llamafile (model + runtime as one portable executable; distribution *as* the feature) *(dossiers: `runners/ramalama.md`; `runners/llamafile.md`)*; and the **server constellation** — the ecosystem that formed around `llama-server` itself (llama-swap, LocalAI, chat front-ends, packagers), proof that a stable, permissively licensed single-binary server surface lets other people do the product work the core refuses *(dossier: `runners/llamacpp-server-ecosystem.md`)*. Ecosystem position decides maintenance burden: a substrate inherits every consumer's bug reports; a launcher inherits its upstream's release cadence; a router inherits *every* engine's release cadence plus the routing bugs that are uniquely its own.

### 2.4 Two cautionary histories the anatomy must record

**Ollama's architecture inversion — the dated truth.** Any single-sentence description of Ollama's engine is wrong for at least one era, and the dossier insists on the dates *(dossier: `runners/ollama.md`, §2.3)*. Era 1 (July 2023 – late 2024): a wrapper around llama.cpp, with the integration mechanism flipping repeatedly (cgo bindings, subprocess, in-process library, subprocess again). Era 2 (late 2024 – May 2026): Ollama's own engine, written in Go directly over GGML, beginning with Llama 3.2 Vision (v0.4.0, November 2024) and announced as strategy in May 2025 — the stated reason being that llama.cpp's glue of a separate text decoder plus vision encoder was becoming the correctness bottleneck as multimodal releases accelerated, and that faithful per-model attention (Gemma 3's sliding window, Llama 4's chunked attention and 2-D rotary embeddings) mattered because an approximately-right attention implementation degrades output progressively over long contexts. Era 3 (June 2026 – present as of this volume): on 2026-05-29 a change removing the vendored GGML backend, the CGO runner, and the Go model implementations was merged; it shipped in v0.30.0 (2026-06-01) with Vulkan enabled by default, so GGUF models are again served exclusively by a pinned upstream `llama-server` subprocess with a small Ollama-side compatibility layer. Separately, MLX arrived as a preview on Apple Silicon in v0.19.0 (March 2026) and became the default there for supported architectures in the v0.40.0 line (September 2026). The lesson is not that either choice was wrong; it is that *engine ownership is a recurring cost decision, not an identity*, and that a fork must price the maintenance surface it inherits (ledger R12 reaches the same verdict from the other direction).

**NVIDIA's retreat from compile-first.** TensorRT-LLM was commissioned into this corpus as the compile-first extreme: convert a checkpoint, build a serialised engine for one model, precision, architecture, and shape envelope, serve it with a C++ executor doing in-flight batching over paged KV. The research found that framing historically true and currently incomplete *(dossier: `runners/tensorrt-llm.md`)*. A PyTorch backend appeared experimentally in 0.17; in the 1.0 line it became the stable default for both the `LLM` API and the `trtllm-serve` server; NVIDIA's current migration guide states without hedging that the TensorRT engine backend **has been removed**, PyTorch is the (sole) backend, and the `tensorrt` pip dependency has been dropped. The dossier's "what not to steal" draws the moral this volume adopts as law: tuning output may exist as a *cache* that can be deleted without consequence, never as a required artefact; a request larger than the tuned profile must run slower, never fail for being unplanned — rigidity was the engine model's fatal product flaw, and NVIDIA's own retreat is the strongest evidence available. The same dossier refuses, for the personal target, datacentre defaults (0.9-of-VRAM KV fractions, benchmark-first defaults), precision maximalism (FP8/FP4 recipes presume tensor cores Turing lacks), and disaggregated serving on one card.

**The compile-anywhere pole survives — as a compiler, not an artefact (v2).** MLC-LLM is the necessary counterweight to a too-simple reading of NVIDIA's retreat *(dossier: `runners/mlc-llm.md`)*. Compilation itself was not refuted; what was refuted was the *per-configuration serialised engine artefact* — keyed to one model, precision, GPU and shape envelope, refusing what it did not plan. MLC compiles through a portable intermediate representation (TVM Relax) to many targets from one model definition, with just-in-time compilation on first run as the default posture: the compiler stays in the dependency graph, but no artefact store gates what may run. For this fleet's cross-platform law the lesson is precise: portability can be the *compiler's* job (MLC), the *file format's* job (GGUF), or the *runtime's* job (ONNX Runtime GenAI's execution providers) — three different bills, paid by three different parties. The personal runner's load-anything pole remains GGUF's, but a master should be able to say what the other two bills buy. Jan's Cortex episode (ledger R12, v2 extension) is the same lesson from the platform side: an abstraction layer between a product and its engine was built, kept roughly a year, then removed in favour of direct integration — abstractions are earned by a second shipped engine, never anticipated.

## Chapter 3 — Scheduling: who advances, and by how much

### 3.1 From request-level to iteration-level (Orca)

Before Orca, serving systems batched at request granularity: a batch was formed, every sequence in it ran to completion, and only then did new requests enter — so a batch containing one long generation held its finished neighbours' capacity hostage for its whole length. Orca (OSDI 2022) replaced the unit of scheduling: **iteration-level scheduling** re-decides the batch after every single forward pass, admitting new sequences and retiring finished ones at token granularity. Its companion technique, **selective batching**, batches the position-wise operations that can be shared while keeping per-sequence attention separate. The headline — **36.9× throughput at the same latency** against NVIDIA FasterTransformer on GPT-3 175B — measures how badly request-level batching wastes capacity under variable-length load; it is a scheduling result, not a kernel speedup, and the dossier forbids citing it in any single-user context *(dossier: `papers/orca.md`, §§3, 5, 7)*.

What transfers to a personal runner is not the throughput but the **step-loop shape**: run one token, then re-decide. That loop is the natural place to stream each token immediately, to check stop conditions and user interrupt between tokens rather than at request end, and to interleave a lightweight second job (a background summarisation, a second character's turn) without waiting for the first to finish. The concurrency-cap lesson also transfers in miniature: bound live sequences by KV bytes, not by request count *(dossier: `papers/orca.md`, §7a)*. For hybrid models the cap becomes a mixed budget — paged KV bytes for attention layers plus fixed state bytes per live session (Chapter 4, §4.6) *(dossier: `papers/orca.md`, §7b)*.

Orca left memory unsolved: contiguous KV allocation still fragmented and capped concurrency. PagedAttention, a year later, supplied the memory granularity to match Orca's scheduling granularity (Chapter 4). Neither alone is a complete serving design — a sentence the corpus repeats in both dossiers, and the correct mental model for the whole fleet literature that follows.

### 3.2 Continuous batching and the token budget

Iteration-level scheduling, productised as **continuous batching**, is now the default posture of every serious engine in the corpus: vLLM's V1 scheduler, SGLang's runtime, LMDeploy's independently-named **persistent batch**, and mistral.rs's default all re-form the batch per step. The mature form of the idea replaces separate prefill and decode phases with a single per-step **token budget**: each step may process at most *B* tokens in total, filled decode-first (decodes are latency-critical and cheap per token), with remaining budget spent on prefill chunks. vLLM's V1 engine — the only engine since v0.11 (October 2025) — is exactly this: one unified scheduler, no prefill/decode phases, chunked prefill on by default, prefix caching on by default (the V1 team measured under 1% throughput cost even at a 0% hit rate — a project figure from a secondary source, flagged as such in the dossier) *(dossier: `runners/vllm.md`, §§2–3)*. LMDeploy reaches the same execution model from a C++-first culture in TurboMind, with the operational warning that its two-engine package can silently fall back from TurboMind to its PyTorch engine — a planned deployment quietly becoming a different one — unless operators assert the backend from the startup log *(dossier: `runners/lmdeploy.md`, §§2–3)*. **[Synthesis]** Silent fallback is a disclosure failure of the same species as silent truncation (Chapter 8); the corpus treats both as defects, whatever the performance involved.

### 3.3 The stall, and chunked prefill (Sarathi-Serve)

Mixing a compute-bound prefill into a decode batch stretches every decode step in that batch: one long prompt's prefill becomes every other sequence's pause. Sarathi-Serve (OSDI 2024) named this the **stall** and fixed it with two mechanisms that only work together: **chunked prefill** (split a long prefill into budget-sized chunks spread over several steps) and **decode-maximal batching** (fill each step with all waiting decodes first, then top up with one prefill chunk) under a fixed token budget. Every step's latency is then bounded by the budget, not by the longest prompt *(dossier: `papers/sarathi-serve.md`, §§2–3)*.

The numbers, with their context: on Mistral-7B on a single A100-80GB, up to **2.6×** the serving capacity of vLLM under a P99 time-between-tokens objective; on Yi-34B on 2× A100, up to 3.7× vs vLLM under the strict objective; larger gains still on pipelined 70B/180B configurations where pipeline bubbles compound the stall. The ablation is the cleanest evidence: hybrid batching alone keeps time-to-first-token low but lets stalls through (worst time-between-tokens); chunking alone bounds the stall but pays in TTFT; only the combination is good on both axes. The honest price: chunking costs at most ~25% extra prefill time at a 512-token budget, falling to negligible at 2,048 *(dossier: `papers/sarathi-serve.md`, §4)*. Nothing in the paper was measured on consumer hardware, at batch 1, or on a single-user workload — the dossier says so in bold, and this volume repeats it because the next paragraph depends on the translation, not the headline.

**The personal-runner reduction of Sarathi.** With one user there is usually no second sequence to stall — but there is a stall of a different shape: a single long ingest (a pasted document, a lore dump, a restored history) freezing the application for its whole prefill, with no progress, no cancellation, and no interleaved decode. The corpus's own baseline makes the pathology concrete: Ollama-via-Voxta re-evaluating **14,712 tokens in 52.1 seconds** per turn *(dossier: `aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`, §2.2, recorded MAP baseline)*. Chunked prefill does not shorten that wait; it **bounds** it — turning one 52-second silence into a sequence of budget-sized steps with progress reporting and a working cancel button. The ledger ranks this as robustness, explicitly with zero raw-speed claim (idea #18) *(dossiers: `papers/sarathi-serve.md`, §6; `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, #18)*.

### 3.4 Disaggregation and its single-GPU limit (DistServe, Splitwise)

If prefill and decode interfere even when chunked, why not give them different machines? DistServe (OSDI 2024) disaggregates the phases onto separate GPU pools, searches each pool's parallelism independently, and hands KV between them with bandwidth-aware placement. Its gains are **goodput** gains — request rate sustainable at ≥90% SLO attainment — and must be quoted with workload and baseline attached: up to **7.4×** more requests is the top of the vs-DeepSpeed-MII chatbot range; up to **12.6×** tighter SLOs is summarisation vs vLLM; chatbot vs vLLM is 2.0–4.6×. Its strongest evidence is a null result: vLLM given its *best* parallelism by exhaustive search performed the same as stock vLLM — parallelism tuning cannot recover what interference takes *(dossier: `papers/distserve.md`, §4; setup: 32× A100-80GB, OPT-13B/66B/175B in FP16 MHA)*.

And then the paper limits itself, in the passage this volume treats as definitive for personal hardware: in resource-constrained settings — "only a few or even a single GPU" — DistServe's design space "is significantly limited", it "struggles or even fails" to find useful allocations, and the authors recommend **non-disaggregated systems** there *(dossier: `papers/distserve.md`, §4, on the paper's §7)*. Splitwise reaches the matching conclusion from the cluster side: saturate the system and phase splitting devolves to the baselines *(dossier: `papers/splitwise.md`, §4)*. What survives the trip to one GPU is not the mechanism but the **budget discipline**: TTFT and time-per-output-token as separate, separately-owned budgets, and phase interference treated as a named cost wherever the phases share hardware — which, on one card, is inside the step loop (§3.3), not across a network.

### 3.5 The personal-runner reduction: sessions, residency, bounded steps

**[Synthesis — the corpus's scheduling position, assembled from `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, §§2–5, and the runner dossiers.]** A single-user runner's scheduler is not a shrunken fleet scheduler. Its jobs are different:

- **The unit is the session, not the request.** A durable session object owns its KV for its whole lifetime; turns append to it. Scheduling decides among *sessions* (the live chat, a background summarisation, a swipe being regenerated), not among anonymous requests (ledger #1; Aesir HS-6, Chapter 8).
- **Steps are bounded and interruptible.** The Orca step loop plus a Sarathi-style token budget gives per-token streaming, between-token interrupt checks, and chunked ingest with progress and cancellation (ledger #18, #25). CPU work — tokenisation, grammar-mask preparation, sampling bookkeeping — must live off the decode critical path, because at batch 1 the CPU term is worth proportionally more the smaller the model (ledger #25).
- **Ordering is cache-aware where bursts exist.** A single user still produces bursts: a reply plus its memory-extraction sidecars, a set of swipe alternatives. Ordering pending generations by shared-prefix length — SGLang's cache-aware scheduling, which reached on average 96% of the optimal hit rate on the paper's benchmarks *(dossier: `papers/radixattention-sglang.md`, §5)* — stops a 6 GB pool thrashing at exactly the moments the user is waiting (ledger #24). Fairness machinery, by contrast, has no one to be fair to; SGLang's own dossier records starvation of low-hit requests as the known cost of cache-aware ordering in multi-tenant use, a cost that mostly evaporates when the "tenants" are one person's own jobs **[synthesis]**.
- **Overflow spills idle, never running.** Fleet preemption-by-recompute is rejected as a sole policy for this target (ledger R2): on a laptop, re-prefilling a 32K-token conversation costs minutes, paid by the person waiting on exactly that conversation. The local policy inverts it: spill *idle* sessions to RAM/disk, recompute only short spans, keep recompute as the always-working fallback (ledger #11). vLLM V1's removal of swap in favour of recompute-only preemption is correct *for vLLM's setting* and wrong to copy into this one *(dossiers: `runners/vllm.md`, §3.3; `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §4.3)*.

What is refused, explicitly (ledger R11): continuous batching *as a throughput project*, multi-process engine cores, CUDA graphs as a headline investment, disaggregated prefill/decode, and tensor/pipeline parallelism — fleet machinery solving Python contention, multi-GPU communication, and fairness problems a single-user Go binary does not have. The step loop, the token budget, and block allocation are already extracted; the rest stays in the datacentre.

## Chapter 4 — KV cache, the heart

> **Depth pointer.** This chapter gives the runner-side essentials: the arithmetic, the allocation geometry, and the policy families every builder must command. The full design space — layout trade-offs, sharing semantics, lifecycle policies, precision theory, hybrid-state handling, and the decision procedure with its worked 6 GB case — is the province of the memory corpus's crown volume, `memory/MEMORY-COMPENDIUM.md`, synthesised principally from `memory/kv-cache-design.md`. **(v2)** That volume is **now banked** (the v1 gap flag is closed): its Chapter 3 is the deepest KV text in the corpus, its Chapter 8 reads this book's ledger through the memory lens, and its Chapter 10 carries the memory workstream's own gaps-and-frontiers agenda. This chapter stands alone as the runner-side treatment and defers to it on design-space depth.

### 4.1 Why the cache exists, and why it is exact

A decoder-only Transformer chooses token *t+1* by letting the new token's query attend to the keys and values of all previous tokens. Those keys and values are deterministic functions of the earlier tokens and the weights: token *i*'s key at layer *l* does not change when token *i+1* arrives. Recomputing them every step would make generation quadratic per token, cubic over a generation. The KV cache is the memoisation of that observation: prefill computes and stores each token's keys/values once; each decode step computes one new token's pair, appends it, and reads the stored history. Per-step work drops from O(*t*) forward passes to O(1) new-token work plus an O(*t*) *read* — converting an impossible compute bill into a merely large memory bill, which is Chapter 1's trade in its purest form *(dossier: `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §1.1)*.

Three consequences shape everything below. The cache is **exact**, not a summary: attention over the full cache is mathematically the attention the model would compute from scratch, so every lossy technique in §4.5 is a deliberate, gradeable departure from exactness. The cache is **append-mostly and read-whole**: written once, in order, never modified, read in full every step — the access pattern almost every layout decision exploits, and whose breaking points (branching, editing, sharing) are where the complications live. And the cache is **per-sequence state, not model state**: weights are shared by every user of a model; the cache belongs to one conversation — which is why sessions, privacy, persistence, and preemption all live here *(dossier: `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §1.1)*.

### 4.2 The size arithmetic

For a dense Transformer, one token contributes one key vector and one value vector per KV head, per layer:

> bytes per token = 2 (K+V) × *L* (attention layers) × *H_kv* (KV heads — **not** query heads) × *D* (head dimension) × *b* (bytes per element)

Total cache = bytes-per-token × tokens × concurrent sequences. Two refinements matter for real models: sliding-window layers need only min(tokens, window) entries, and hybrid models pay this only on their attention layers (§4.6) *(dossier: `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §1.2)*.

Fix the scale with the corpus's reference model. A Llama-3.1-8B-class model at FP16 costs **131,072 bytes per token** (128 KiB): ≈0.5 GiB at 4K context, ≈4 GiB at 32K, ≈16 GiB at the full advertised 128K — on top of ≈5 GiB of 4-bit weights *(dossier: `runners/ollama.md`, §5.5)*. The **crossover context** *T\** = weight bytes ÷ bytes-per-token is where the cache outweighs the model; beyond it, context policy *is* memory policy. This single equation explains most small-card misery in the corpus, and the ledger's first memory rule follows from it: **budget from `kv_rate` (bytes/token computed from model metadata), never from parameter count or advertised context** (ledger #12; `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §7.3, Rule 1).

The worked case that governs the build campaign (purplelongship: RTX 2060 Max-Q, 6 GB VRAM, verified in our records at driver install, 2026-10-28; one long character chat; 8B-class model, Q4_K_M weights 5,018 MiB on the 6,144 MiB card) runs the decision procedure of `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §7.2 — **restated in v2 on the split reserve** of §1.6 *(dossier: `memory/gpu-memory-internals.md`, §7.3)*. **Headless case** (reserve ~512 MiB → ~614 MiB KV budget): v1's numbers stand — **4,912 tokens at FP16**, which *fails* even the 8K minimum for this workload, and **9,246 tokens at q8_0**, which meets 8K with ~13% headroom; hence the campaign's headless defaults: q8_0 KV on small cards (ledger #3) and `num_ctx` 8,192 for this pairing. **Display-attached case** — purplelongship's normal state, a laptop driving its own panel (reserve **0.9–1.1 GiB** → KV budget **≈0–250 MiB**): FP16 affords **at most ~2,000 tokens and possibly nothing at all**; q8_0 at most **~3,800**; even q4_0 at most ~7,100. On the desk configuration the 8K minimum is therefore **unreachable for this model at any cache precision**, and the honest levers become a smaller weight quant chosen by lineage (ledger #42), the sub-8-bit KV tiers (ledger #19/#20), or headless running — chosen by the budget (ledger #12), never discovered by an out-of-memory error. The reserve is still a bounded planning range, not a fleet measurement; the measurement that retires it is Chapter 10's Q17 **[flagged uncertainty, narrowed in v2 from an unexamined constant to a named case split]**. The procedure's alternative branch is equally instructive: a 3B-class GQA model (`kv_rate` = 2×28×8×128×2 = 114,688 B/token at FP16; Q4 weights ≈2.0 GiB) buys context by architecture, not by precision — the first hint that *H_kv* is the most powerful term in the formula (§4.5).

### 4.3 Allocation: from contiguous arenas to paged blocks

The classical design gives each sequence one contiguous tensor region sized to its maximum context, allocated up front (early llama.cpp; Ollama's inherited model; slot-partitioned windows of `num_ctx × num_parallel` tokens). It is simple, and it wastes exactly what a small card cannot spare: the full window is paid whether or not the conversation grows into it, and internal/external fragmentation strands the remainder. PagedAttention's profiling found prior systems using only **20.4%–38.2%** of allocated KV memory for actual token states *(dossier: `papers/pagedattention-vllm.md`, §5)*.

**Paged (blocked) allocation** replaces the arena with one global pool of fixed-size blocks — 16 tokens by default in vLLM — plus a per-sequence block table mapping logical to physical blocks, allocated on demand as tokens arrive. Fragmentation waste falls to at most a partial last block per sequence; blocks can be shared between sequences with reference counts and copy-on-write at branch points; preemption, retention, and tiering all become operations *on blocks* *(dossiers: `papers/pagedattention-vllm.md`, §§3–4; `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §2.1)*. The throughput consequence in the paper's setting was **2–4× at the same latency** over FasterTransformer and Orca, scaling with concurrency — a number that does *not* transfer to batch 1, where there is little fragmentation from concurrency to remove. What transfers is the accounting: on-demand blocks are what let a long chat grow without pre-paying for context it may never use, and what make the ledger's residency ideas (#5, #9, #10) implementable at all. The ledger therefore ranks block allocation in one pre-sized arena as foundation #4, with its acceptance test stated as *memory honesty* — exact KV accounting, teardown returning provably to baseline, in the MimirAlloc lifecycle shape — not speed *(dossier: `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, #4, §4)*.

**Block size** is the layout's one true tuning knob, trading internal fragmentation (≈*B*/2 wasted token-slots per sequence), sharing granularity (reuse is claimable only in whole blocks; vLLM caches only full blocks), and kernel/transfer efficiency. The PagedAttention paper's sweep found 16–128 tokens good on long ShareGPT traces. The corpus disagrees with itself, honestly, about the single-sequence optimum: the KV dossier defaults to 16, the vLLM dossier argues 32–64 for our shape — ledger Open Question 11, to be settled by a small sweep on the target during implementation *(dossiers: `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §2.2; `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, §6)*. Inside the block, K and V arrangement is a kernel contract, not a cosmetic choice: separate K and V tensors (llama.cpp's form), transposed-K variants, and layer-first vs token-first organisation each favour different kernels and different tier-transfer shapes *(dossier: `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §2.3)*.

### 4.4 Prefix sharing: reuse is the largest free prefill

If two sequences share a token prefix, the prefix's KV is identical — *provided* positions, model, quantisation, and cache precision all match. The corpus knows three mechanisms for exploiting this, one invariant underneath: **reuse is exact-position or it is not reuse** *(dossier: `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §§3.1, 3.4)*.

1. **Last-prompt prefix match (llama.cpp/Ollama).** The runner compares the incoming prompt with the prompt previously processed *in that slot* and evaluates only the differing suffix. Present and automatic in Ollama — every request carries the runner's prompt-caching flag set true — but narrow: per-slot, last-prompt only, exact-token-prefix, not shared across slots or models, and destroyed when the runner unloads *(dossier: `runners/ollama.md`, §4.4)*. In an append-only chat this narrow mechanism is already worth a great deal: each new turn reuses everything except the newest exchange, collapsing per-turn prefill from O(history) to O(new tokens) — *if* prompt rendering is byte-stable (ledger #13) and history is never reassembled in a different order.
2. **Hash-chained blocks (vLLM).** Each full block is named by a hash of (parent block's hash, this block's token IDs, extras such as LoRA id and cache salt). Because the parent hash is an input, a block's name certifies its entire prefix; matching names can be shared safely, and a mid-history edit invalidates exactly the suffix after the edit. Copy-on-write handles the branch point: a block with reference count > 1 is copied before either branch writes past it *(dossier: `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §3.1)*. For chat, CoW is the swipe/regenerate primitive: a rejected reply costs one boundary-block copy plus the new suffix, not a re-prefill *(dossier: `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §3.2)*.
3. **Radix tree (SGLang).** The same paged pool under a different index: a compressed trie over token sequences, so *partial* overlaps (same character, different scenario; same scenario, different character) are captured by node splitting, finished requests' KV is retained under LRU eviction, and scheduling can be cache-aware. The paper reports hit rates of **50%–99%** across its benchmarks and up to **6.4× throughput / 3.7× lower latency** against 2023-era baselines (vLLM v0.2.5 among them; A10G/A100 hardware, Llama-2 7B–70B, Mixtral, LLaVA) — period measurements, not current rankings, as the dossier insists. The production datum travels better: one month of Chatbot Arena serving showed hit rates of 52.4% (LLaVA-NeXT-34B) and 74.1% (Vicuna-33B), cutting first-token latency by an average of 1.7× for Vicuna-33B. The honest boundary also travels: for multi-turn chat with *long* outputs the paper reports almost no speedup — decoding dominates and sessions share little *(dossier: `papers/radixattention-sglang.md`, §5)*. SGLang's current frontier extends the tree across tiers (HiCache: GPU → host DRAM → external storage, with different layouts per tier) and across memory kinds (the Unified Radix Cache work) *(dossier: `runners/sglang.md`)* — the tiering direction the ledger adopts as #10/#11, at personal scale.

Two disciplines complete the picture. **Scope and privacy:** a prefix hit is measurably faster than a miss, so in multi-tenant serving the cache is a timing side channel for probing other tenants' prompts; vLLM's mitigations (cache salts, scoped sharing) exist for that reason. The personal translation is scope-keying: hash-chained blocks keyed by (parent hash, tokens, model + quant + cache precision, application-chosen scope id — persona/session family), so sharing happens exactly where the application intends it *(dossier: `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §§3.3, 7.3)*. **Non-prefix reuse is a different, approximate problem.** Prompt Cache (declared reusable segments with precomputed states, spliced into new prompts; largest gains on CPU) and CacheBlend (reuse every chunk's KV anywhere, then repair the missing cross-attention by recomputing only the highest-deviation ~15% of tokens; EuroSys 2025, RAG workloads) both attack lore/RAG assembly, where chunks arrive in varying order and never form a clean prefix. Both are approximate in ways prefix reuse is not, and the ledger defers them behind a quality gate (R8): CacheBlend's ~15% figure comes from FP/8-bit datacentre models on QA/summarisation and must be re-measured on roleplay models and quantised weights before it means anything here *(dossiers: `papers/prompt-cache.md`; `papers/cacheblend.md`; `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, R8)*.

**Persistence** is prefix retention across time. A saved session — KV blocks plus a validity envelope naming model, quantisation, cache precision, and token stream — restores in seconds what cold prefill repays in minutes. The Aesir dossier's stateless-resend case is the hybrid form (§4.6); the KV dossier's restore comparison, carried in the ledger, is **2.14 s restore vs 160.7 s cold prefill for 7.3K tokens on a CPU-only box** (ledger #10, grade A) — the single most lopsided cost ratio in the corpus, and the reason tiering is a policy over *retained prefix objects*: there is nothing worth tiering until prefix retention (#5) exists to retain *(dossiers: `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, #10, §4; `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §4.4)*.

### 4.5 Shrinking the cache: precision, selection, architecture

When the cache still does not fit, three families shrink it — by bytes per entry, by entries kept, and by architecture. They compose, and the ledger's ordering among them is deliberate: precision policy (#3) before eviction scoring (#9), because scoring tuned against a cache whose size is about to halve is work done twice *(dossier: `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, §4)*.

**Precision — the ladder.** The cache is a numerical object, and its precision ladder in the llama.cpp formats runs FP16 (1×) → q8_0-class (≈0.53× of FP16 bytes) → q4_0-class (≈0.28×). Ollama exposes the ladder globally as `OLLAMA_KV_CACHE_TYPE` (`f16` default; `q8_0` about half the memory at a project-described very small precision cost; `q4_0` about a quarter, with cost growing more noticeable at large contexts), gated by Flash Attention support, with the project's own caution that many-GQA-head models can be more sensitive *(dossier: `runners/ollama.md`, §4.5)*. The ledger's refinement is to make the choice **per-model and K/V-separate** (#3): keys and values have different error geometry, and a global switch forces the most sensitive model in the library to set everyone's context.

The research frontier refines *where* the bits go. **KIVI** quantises keys per-channel and values per-token — the asymmetry is the discovery: key channels carry persistent outliers, value tokens do not — at 2 bits with a 128-token FP16 residual window, matching FP16 quality on its suites at **2.6× less peak memory** (Llama-2-7B, single A100-80GB, ShareGPT-derived workload) and 2.35–3.47× throughput in batched serving. Its warnings are part of the result: Falcon/MQA, already head-compressed, needed 4 bits; LongBench evaluation capped at 4,096 tokens for most models, so nothing validates 2-bit at 32K+ *(dossier: `papers/kivi.md`, §§5–6)*. **KVQuant** adds pre-RoPE per-channel key quantisation, Fisher-weighted non-uniform datatypes, and 1% outlier sparing: at 3-bit-1%, **<0.1 perplexity degradation** (WikiText-2/C4) at 4.8× cache saving; at 4-bit-1%, <0.02 degradation at 3.7×. Its retrieval evidence is the sharpest in the family: on passkey retrieval to 32K, KVQuant-2bit-1% holds ≈1.00 at every length where KIVI-2 decays to 0.68–0.76, and on RULER at 32K, FP16's 56.40 becomes 53.65 (KVQuant-3bit-1%) against 39.78 (KIVI-2) — perplexity and retrieval are different measurements, and retrieval degrades far faster *(dossier: `papers/kvquant.md`, §5)*. The ledger extracts KVQuant's near-free parts as #20 (sink/head FP16 protection; 1% outlier sparing) and rejects the full five-mechanism stack as a default (R6): per-model calibration artefacts that community GGUFs do not ship, and a pre-RoPE cache format incompatible with every existing engine's store. KIVI's full asymmetric scheme is ranked as #19; its 2-bit *default* is deferred behind per-model grading at the longest supported context (R7). The standing rule (ledger Open Question 9): **no sub-8-bit scheme is labelled for a context length it has not been retrieval-graded at.**

**Selection — which entries earn their bytes.** Eviction policies depart from exactness by dropping entries. **StreamingLLM** discovered why naive windowing collapses: models dump attention mass onto the first few tokens (the **attention sink**) regardless of content, so evicting the opening tokens destroys stability itself — on PG-19, window attention's perplexity hit **5,158** where full recomputation scored 5.43 and sink+window **5.40**. Pinning ~4 initial tokens plus a sliding window gives constant-memory, stable streaming past 4M tokens, up to 22.2× faster per token than recomputation baselines (single A6000, Llama-2-7B/13B) *(dossier: `papers/streamingllm.md`, §5)*. The honesty boundary is the dossier's and the ledger's (R13): stability is not recall — anything evicted is gone, and "infinite chat" must never be allowed to imply "remembers everything". **H2O** splits a fixed budget between heavy-hitter tokens (by accumulated attention) and recent tokens, holding near-full-cache quality at a **20% budget** across its suites; its premise is measured attention sparsity above 95%, and its cautionary scale example is a 30B model at batch 128 × 1,024 tokens needing ~180 GB of full cache *(dossier: `papers/h2o.md`, §5)*. **SnapKV** selects prompt KV per head by voting from the prompt's final observation window — before generation begins — serving 16K prompts from constant-size caches with an **8.2× memory-efficiency** figure at 16K (single A100-80GB) and processing up to 380K context tokens from a 1,024-entry cache in its needle test, with summarisation of long single documents as the named weak spot *(dossier: `papers/snapkv.md`, §5)*. **PyramidKV** observes that information funnels with depth — lower layers aggregate broadly, upper layers focus — and allocates layer-decreasing budgets: matching full-cache LongBench at **12% retention** (LLaMA-3-8B average 41.49 vs full cache 41.46), and earning its keep exactly when budgets starve (at average 64 entries/layer: 34.76 vs SnapKV's 33.05, H2O's 33.89) *(dossier: `papers/pyramidkv.md`, §5)*. The ledger composes these into one declared policy (#9, #21, #22): pinned head (persona + sinks) + scored middle (heavy-hitter where the kernel can supply scores; SnapKV-style re-voting per turn as history compaction) + protected recent window, with per-layer pyramidal budgets over the scored selection. Two open costs are named honestly: fused attention kernels do not materialise the scores these policies consume (ledger Open Question 6 — the corpus's largest single technical unknown), and per-turn re-snap is the corpus's own adaptation, unmeasured anywhere (Open Question 7).

**Architecture — the terms of the formula itself.** Runners cannot retrofit architecture, but builders must read it, because *H_kv* dominates the arithmetic. **GQA** shares each KV head among a group of query heads, cutting cache by *H/G* against MHA; the paper's uptrained GQA-8 ran at **0.28 s/sample against MHA's 1.51** at essentially equal quality (47.1 vs 47.2 average) on T5 XXL (TPUv4 testbed — encoder-decoder evidence, as the dossier flags; decoder-only deployments postdate it), with conversion costing ~5% of original training compute *(dossier: `papers/gqa.md`, §5)*. **MLA** (DeepSeek-V2) goes further: cache a single 512-dimensional latent plus a 64-dimensional RoPE key per token per layer, reconstructing per-head keys/values on the fly — cutting KV by **93.3%** against DeepSeek 67B while *beating* MHA in controlled ablations (cache per token 110.6K → 15.6K elements at small-MoE scale; 860.2K → 34.6K at large scale, with benchmark scores rising, not falling). The flagship's 5.76× generation-throughput figure is a *system* result (MLA + MoE sparsity + FP8 + KV quantisation + serving stack, 8× H800 node) and must never be quoted as MLA alone. MLA is train-from-scratch: no runner can retrofit it onto a GGUF *(dossier: `papers/deepseek-v2-mla.md`, §§5–6)*. **[Synthesis]** GQA and MLA together mark the field's direction: the cache is being designed away at training time, one architectural generation at a time — which is why Chapter 10 lists MLA-class architectures as a frontier a runner master watches rather than a feature a runner ships.

### 4.6 Hybrid and SSM state: the cache is no longer the whole memory (v2 — two memory disciplines)

A growing model class mixes attention layers with recurrent (SSM/Gated-DeltaNet) layers. A recurrent layer's inter-token memory is a **fixed-size state** — a recurrent vector plus convolution state — not a per-token list. It does not grow with context, it cannot be paged token-by-token, and a prefix of it cannot be "kept": the state after token *n* is one opaque summary of the whole prefix. The honest fit equation therefore becomes per-component: `weights + T × kv_rate + sessions × state_bytes + checkpoints × state_bytes + buffers`, where `kv_rate` counts attention layers only *(dossier: `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §6)*.

**The two-memory-disciplines rule (v2).** The Mamba dossiers turn this observation into the chapter's governing rule for hybrids *(dossiers: `papers/mamba.md`, §6; `papers/mamba-2.md`, §6)*:

> A hybrid session's memory is managed under **two different disciplines at once**: **paged KV, priced per token, on the attention layers** — block-allocated, shareable, evictable at block granularity, exactly as in §§4.3–4.5 — and **bounded, bit-exact checkpoint state, priced per snapshot, on the recurrent layers** — whole-blob snapshot/restore at prefix boundaries, restored bit-exact or not at all, kept under a hard count bound with a named eviction rule. Neither discipline may be applied to the other component: there is no per-token scoring *inside* a fused recurrent state (no H2O/SnapKV analogue exists for it), and no whole-blob checkpointing of a KV cache that blocks would serve better.

The lineage explains why the state is the size it is. Mamba-1's selective SSM carries per layer a recurrent state of channels × *N* with *N* = 16 in its released language models, plus a short convolution state (kernel width ~4); neither grows with sequence length, so a pure-SSM model's per-sequence memory is a constant and its decode step is O(1) in context — the crossover arithmetic of §4.2 simply does not apply to those layers *(dossier: `papers/mamba.md`, §§3, 6)*. Mamba-2's structured state-space duality (SSD) restricts the transition to scalar-identity form and computes chunk-wise — matmul inside chunks, recurrence between them — importing attention's vocabulary (heads, head dimensions) into the SSM; its lineage runs *N* = 64–128 as standard, **4–8× Mamba-1's per-layer state**, and the state is commonly kept in **FP32** even when weights and KV are quantised, because it is accumulated over the whole sequence. "Constant-size" at *N* = 128 across dozens of recurrent layers is how a snapshot reaches the size below *(dossier: `papers/mamba-2.md`, §§3, 6)*. SSD also tells the runner where checkpoints are cheap: chunked prefill already materialises clean state at regular chunk boundaries, so snapshotting at chunk boundaries pays almost nothing extra to create reuse points during a prefill the runner was doing anyway; and a backend that falls back to a fully recurrent prefill because no chunked kernel was written for it has silently lost the architecture's main advance *(dossier: `papers/mamba-2.md`, §6)*. Production went hybrid for a measured reason the dossiers record: pure recurrent models trail attention on exact recall and copying, so the field's answer is attention at intervals for retrieval, recurrence for the bulk — which is precisely the shape that forces both disciplines into one runner *(dossier: `papers/mamba.md`, §5.2)*.

Our own Aesir evidence is the corpus's worked example. The qwen35 27B (64 blocks, hidden 5,120, 24 query heads / 4 KV heads, head dim 256, context 262,144; most layers recurrent, full attention at intervals) costs **4,096 B/token per attention layer** at FP16 by the §4.2 formula. If attention sits at every fourth block — 16 of 64 layers — the hybrid total is a fraction of a dense model's; the exact interval count is **unstated in the Aesir records read**, so the dossier gives the exact per-layer rate and an explicitly labelled illustrative total, and the ledger makes the settling step routine: one GGUF metadata read per hybrid model at onboarding (Open Question 14) **[flagged uncertainty, carried from the dossiers]** *(dossiers: `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §6.2; `aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`, §3.2)*.

Prefix reuse for hybrids required a new mechanism, and Q35-5 is the corpus's most original result: **snapshot the hybrid state** (SSM + convolution + boundary KV) at a stable prefix boundary; restore is **bit-exact** (state/convolution/KV diffs 0.0); a stateless resend reused **72 of 90 tokens** via snapshots after residency was destroyed; the store is bounded at **4 snapshots per session**; each snapshot costs ≈**158.9 MB** of SSM+convolution state at 27B scale, plus boundary KV. Branching, cheap for KV (copy-on-write blocks), is expensive for state (a full snapshot copy) — branch-heavy product features must be priced accordingly *(dossiers: `aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`, §3.2; `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §6.2; ledger #23)*. SGLang's Unified Radix Cache is the fleet-side generalisation of the same recognition — one tree indexing several memory kinds — described in the corpus without a verified claim that it is the default for all model classes *(dossier: `memory/kv-cache-design.md`, §6.3)* **[flagged as unverified in the source dossier]**.

### 4.7 RoPE scaling: trained, scaled, allocated — three numbers, never one (v2)

Everything above prices context in bytes. This section prices it in *meaning*: whether position *n* is a position the model can actually use. Rotary position embeddings (RoPE) assign each dimension pair its own rotation frequency and hence its own wavelength; beyond the trained length, the low-frequency dimensions' angles go out of distribution and the model degrades. Scaling methods renegotiate that boundary — and the corpus's two new dossiers (YaRN, LongRoPE) give this book its rule: **every model a runner serves has three lengths, and an honest runner displays all three** — (i) the *trained* length *L*, (ii) the *scaled* length *L′ = s·L*, valid only under the scaling configuration the model was fine-tuned or validated with, and (iii) the *allocated* length, the user's `num_ctx`, which is what memory is actually spent on *(dossiers: `papers/yarn.md`, §6a; `papers/longrope.md`, §6a)*.

**YaRN** is the analytic method: NTK-by-parts interpolation keyed to each dimension's wavelength ratio *r(d) = L/λ_d* — interpolate fully where *r < α*, extrapolate untouched where *r > β*, blend between (Llama-family values α = 1, β = 32, surviving in serving configs as `beta_slow`/`beta_fast`) — plus an attention temperature correcting the entropy growth interpolation causes, fitted in closed form and folded into the rotary embeddings so the kernel is untouched. Its fine-tuned results are real (Llama 2-7B extended *s* = 32 to 128K with perplexity still falling at 128K, at ~10× fewer tokens than prior extension methods), and its costs are equally real and concentrated: short-context benchmarks degrade measurably, worst on MMLU (−2.1 points at 7B *s* = 32; −3.9 at 13B), growing with the extension factor. Without fine-tuning, the reliable form is **Dynamic-YaRN at >2×** — scale recomputed from current length, *s = max(1, l′/L)* — and static YaRN applied to a raw, unextended model can *collapse* (the LongRoPE paper's comparison: perplexity 32.64 @ 8K, 87.89 @ 16K on PG19, worse than plain interpolation) *(dossier: `papers/yarn.md`, §§3–4)*. **LongRoPE** is the searched limiting case: per-dimension rescale factors found by evolutionary search (seeded with PI/NTK/YaRN, monotonicity-constrained), an initial-token threshold that leaves the attention-sink tokens uninterpolated, progressive fine-tuning to 256K, then a second search to 2048K with no training above 256K — and a separate *recovery* search for short inputs, because a 512× interpolation crushes the home window (recovery is worth ~3.6 average benchmark points in its ablation). Its 2048K claim is precise and narrow: perplexity does not cliff and a single hidden passkey is usually retrievable (≥90% to 2048K for the LLaMA2 variant, *n* = 10 iterations), in models fine-tuned to 256K; no multi-needle, aggregation or QA task is run at extreme lengths, and no serving cost is ever mentioned *(dossier: `papers/longrope.md`, §§3–4)*.

Three runner consequences follow, all load-bearing. **First, scaling does not discount the cache by one byte.** Positions beyond *L* cost exactly what unscaled positions cost: at the Llama-3.1-8B-class GQA rate of 128 KiB/token, a 128K window is 16 GiB of FP16 cache; at a Llama-2-7B-class MHA rate of 512 KiB/token, LongRoPE's 2048K is **1 TiB** — and even at the GQA rate it is 256 GiB. RoPE scaling decides whether positions are *meaningful*; §§4.2–4.3 decide whether they are *affordable*; a UI that lets a scaling headline anywhere near a context control is lying by juxtaposition. **Second, scaling is part of cache identity.** A block computed under YaRN ×4 is a different tensor from the same tokens under ×2 or unscaled; dynamic scaling and LongRoPE's short/long factor switch both invalidate post-RoPE cached keys across the change, so a session must pin its scaling at creation (deciding the factor set from the session's *allocated* window, not its current length) or store keys pre-RoPE and pay rotation in the read path — chosen explicitly, never mixed silently *(dossiers: `papers/yarn.md`, §6; `papers/longrope.md`, §6)*. **Third, the failure mode is quiet.** Beyond ~2× the trained length without a model fine-tuned for it, expect fluent text with worsening recall — in a character chat it reads as the character *forgetting*, and will be blamed on the model, not the setting. The runner must say so at the moment of the setting, and must surface scaling policy and eviction policy (§4.5) as separate decisions: a YaRN-extended 128K window with a 4-token head preservation is a larger room with the same trapdoor (Chapter 8).

### 4.8 MoE models: total-vs-active, and the expert cache (v2)

Mixture-of-experts models split the two quantities every other chapter could treat as one. **Residency follows the total parameter count; per-token cost follows the active count.** Mixtral 8×7B holds ~47B parameters in total and activates ~12.9B per token (top-2 of 8 experts per MoE layer, router a single learned projection; attention, embeddings and head are dense and shared, ~1.6B parameters, touched by every token). Its quality case is paper-measured — matching or beating Llama 2 70B on most of the authors' pipeline benchmarks (MMLU 70.6% vs 69.9%; the large wins in maths, code and recall, a few commonsense measures the other way) at 5× fewer active parameters *(dossier: `papers/mixtral.md`, §§4–5)*. Its serving arithmetic on this fleet is unforgiving: at Q4_K_M the model is ≈26–29 GB — inside the fleet host's 39 GB of RAM, **never inside 6 GB of VRAM at any quant** — while its KV cache is an ordinary GQA cache, priced by §4.2 entirely unaffected by the MoE structure: MoE sparsity saves weight bandwidth, never cache bytes *(dossiers: `papers/mixtral.md`, §7a; `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, #38)*.

The mechanism that makes such a model usable at all is a second cache, and MoE-Infinity's trace studies locate its signal precisely *(dossier: `papers/moe-infinity.md`, §§3–5)*:

- **Locality is request-level.** Per token, a token activates a tiny slice; per *request*, the slice stays small and stable — fewer than 5% of experts repeatedly activated during one request's decode for many-expert models, ≈25% for coarse Mixtral; across requests the skew evaporates toward uniform. Any policy built on *global* expert frequencies averages away exactly the signal it needs. Mixtral's own routing analysis supplies the micro-foundation: routing tracks syntax and token type more than topic, and consecutive tokens repeat an expert in either slot ≈62–67% at middle layers *(dossier: `papers/mixtral.md`, §5)*.
- **The policy is trace, match, prefetch, evict by predicted irrelevance.** Routing decisions accumulate into a per-request Expert Activation Matrix; new requests are matched by cosine similarity to stored traces (the EAM Collection — best latency at ~120 traces in the paper's sweep), producing a predicted activation map that drives both prefetch (upcoming layers, decayed by layer proximity) and eviction (lowest predicted priority out, with a structural bonus for early layers, whose misses cannot be hidden behind earlier compute). Dense parameters and KV are pinned in GPU memory and never evicted; only experts move over PCIe; the full model lives in host memory.
- **The honest shape of the results.** On a single RTX A5000 (24 GB) the headline range is 3.1–16.7× per-token latency over vLLM/Ollama/DeepSpeed/BrainStorm baselines across many-expert models — and **1.4× for Mixtral**, explicitly the authors' worst case, because its experts are few (≈340 MB each in the paper's serving precision), large, and its per-request activation ratio the highest studied. No consumer 6–8 GB card, no quantised models, and no batch > 1 appear in the evaluation: **the policy transfers, the latencies do not.**
- **Fleet arithmetic (the ledger's translation, grade A/C).** On the 6 GB card, ≈3–4 GB of expert cache (after shared weights, KV, buffers and the §7.3-class reserve — display-attached case, §1.6) holds ≈30–40 of Mixtral's 256 experts at Q4 (~100 MB each) — 12–15% of the pool against a measured per-request working set of ~25%. At ≈8 ms per expert fetch over PCIe Gen3 ×16 (≈4 ms at Gen4), a 90% hit rate costs ≈50 ms/token of bus time (~20 tok/s ceiling from misses alone, partly overlappable); at 75%, ≈130 ms (~7–8 tok/s). The hit-rate split at a cache this small is **unmeasured anywhere** (Chapter 10, Q19). The mechanism's saving grace is its failure shape: a miss costs time, never correctness — the safest failure shape a serving optimisation can have *(dossier: `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, #38)*.

The incumbent local stack already contains the static version of this design — llama.cpp's `--cpu-moe` placement plus a `--moe-cache-mib` GPU cache size knob — with no activation tracing *(dossier: `runners/llamacpp-ggml.md`, §5.2)*. The ledger ranks the traced, request-level upgrade as idea #38, with expert-cache hit rate exposed as a first-class metric: on small cards it predicts MoE decode speed better than any FLOP figure. **[Synthesis]** Read beside thesis 2, the expert cache is the KV cache's sibling, not its rival: both are per-conversation, both are priced against the same pool by one arbiter (ledger #31), and both are why "the cache is the product" now names a family of caches, not one.

## Chapter 5 — Quantization: weights as provisioning

### 5.1 Why quantization is the decode lever

Chapter 1's ceiling — tokens/sec ≲ bandwidth ÷ bytes-per-token — makes quantization arithmetic before it is numerics. Halving the bytes a decode step must stream roughly doubles the ceiling, *if* the step is truly on the bandwidth slope and dequantization does not become the new ceiling. That conditional carries the whole chapter. It is why 4-bit-class formats dominate local inference, why the corpus grades every quantizer twice (once for size, once for fidelity, never letting one verdict stand in for the other), and why the Aesir record's cautionary tale (§5.4) is about *where* dequantization happens in the loop nest rather than about any format's maths.

Weights, KV cache, and activations are three separate quantization axes with different error geometry and different owners. This chapter is about weights; KV precision was Chapter 4 (§4.5); the two interact operationally — a runner's *effective* quality is set by whichever axis was squeezed hardest — and the ledger insists both be reported, per model, in force (ideas #3, #6).

### 5.2 Three provisioning philosophies

The corpus's runners embody three answers to the question *when is precision chosen?*

**Authored menus — GGUF.** The GGUF ecosystem pre-computes precision at conversion time and ships it as a file choice. Its K-quant family quantises in blocks with two levels of scale metadata, buying fidelity per bit over flat legacy schemes; nominal costs run Q2_K 2.625, Q3_K 3.4375, Q4_K 4.5, Q5_K 5.5, Q6_K 6.5625 bits per weight. The deeper idea is that **a file type is a recipe, not a tensor type**: `Q4_K` is a tensor type, while `Q4_K_M` is a per-tensor assignment policy — most tensors at Q4_K, the empirically sensitive ones (classically half of `attn_v` and `ffn_down`, and the token embedding/output in many recipes) promoted to Q6_K. Measured on Llama 3.1 8B in llama-quantize's own documentation (via secondary compilations read for the dossier), Q4_K_M lands at ≈**4.89 bpw effective**, not 4.5 — which is why Q4_K_M became the ecosystem default: sensitivity is structured, not uniform, and the recipe spends bits where the structure says they matter *(dossier: `runners/llamacpp-ggml.md`, §2.2)*. Ollama's library inherits this wholesale: tags select size and quantisation together, with Q4_K_M the conventional default recommendation *(dossier: `runners/ollama.md`, §6.1)*. The menu's virtue is zero load-time cost and total predictability; its cost is coarseness — the user who needs 4.2 bpw to fit a model beside a long KV cache is offered 3.9 or 4.89 and nothing between.

**Continuous bit-rate — EXL2.** ExLlamaV2's format refuses the menu: different parts of the same model are quantised at different bit-widths drawn from {2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8}, at two granularities (across layers/matrices, and *within* a single layer), so the model's **average** bits per weight is a continuous dial — 4.65, 3.0, 2.55 bpw are all ordinary artefacts, chosen by a GPTQ-derived, calibration-driven assignment. The runner and the format were designed against each other: kernels co-designed with the format (§5.3) are what make arbitrary mixtures fast rather than merely storable *(dossier: `runners/exllamav2.md`, §§3, 9.1–9.2)*. The ledger's steal is the *thinking*, not the format: continuous bit-rate reasoning — "what average bpw fits this model beside this context on this card?" — as the provisioning question, with calibration-driven assignment as the method (ledger, via `runners/exllamav2.md`, §9.1–9.2). LMDeploy makes a specialisation bet of the same family from the fleet side: AWQ-first W4A16 weight quantisation with its own calibration tooling (`lmdeploy lite`), composed with blocked KV, online INT4/INT8 KV quantisation, and prefix caching in one package *(dossier: `runners/lmdeploy.md`, §§5, 10.2)*.

**Load-time — ISQ.** mistral.rs quantises *while loading*: almost any Hugging Face checkpoint can be quantised in-situ (ISQ) instead of requiring a pre-converted file, so provisioning becomes a load flag rather than a publishing pipeline *(dossier: `runners/mistral-rs.md`, §§2.3, 9.1)*. The user experience is the steal the ledger names: one artefact (the original checkpoint), precision chosen at the moment the machine's budget is known. The costs are load time and the loss of a shared, pre-graded artefact — every user's quantisation is their own, ungraded by anyone upstream — which is why the faithfulness discipline of §5.4 matters more, not less, in an ISQ world **[synthesis]**.

### 5.2a The quantization canon: activation statistics decide, method beats bit count (v2)

The four paper dossiers banked since v1 — GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant, QuIP# — are one canon, and they reorganise this chapter's provisioning philosophy from a menu into a method. Their shared discovery deserves statement as a principle: **quantization error is governed by activation statistics, not by weight statistics alone.** The weights are what is stored, but the *activations* decide which weights matter, where the outliers live, and how much damage a rounding does. Every method in the canon is, underneath its machinery, a way of measuring activations on calibration data and spending that knowledge at quantize time.

- **GPTQ — compensate with second-order information.** Quantize one layer at a time against the layer-wise reconstruction objective; when a column is rounded, distribute its error over the not-yet-quantized columns weighted by the inverse Hessian (the Optimal Brain Surgeon lineage), in a fixed order, with lazy batch updates and a Cholesky reformulation that keeps the inverse-Hessian arithmetic stable at 175B scale. Calibration: 128 random 2,048-token segments from C4. Results, as reported (A100): OPT-175B quantised in 4.2 hours; 4-bit near-lossless where round-to-nearest is not (WikiText2 perplexity FP16 8.34 → GPTQ-4bit 8.37 vs RTN-4bit 10.54); 3-bit enters a regime RTN cannot (8.68 vs ~7.3×10³, total collapse). Two patterns a provisioner must internalise: **larger models quantize more easily** (OPT-6.7B degrades 10.86 → 11.39 → 14.86 across FP16/4-bit/3-bit; OPT-1.3B far worse), and grouping helps at a *true-bitrate* cost that must be accounted — group-128 scales add ≈0.15 bits/weight, so nominal 2-bit grouped is ≈2.2 effective bits, group-32 ≈2.6 *(dossier: `papers/gptq.md`, §§3–4)*.
- **AWQ — protect the salient channels by scaling, not sparing.** Per-channel activation averages identify the ~1% of weight channels that carry the output; scaling those channels up (and their activations down, fused into the preceding operator offline) cuts their relative quantization error by ~1/*s* inside an ordinary uniform grouped format — no mixed precision, no reconstruction loop, one searched exponent α per layer. The ablation is the canon's cleanest exhibit: at INT3, keeping 1% of channels in FP16 *chosen by activation* recovers most of RTN's collapse (OPT-6.7B 23.54 → 11.39, FP16 10.86); chosen by weight magnitude or at random, it barely moves. At 4 bits all three methods (RTN/GPTQ/AWQ) sit close to FP16; **at 3 bits method decides usability** — LLaMA-7B INT3-g128 perplexity: FP16 5.68, AWQ 6.35, GPTQ-with-reorder 6.53, GPTQ 8.81, RTN 7.01 — and AWQ's low-capacity calibration (average magnitudes + one scalar per layer) is why it generalises: cross-distribution, AWQ degrades +0.5–0.6 perplexity where GPTQ degrades +2.3–4.9, and perplexity on a base corpus did not predict GPTQ's instruction-tuned and multi-modal degradation in AWQ's experiments *(dossier: `papers/awq.md`, §§3–4)*.
- **SmoothQuant — migrate the difficulty.** Activations carry persistent per-channel outliers that make W8A8 collapse (OPT-175B naive W8A8: perplexity 93,080, destroyed). Insert a per-channel diagonal scaling and its inverse — *Y = (X diag(s)⁻¹)(diag(s) W)* — moving range from activations (which cannot bear it) to weights (which can), split by a migration strength α (0.5 the general sweet spot; higher for more pathological models). After smoothing, even fully static per-tensor W8A8 holds accuracy (OPT-175B 66.5–66.8% average vs FP16 66.9%), and the speed exists because the *arithmetic itself* runs on INT8 tensor cores: up to 1.56× with ~2× memory saving in its FasterTransformer context. For this book's target the dossier's verdict governs: **W8A8 is a quality reference point, not a 6 GB deployment target** — it halves weight bytes where W4A16 quarters them, and its INT8-arithmetic prize belongs to compute-bound prefill, not bandwidth-bound single-user decode *(dossier: `papers/smoothquant.md`, §§3–4; ledger #42)*.
- **QuIP# — mix the outliers away, then quantize vectors.** Conjugate weights and Hessian by randomised Hadamard transforms (incoherence processing): the function is unchanged, the transformed weights become ball-shaped and outlier-free as *coordinates*, at O(*n* log *n*) cost and under 0.01 bits/weight of sign vectors. Then round 8-weight blocks jointly to an E8-lattice codebook (E8P) whose 2¹⁶-entry codebook decodes from a 256-entry, 1 KiB table — small enough for GPU L1, which is what makes vector quantization *fast*: AQLM's 1 MiB per-layer codebooks could not fit L1 and its 2-bit 7B ran *slower than FP16* (20.6 vs 33.1 tok/s in the paper's HF-harness comparison; QuIP# 106.3). At 2 bits QuIP# lands near others' 3-bit quality (Llama-2-7B 6.66 at context 2048; 6.19 at 4096), and its scaling claim inverts GPTQ-era advice: with this lineage, a *larger model at fewer bits* can beat a smaller model at 4 bits. The machinery is priced for the extreme regime — removing the E8 codebook costs ~5 perplexity points at 2 bits and ~0.03–0.17 at 4 bits — and its calibration is a materially larger investment (6,144 sequences from RedPajama-1T) that must be quoted with its results *(dossier: `papers/quip-sharp.md`, §§3–4)*.

**[Synthesis — the canon's three structural answers, as the ledger states them (#20, #42).]** Persistent per-channel activation outliers admit exactly three families of answer in the corpus: *rescale* the channels (SmoothQuant, AWQ), *mix* them away (QuIP#'s incoherence processing), or *choose the quantization axis* so outliers harm only themselves (KIVI's per-channel keys, Chapter 4). A designer who holds all three picks per artefact instead of by habit. The provisioning rule the ledger extracts as **#42 — choose artefacts by method lineage per bit-width, with true-bitrate accounting and a mature-kernel requirement, never by the bit count on the label** — is this section compressed: at 4 bits, GPTQ/AWQ/Q4_K-class artefacts are all sound and kernel maturity decides; at 3 bits, AWQ-class > GPTQ > RTN; at 2 bits, only the incoherence/codebook lineage (QuIP#/AQLM-class; GGUF IQ-class with activation-derived importance data) is credible, and scalar methods pushed below their regime are not a provisioning option. This rule is what makes Chapter 4's "buy VRAM back with a smaller weight quant" lever safe to pull — and on the display-attached card (§4.2, v2) that lever is now routinely load-bearing.

### 5.3 Kernel co-design: a format is its dequant path

A quantised weight is stored packed and consumed unpacked, and the unpacking has to live somewhere. The corpus's evidence converges on one rule, stated in the Aesir dossier as the QS-0 lesson: **dequantisation belongs outside the per-token loop** — once per layer per forward pass at most, never inside it. The briefed QS-0 finding is that re-unpacking weights every step made the prompt path **~35× slower**. That figure is carried in the Aesir dossier as *briefed-but-not-verified*: the VM-side records name the QS-0 baseline and its indictments but do not reproduce the number, which must be confirmed against `QS-0-BASELINE.md` on purpleraider (branch `yrsa/hip-serve`, commit `bad65106`) before being quoted outward. The design rule stands on the mechanism regardless of the exact factor, and the ledger repeats it as idea #25's foundation (Open Question 12) **[flagged uncertainty, carried from the dossiers]** *(dossiers: `aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`, §4.2; `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, #25, §6)*.

The positive form of the same lesson is Aesir's SIMD decode-width result: in single-row GEMMs the scalar dequantisation loop *was* the cost — IQ's scalar 256-lane dequant was ~90% of IQ cost — and S-030's width-16 SIMD decode gave **~3× with numerics proven unchanged three ways**, while batched prefill bought only ~1.4×. The wave crown result, Q8_0 decode-once SIMD fast path on Coder-3B, ran **776.88 s → 269.61 s (2.88×)**. The same scalar pocket in `gemm_q8_0` is the proven Coder-7B Q8_0 speed defect — never cite Q8_0 walls as a baseline until it is fixed *(dossier: `aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`, §6.1, from the Aesir Part II retrospective, S-053)*. ExLlamaV2 is the ecosystem proof of the co-design principle from the other direction: by owning format and kernels together, it removed the constraint that had bounded its V1 (GPTQ weights with generic kernels) and made batch-1 decode on consumer NVIDIA hardware its defining strength *(dossier: `runners/exllamav2.md`, §2.3)*. ggml's position is the portable version: block formats whose dequantisation is cheap in SIMD on every backend, from Raspberry Pi to multi-GPU server, because the format was designed against the weakest consumer, not the strongest *(dossier: `runners/llamacpp-ggml.md`)* **[synthesis in the final clause]**.

### 5.4 The faithfulness discipline: grade packed against original

Quantization is the place where "faster" most easily launders "wronger", so the corpus's grading discipline is stated here in full. It comes from Aesir's campaigns and binds every lossy change in the programme (weights, KV precision, eviction) via ledger idea #34.

- **Grade with teacher-forcing rank + logit gap on the raw prompt**, recording the oracle backend per row; CPU engine against CPU comparator only. Never grade against a templated CLI invocation (`llama-cli --single-turn` template-renders the prompt — a different input — and that trap manufactured a false root cause in the Zen wave era).
- **The near-tie band is the pass line, per record:** rank ≤ 2 with sub-logit gap = faithful. A product is FAITHFUL only when *every* record is in-band; one named failing record holds the DIVERGENT verdict. There is no partial credit by averaging.
- **Comparator artifacts are a verdict class:** template rendering, BOS rendering, backend mismatch, and prompt-boundary step-0 effects each fake divergences present in neither engine's maths. Check the comparator before suspecting the engine.
- **Token-exactness is not always a lawful demand:** ggml's own CPU vs CUDA IQ1_S kernels disagree with each other (S-029), so IQ-class parity is graded by coherence, never by token-exactness the reference itself cannot meet.
- **Trust only disk:** every number from files, git objects, or catalogue listings; every model sha256-pinned before running *(dossier: `aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`, §6.2)*.
- **Calibration integrity (v2, from the quantization canon; ledger #34):** never tune on the grading set — calibration data and grading data are disjoint by construction *(dossier: `papers/gptq.md`, §6b)*; prefer low-capacity calibration statistics and prove generalisation with AWQ's stress test — calibrate on one distribution, grade on another, report the cross-distribution delta (AWQ +0.5–0.6 vs GPTQ +2.3–4.9 perplexity, OPT-6.7B INT3): a quantizer whose faithfulness collapses off-distribution has memorised its calibration set *(dossier: `papers/awq.md`, §6b)*; account **true bitrate** including all per-group scales and zero-points, as GPTQ's grouped figures do (≈2.2 / ≈2.6 effective bits at nominal 2-bit); and grade instruction-style behaviour separately from perplexity, which did not predict instruction-tuned degradation in the canon's own experiments.

**The compression school as worked example.** Volmarr's 2026-11-14 commission — teach Aesir to *write* the packed forms it already reads — built `aesir quantize` (a GGUF writer plus streaming writer, Q8_0 and Q4_K/Q6_K packers; QC-0…QC-7, complete 2026-11-14). Its verdicts, read carefully, are the discipline in action *(dossier: `aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`, §5.1)*:

| Product | Verdict | Note |
|---|---|---|
| stories260K → Q8_0 | **FAITHFUL** | Cross-proven in llama.cpp |
| Gemma4-E2B → Q8_0 | **NOT FAITHFUL** | 98.73% in-band — one record short of the gate |
| stories → Q4_K_M | **DIVERGENT** | The flat-tiling lesson: block layout when packing matters as much as the quantisation maths |
| 27B → Q4_K_M | **DIVERGENT** | 99.49% in-band, one named record (p5 pos22); product is 16,223,236,576 B = 56.7% of Q8_0, admitted by all four readers |

The last row is the one to internalise: a 27B in ~16.2 GB instead of ~28.6 GB is a real residency prize, the file is admitted by every reader — and it is *still* DIVERGENT, because size and fidelity are separate verdicts and neither may stand in for the other. The response to the flat-tiling failure was a **searched-fit packer** that searches for a fitting layout rather than assuming one, with the one record it still misses (p5 pos22) named as its target for the next round. The ladder order is evidence, not preference: Q8_0 first (the faithful rung), then K-quants *(dossier: `aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`, §§5.2–5.3)*.

Two adjacent provisioning rules complete the chapter. **Residency is a weight choice as well as a KV choice:** Aesir's gate serve runs the 27B in a Q8_0-resident CPU configuration — weights held packed, dequantised as needed under the §5.3 rule — because the F16 path peaks at ~57 GB RSS (40B F16 estimated ~83 GB); the transferable part is the *measured-fit gate* that chooses the mode per model per machine and a health endpoint that reports which mode is actually live *(dossier: `aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`, §4.1)*. And **tiered weight residency** generalises placement: hot-first placement of weights across VRAM/RAM/storage tiers, priced by the roofline per tier, is the form in which PowerInfer's placement insight survives into the ledger (#29) after its sparsity engine is rejected (Chapter 9).

## Chapter 6 — Speculation and decoding: spending idle compute on latency

### 6.1 The verification trick

Decode at batch 1 is bandwidth-bound: the target model's weights stream past arithmetic units that are mostly idle (Chapter 1). Speculative decoding spends that idle compute on *verification*. A cheap **drafter** proposes γ tokens; the target model scores all γ+1 positions in **one** forward pass — costing roughly one ordinary decode step, because the pass is still one weight-streaming — and a verification rule accepts the longest prefix of drafts that the target itself could have produced, plus one corrected/bonus token. Each target pass therefore yields, on average, τ ≥ 1 tokens (the **acceptance length**) instead of exactly one. The economics are governed by two quantities: α, the per-token acceptance probability, and *c*, the drafter's cost as a fraction of the target's step. Larger drafters raise α but raise *c*; the founding papers show the optimum is a *small* drafter — on T5-XXL translation at temperature 0, a 77M draft (α 0.75) gave **3.4×**, a 250M draft (α 0.80) gave 2.8×, and an 800M draft (α 0.82) gave only 1.7× *(dossier: `papers/speculative-decoding.md`, §5)*. When α < *c* there may be no γ that helps at all — speculation has a starvation regime, and §6.4 prices it for a 6 GB card.

**Correctness is the family's founding virtue, and it is precise.** Leviathan et al. (ICML 2023) and Chen et al. (2023), arriving independently, both use **modified rejection sampling that provably preserves the target distribution**: speculative sampling is *lossless* in distribution — "same distribution, faster" — while remaining non-identical run-to-run under sampling, because exactness is distributional, not bitwise *(dossier: `papers/speculative-decoding.md`, §§3, 5–6)*. Any deployment that promises identical outputs while using a non-rejection acceptance rule is overclaiming; the corpus catches exactly this in Medusa (§6.3). Headline results, with context: Leviathan — **2–3×** on T5-XXL (11B), batch 1, single TPU-v4; Chen — **2–2.5×** on Chinchilla 70B distributed over 16 TPU-v4s, with a purpose-shaped 4B draft (1.8 ms/token against the target's 14.1 ms/token) *(dossier: `papers/speculative-decoding.md`, §5)*. Code accepts better than summarisation; sharper (lower-temperature) distributions accept better than diffuse ones. Both facts recur in every later paper in the family.

### 6.2 The family tree

The corpus's six speculation dossiers form one lineage, best read as successive answers to the question *where does the draft come from, and what does it cost to keep?*

- **Separate draft model** (the founding form, above). Best-understood economics; costs a second resident weight set and a second KV cache — the cost that dominates on small cards (§6.4).
- **Medusa — heads on the target itself.** Extra decoding heads read the target's own hidden state and predict several future positions at once, verified with tree attention. No draft model to house; residency is a few hundred MB of heads for a 7B-class target — the best in the trained family. Reported **2.2–2.8×** on Vicuna-7B (batch 1, HF baseline, MT-Bench), with roughly half the gain attributable to the tree-verification machinery rather than the heads. Two cautions are load-bearing: the default *typical acceptance* rule does **not** preserve the output distribution (only rejection sampling is lossless), and Medusa-2 fine-tunes the backbone, inheriting a fine-tune's regression burden *(dossier: `papers/medusa.md`, §§5–6)*.
- **EAGLE — draft at the feature level.** Instead of predicting tokens, EAGLE's single-layer drafter predicts the target's next *feature* (hidden state), conditioned on the shifted token sequence — removing the uncertainty that token-level sampling injects. Lossless **2.7–3.5×** on LLaMA2-Chat 70B (batch 1), acceptance length τ ≈ 3.2–4.5 at temperature 0, with 0-α of 0.74–0.79 against ~0.6 quoted for Medusa heads — the α gap at a drafter cost of one decoder layer per step is the paper's central claim *(dossier: `papers/eagle.md`, §5)*.
- **EAGLE-3 — scale the drafter's training.** Direct token prediction, multi-layer feature fusion, and training-time test (unrolled drafting during training) push reported speedups to **up to 6.5×** — specifically Vicuna-13B on HumanEval at temperature 0 (6.47×, τ 7.54); five-task means at temperature 0 run 4.12–5.51× across Vicuna-13B, LLaMA-3.1-8B, LLaMA-3.3-70B, and a distilled reasoning 8B, compressing to 3.45–4.65× at temperature 1. Summarisation is consistently weakest; drafter training data *chooses* where the speedup lives. The amortisation argument — train once, serve many queries — is strong for a hosted model and weak for a user's arbitrary local model, and each target still needs its own drafter, now with a larger training bill *(dossier: `papers/eagle-3.md`, §§5–6)*.
- **Lookahead — no drafter at all.** Jacobi iteration grows an n-gram pool from the target's own trajectory while verifying candidates in the same pass. Reported **1.8×** on MT-Bench (A100), τ ≈ 1.5–2.0 — the lowest acceptance in the wave — at a cost of **56–120× extra FLOPs** per step in its tuned A100 configurations, absorbed only while decoding is bandwidth-bound with compute to spare. Its own RTX 3090 result (~1.3× on MT-Bench) is the realistic reference class for consumer cards *(dossier: `papers/lookahead-decoding.md`, §§5–6)*.
- **Online speculative decoding — the drafter learns the workload.** The draft model retrains continually on live traffic, starting from rejection points, lifting acceptance by **+0.1–0.65** and dividing latency by **1.42–2.17** (final ICML figures) as it specialises to the actual query distribution *(dossier: `papers/online-speculative-decoding.md`, §5)*. The personal translation is the ledger's #32: session-granularity draft adaptation — per-persona drafters, specialised by exactly the repetitive distribution a single character chat presents. It ranks low not because it is weak but because it presupposes the whole speculation stack beneath it.

A seventh member costs a paragraph because it reframes the economics: **embedded multi-token-prediction (MTP) drafting**, where the model itself ships prediction heads as part of its checkpoint. When the heads exist, drafting costs no separate artefact at all — the ledger ranks it #28, conditional on the model shipping the heads, for the same reason EAGLE-3 is #27, conditional on a checkpoint existing for the exact model *(dossier: `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, #27, #28)*.

### 6.3 Where speculation pays at batch 1 — and the free floor

Speculation is a **latency technique for small batches**. As batch grows, verification stops being free — the target pass does real work for every drafted position of every sequence — and throughput-oriented batching wins instead *(dossier: `papers/speculative-decoding.md`, §6)*. Batch 1, bandwidth-bound, latency-is-the-product: that is precisely the personal runner's home turf, which is why this family matters here at all.

Within the family, the ledger's ordering inverts the papers' headline ordering, for one reason: **residency**. The free floor comes first. **N-gram / prompt-lookup speculation** drafts from the prompt and the generation itself — repeated phrases, quoted text, structured continuations — with *zero resident weights*. Its founding datum is already in Leviathan: even a trivial bigram drafter on T5-XXL translation reached α ≈ 0.20 and, because its *c* ≈ 0, a reported **1.25×** speedup. llama.cpp's shipped n-gram modes are mechanically Lookahead's pool-and-verify idea with table-lookup drafting instead of extra model FLOPs, and they dominate full Lookahead on a 6 GB card (ledger R5). Character chat is unusually repetitive — greetings, speech mannerisms, scene boilerplate, the user's own quoted lines — so the free floor is worth more here than benchmark suites suggest; how much more is unmeasured, and the ledger mandates instrumenting α on real transcripts before sizing any larger investment (Open Question 3) *(dossiers: `papers/speculative-decoding.md`, §5; `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, #15, R5, §6)*. The ledger ranks n-gram speculation as the default accelerator (#15), a small same-family draft model only where headroom survives the KV decision (#26, reporting 1.5–2.6× for well-matched pairs in community llama.cpp-line measurements), and EAGLE-3 heads only where a checkpoint exists for the exact model (#27).

### 6.4 The 6 GB starvation argument

**[Synthesis of `papers/speculative-decoding.md`, §7a, as ranked in `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, R3.]** On a 6 GB card running a 7–8B model at Q4, target weights already occupy roughly 4–5 GB, leaving ~1–2 GB for KV cache, compute buffers, and everything else. **(v2)** That remainder is the *headless-case* figure (§1.6): display-attached, the §4.2 restatement leaves a KV budget of ≈0–250 MiB for the flagship 8B pairing, so the starvation argument below is, on the machine as actually used, stronger than v1 stated — there is often no headroom for a drafter to compete *for* until the weight/KV decisions (#42/#3/#19) have created some. A draft model spends the scarcest resource on the card — VRAM — byte-for-byte against the KV cache a long chat needs. A default that evicts context to fund a drafter is therefore the wrong trade for this workload, however good its benchmark is: the context it evicts *is* the product (Chapter 4), while the speed it buys is a multiplier on a foundation that must first be correct and resident. Hence the ledger's exact disposition: draft-model speculation **rejected as the default, kept as a per-pair, telemetry-gated option** (#26) — do not re-litigate the default; litigate individual (target, draft) pairs with acceptance data. Medusa-style heads would resolve the residency tension and are deferred instead on artefact grounds (R4): heads must be trained per target on the unquantised backbone, community GGUFs ship without them, and the deployment must use rejection sampling — not the default typical acceptance — to keep the losslessness promise. Speculation also interacts with the runner's concurrency shape in a way the corpus could not verify: the `--parallel 1` restriction for speculation is asserted by multiple secondary guides but not stated in the llama.cpp server README the dossier read (Open Question 4) **[flagged as unverified in the source dossiers]**.

**A MoE note closes the chapter (v2).** Speculation's economics are computed in *active* bytes for MoE targets: verification streams the active experts, not the resident total, so the bandwidth ceiling speculation multiplies is the §4.8 active-bytes ceiling — while the drafter's residency still competes with the expert cache and the KV cache in the same pool. No dossier in the corpus measures speculation on a locally served MoE; the interaction is named here as arithmetic, not evidence, and no ledger entry depends on it **[synthesis, flagged as unmeasured]**.

## Chapter 7 — Kernels: attention without the traffic

### 7.1 FlashAttention: avoided traffic as the whole idea

Standard attention materialises the *N*×*N* score matrix in high-bandwidth memory: write it, read it back for softmax, read it again for the value product. At long context the matrix, not the maths, is the cost. FlashAttention (NeurIPS 2022) never materialises it. The kernel tiles the computation into SRAM-sized blocks and maintains the softmax with the **online-softmax** rescaling trick — running maximum and running sum, corrected as each block arrives — so exact attention is computed with HBM traffic linear in sequence length rather than quadratic in footprint. The backward pass recomputes attention from stored statistics instead of stored matrices, trading roughly 13% more FLOPs for roughly 9× less traffic (the bibliography's summary of the trade) — Chapter 1's exchange rate, accepted deliberately *(dossiers: `papers/flashattention.md`, §§3–4; `BIBLIOGRAPHY.md`)*.

Reported results, with context (all A100 hardware; attention microbenchmarks on a single A100-40GB, end-to-end training on 8× A100): up to **3× faster** than the standard PyTorch implementation across sequence lengths 128–2K in the sweep (the GPT-2 attention figure shown is 7.6×); memory footprint linear in sequence length and up to **20× more memory-efficient** than exact-attention baselines; end-to-end, up to 3× over the HuggingFace GPT-2 implementation and 1.7–1.8× over Megatron-LM at identical perplexity, and a 15% end-to-end BERT-large training speedup over the standing MLPerf record implementation. Runtime still grows quadratically with *N* — the FLOPs are unchanged in order; it is the constant, set by HBM traffic, that collapses *(dossier: `papers/flashattention.md`, §4)*. For a runner, the decode-side reading matters most: fused attention is also what *gates* other features — in Ollama, Flash Attention (automatic where backend and device support it, since late 2025) is the prerequisite for KV-cache quantisation *(dossier: `runners/ollama.md`, §4.5)* — and what *withholds* the attention scores that H2O/SnapKV-style policies consume (Chapter 4, Open Question 6). A kernel choice is never only a speed choice **[synthesis]**.

### 7.2 The lineage: better partitioning, then new hardware

**FlashAttention-2** (ICLR 2024) kept the algorithm and fixed the schedule: deferred rescaling (fewer non-matmul FLOPs — the paper's diagnosis being that non-matmul throughput sits ~16× below matmul throughput), sequence-parallel loop order, and split-Q warp partitioning to avoid shared-memory reductions. Reported on A100-80GB SXM4: **1.7–3.0× faster** than FlashAttention across the sweep, reaching up to **230 TFLOPs/s and 73% of theoretical peak** in the forward pass (against FlashAttention's 25–40%) — "getting close to the efficiency of GEMM operations". End-to-end training gains are honestly smaller (up to 1.3× over FlashAttention), because attention is only part of a training step *(dossier: `papers/flashattention-2.md`, §4)*.

**FlashAttention-3** (NeurIPS 2024) spends Hopper hardware: warp-specialised asynchronous execution (TMA, warpgroup WGMMA, dynamic register reallocation, thread-block clusters) plus FP8 with block quantisation and incoherent processing. Reported on H100-80GB SXM5: FP16 forward **1.5–2.0× faster** than FlashAttention-2, up to **740 TFLOPs/s (75% of peak)**; FP8 forward close to **1.2 PFLOPs/s**; FP8 error 2.6× lower than a per-tensor-scaled baseline on the paper's synthetic outlier distribution, with the ablation showing the incoherent rotation doing nearly all of the accuracy work *(dossier: `papers/flashattention-3.md`, §4)*.

**What survives on Turing** — the corpus asks this of every kernel paper, because the build target is an RTX 2060 Max-Q (compute capability 7.5). FlashAttention-2's three mechanisms are scheduling ideas, not new instructions: they require nothing Turing lacks, and the non-matmul diagnosis holds qualitatively there too — if anything more strongly, since Turing's scalar/vector units are proportionally weaker beside its tensor cores. FlashAttention-3 is the opposite case, stated plainly in its dossier: every mechanism names a Hopper feature Turing does not have, **no FA3 kernel runs on Turing**, and what transfers is precision lessons only (block quantisation, incoherent processing as error control) *(dossiers: `papers/flashattention-2.md`, §4 caveat; `papers/flashattention-3.md`, §4 caveat)*. The general law: **quote kernel results with their silicon, and port mechanisms, not numbers** **[synthesis]**.

### 7.3 FlashInfer: the lineage becomes infrastructure

FlashInfer (MLSys 2025) is what happens when the attention kernel stops being a paper artefact and becomes serving infrastructure: a unified block-sparse KV layout shared across prefill, decode, and prefix-cached serving; JIT-compiled kernel templates spanning **Turing (sm75) to Hopper**; and a plan/run scheduler that load-balances variable-length batches — the property that lets it beat stock FlashAttention kernels under uniform and skewed length distributions, whose tile sizes suit prefill rather than query-length-1 decode. Reported end-to-end (SGLang with FlashInfer vs SGLang with its Triton backend; Llama 3.1 8B on 1× H100 and 70B on 4× H100; ShareGPT and synthetic variable-length workloads at rates holding P99 TTFT under 200 ms): **29–69% inter-token-latency reduction**, 28–30% for long-context inference, 13–17% for parallel generation; fusing RoPE into the attention kernel alone yielded 1.6–3.7× higher bandwidth utilisation at kernel level in the StreamingLLM case. Adoption is itself a result: FlashInfer ships inside **SGLang, vLLM, and MLC-Engine** *(dossier: `papers/flashinfer.md`, §4)*. For the personal builder the lesson is architectural: the kernel layer is increasingly a *library* decision (adopt a maintained substrate with your block layout as its contract) rather than a from-scratch authorship decision — the same conclusion the Aesir dossier reaches for Go from the opposite direction ("import the profiling discipline and the decode-width lesson, not the kernels") *(dossier: `aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`, §8)*.

### 7.4 Kernel/format co-design, restated as kernel law

Chapter 5 covered co-design from the format side; from the kernel side the corpus adds two exhibits. **ExLlamaV2** exists because its author took ownership of both sides: EXL2's mixed-precision layouts exist in exactly the shapes its CUDA kernels consume fastest at batch 1, and its later paged, dynamically-batched generator reuses prefix pages across jobs in the same kernel-conscious way *(dossier: `runners/exllamav2.md`)*. **ggml** is the portable counterpart: a define-then-execute graph library with pluggable backends (CPU, CUDA, ROCm, Vulkan, Metal, SYCL) and **zero allocations in the hot path**, so the same block formats dequantise cheaply everywhere *(dossier: `runners/llamacpp-ggml.md`, §1)*. Between them sits the sparsity experiment the corpus prices and mostly refuses: **PowerInfer** skips inactive FFN neurons (a skipped neuron skips both a row-read and a column-read — exactly the unit of batch-1 decode traffic), placing hot neurons on GPU and cold on CPU. Its headlines are real and narrowly scoped: up to **11.69×** (FP16, Falcon(ReLU)-40B) and up to 8× (INT4) belong to ReLU-family models with 90–98% exact-zero sparsity; the SwiGLU control outcome in the same dossier is **~1.5×**, because modern chat models are SwiGLU — almost no exact zeros, 40–53% *thresholded* sparsity under a different definition that must not be merged with the ReLU figures *(dossier: `runners/powerinfer.md`, §§2, 6)*. What survives into the programme is placement policy and a free sparsity approximation (gate-first thresholding, ledger #30), not the sparse-hybrid engine (ledger R9, R1).

Finally, the allocator verdict that disciplines all kernel-adjacent ambition: decode is bandwidth-bound, **not malloc-bound**. Our own MimirAlloc — correct, fuzzed, soaked, 117/117 slices on two campaigns (Pi final gate 4,590 run / 0 failed; Z13 final gate 4,988 passed / 0 failed / 77 skipped) — lost to glibc on every general workload on the Pi (**5.4×–65.2×** in the K-002 baseline) and on most core shapes on the Z13, including **all three KV-cache-shaped probes (Z-115, no adoption)**. Its genuine measured virtue was footprint: returning to within **128 KiB** of baseline after free where glibc retained 1,232 KiB — a server-hygiene property to *measure*, not a reason to adopt an allocator. The KV pool is an arena with a free list (Chapter 4); a general allocator serves the structures around it (ledger R10) *(dossiers: `memory/mimiralloc-our-own-proven-work.md`, §§3–4; `memory/allocator-lineages.md`, §§1, 3.4 — whose lineage walk reaches the matching conclusion from theory: KV block pools *are* slab allocators, and a runner's four memory shapes — huge long-lived weights, identically-shaped KV blocks, per-step arena scratch, general server allocations — each have a different right allocator)*.

## Chapter 8 — The personal runner: a different species

### 8.1 The thesis

Everything before this chapter describes techniques whose value depends on the objective function they serve. The datacentre objective — aggregate throughput across many strangers, under latency SLOs, on abundant hardware — produced a magnificent literature, most of which this book has mined. The personal objective is different in kind: **one person's continuity with one model, on one modest machine, over months of conversation.** The corpus's distinctive contribution is to work out what that objective does to every subsystem. This chapter states the resulting design law, exhibits the failure that motivates it, and sorts the datacentre inheritance into *import* and *refuse*.

### 8.2 The failure that defines the species: silent beheading

Ollama's overflow behaviour, characterised from Go source at v0.33.1, is the corpus's most load-bearing finding, and it is stated here in full *(dossier: `runners/ollama.md`, §4.3)*. When a rendered prompt exceeds the window, Ollama trims in **two successive layers with opposite treatment of the system prompt**:

- **Layer 1 — message-level truncation (Go server).** Whole messages are dropped from the front of the conversation — but every system-role message in the dropped span is collected and re-prepended, so the persona survives by design, and the final message is always kept. The event is logged only at debug level, below the default: on a stock server it leaves no trace. It can be disabled with `truncate: false` on the native API.
- **Layer 2 — token-level context shift (runner boundary).** If the prompt *still* exceeds `num_ctx − 1`, a context shift discards a contiguous block from the **middle** of the token stream, preserving only the first `num_keep` tokens — default **4** (effectively 5 with BOS). At this layer the system prompt has no protection at all: beyond its first few tokens it is discarded with everything else in the excised block. Worse, the first shift frees roughly **half the window**, not merely the overflow: at `num_ctx` 4096, a 12,000-token prompt reaches the model as its first ~5 tokens plus its last ~2,046 — the middle ~9,950 tokens vanish (≈16,387 survive a first shift at 32K). Layer 2 logs at warning level, but **no field, header, or finish-reason in any API response reports either layer**: the client receives a fluent HTTP 200 answer computed from a prompt it did not send. `shift: false` converts layer 2 into a loud HTTP 400 — on the native API only; neither knob exists on the OpenAI-compatible schema, whose clients cannot even ask for the loud failure.

Composed, the effective contract is "your system prompt survives until it suddenly, catastrophically doesn't" — for a character-driven workload, the single most important behaviour to redesign. Around it cluster the supporting findings: context is per-runner, not per-conversation (§4.2 of the dossier); the window default itself is three-way inconsistent across Ollama's own surfaces (Modelfile reference 2048; FAQ 4096; shipping code since February 2026 tiered by detected VRAM — 262,144 at ≥47 GiB, 32,768 at ≥23 GiB, **4,096 below 23 GiB**, so the 6 GB card lands in the 4K tier — announced once in a startup log line and reported in no API response); load-time options are part of runner identity, so two clients alternating `num_ctx` values force an unload–reload cycle on every alternation (the reload trap); keep-alive (default 5 minutes) doubles, undocumented, as the prefix-cache and session-continuity lifetime; and the server surface's security posture is "loopback is the authentication" *(dossier: `runners/ollama.md`, §§3.4, 4.1–4.4, 5.2, 7.5)*.

None of this makes Ollama a bad program — its packaging, library, and ergonomics are why local inference has a mass audience at all *(dossier: `runners/ollama.md`, §8)*. It makes Ollama a perfect specimen of the species error: a runner designed around *requests* meeting a workload made of *relationships*.

### 8.3 The laws of the personal runner

The corpus's answer, assembled from the Aesir campaign (which built these properties under test) and the ledger (which ranks them), is six laws. **[Synthesis in the framing; every mechanism cited is a dossier's.]**

1. **The anchor law.** The character prompt must be *unpushable* — incapable of being displaced by history growth. The corpus knows two complementary mechanisms. *Pinning:* a non-truncatable anchor segment at the head, protected by the eviction composition (Chapter 4) — the same tokens StreamingLLM pins for stability turn out to be the tokens chat must pin for identity, a fortunate convergence *(dossiers: `papers/streamingllm.md`, §7a; `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, #2)*. *Composition:* Aesir's post-history anchor slot (HS-5) renders the anchor **after** the history at prompt-assembly time, never writes it into stored history or the committed token stream, and so places it nearest generation and entirely outside the window — immune to truncation by construction, at the honest cost of re-evaluating its tokens every turn *(dossier: `aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`, §2.3; ledger #7)*. The same pattern was set in Voxta the same morning (2026-11-14) — independent convergence on the shape.
2. **Resident sessions.** A session is a durable object owning its KV, with a **token-level committed watermark**: generated tokens are committed the moment they are produced, so a resend straddling the prompt/generation seam reuses everything up to the watermark. Aesir's HS-6 eliminated a measured ~6-token seam loss "by construction"; its HS-3 bench (small scale, stated as such) reused 46 tokens on turn 2 at 610 ms against turn 1's 825 ms — against the baseline disease of 14,712 tokens re-evaluated in 52.1 s per turn *(dossier: `aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`, §§2.1–2.2; ledger #1)*. Model lifetime and session lifetime are decoupled: swapping models must not destroy conversations, and idle sessions spill rather than die (ledger #11).
3. **Byte-stable rendering.** Prefix reuse (§4.4) hits only if the rendered prefix is identical, so prompt rendering is a caching contract: stable content first, volatile content last; memory injection in **fixed order, whole blocks only, under a token budget**, with per-turn reuse counters logged — Aesir HS-7's contract, drawn from Voxta's stable-order rendering and whole-entry admission practice *(dossier: `aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`, §3.3; ledger #13, #14)*. A timestamp injected near the head silently taxes every turn that follows it.
4. **Loud disclosure.** Every truncation, eviction, reuse count, and effective-precision decision is observable: in the response, the session info, or at minimum a default-level log — never debug-only, never absent. Disclosure converts silent integrity failures into observable events, and it is the observability every later lossy technique is graded through; shipping anything lossy before it exists means shipping it unmeasured in production (ledger #6, scheduled first with #2 for exactly this reason). The serve should also *advertise* its memory properties truthfully — Aesir's serve reports `exact_prefix_reuse`, `session_residency`, and `memory_injection` — so clients rely on tested properties instead of assumptions *(dossiers: `aesir/aesir-ideas-for-ollama.md`, §2.4; `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, #6, §4)*.
5. **Honest memory above the cache.** Between sessions, memory is not a cache but **a database with an editor** — and most failures live in the editor. The agent-memory dossier's two-layer truth governs: extraction writes can hallucinate, facts rot when superseded values keep being served, and **append-only memory plus retrieval guarantees eventual contradiction**, because retrieval ranks by similarity and recency, not truth. The honest store is versioned, provenance-carrying, and non-destructive: Zep/Graphiti's bi-temporal design *invalidates* the old edge rather than deleting it — the only fully non-destructive supersession design in the surveyed set. Benchmarks confirm the stakes: LongMemEval finds roughly a **30% accuracy drop** under sustained interaction, worst on knowledge updates; LoCoMo finds systems lagging humans worst on temporal reasoning. MemGPT/Letta contributes the OS metaphor made precise (the model pages between main context and recall/archival stores) and self-editing memory blocks — the direct ancestor of the anchor law — while Generative Agents contributes the retrieval score (recency × importance × relevance) and reflection, with its honesty limits named *(dossier: `memory/agent-memory-architectures.md`, §§1–6)*. The runner's boundary follows: the runner guarantees residency, anchoring, and disclosure *in context*; the store, its editor, and its supersession contract are a separate layer with a separate honesty burden, and neither may impersonate the other (ledger R13 is this law applied to marketing copy).
6. **Budgeting is the safety system — doubly so on unified memory.** On a discrete card, exhaustion fails locally. On unified memory (Apple Silicon; AMD Strix Halo), CPU, GPU, NPU, compositor, and browser share one pool, the GPU's share is typically pinned, and exhaustion is a **system-wide OOM that takes the desktop with it** — proven on purpleraider on 2026-11-14, in a cascade this fleet helped cause by launching a ~24 GB test serve minutes after a reboot. The dossier's sentence stands as law: *on unified memory, capacity is abundant, bandwidth is the price, and the budget is the safety system; a runner that does not budget explicitly has not removed the VRAM wall — it has moved the wall to the whole machine.* The concrete physics: Strix Halo's 256-bit LPDDR5X-8000 gives 256 GB/s theoretical, ~215 GB/s as reported (no AMD primary sustained figure found; no STREAM-class measurement in our records — still owed); the GPU-mappable pool (GTT) defaults to ~50% of RAM and was deliberately capped on purpleraider at **100 GiB = 26,214,400 pages** (the pages-vs-bytes unit being the most error-prone in the area) *(dossier: `memory/unified-memory.md`, §§1–3)*. MLX is the design exemplar from the other vendor: arrays with no device residence, lazy evaluation letting the runtime fuse and schedule across processors without copies — and its retention lesson ("keeping a cache is cheaper than recomputing it") must be *translated*, not transferred, to discrete cards, where resident KV is priced against the weights themselves (ledger R14) *(dossiers: `runners/mlx.md`, §2; `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, R14)*.

**Corroboration (v2) — the persona canon is now two-product practice.** V1 derived the laws above from Ollama's failure and Aesir's campaign. The dossiers banked since document two independent shipping products — KoboldCpp and Sonar (Aphrodite) — built for exactly this workload, and they converge on the same canon: an always-injected head block (Memory) as a first-class object (Law 1's pinning, shipped); a composed near-tail steering note with user-settable injection depth (KoboldCpp's Author's Note — Law 1's composition form, shipped; ledger #7); keyword-triggered, budget-capped lore injection (World Info — Law 3's budgeted whole-block injection, shipped; ledger #14). The ledger records this as corroboration for #2, #7 and #14 in its second cut. The canon's one genuinely new surface is the **sampler pipeline** — temperature, top-*k*/*p*, min-*p*, penalties, DRY-class anti-repetition, XTC, mirostat, with sampler *order* explicit and presets shareable as artefacts — which v1's ledger missed entirely and v2 ranks as idea #37: for long-form character chat the sampler stack is the difference between a persona that stays in voice for 200 turns and one that loops by turn 30 (the Sonar dossier's phrasing; grade B, qualitative). It enters at rank 15, not the top five, for the reason §9.1 gives: a sampler multiplies nothing until sessions, residency and an intact persona exist to sample for *(dossiers: `runners/koboldcpp.md`, §9; `runners/aphrodite.md`, §9; `synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md`, #37, §4)*.

### 8.4 Import and refuse: the sorted inheritance

**Import from the datacentre** (each priced for one user in Chapters 3–4): paged/block KV allocation; hash-chained prefix retention with copy-on-write branching; the iteration-level step loop and the token budget; chunked prefill as a stall bound; cache-aware ordering for bursts; measured placement — Ollama's own September 2025 rework replaced heuristic memory estimation with pre-load measurement for its then-new engine, and its published example (12B Gemma-family at 128K on a 24 GB RTX 4090) gained **~64% generation speed (52.02 → 85.54 tok/s)** from packing alone, layers on GPU rising 48/49 → 49/49. How completely that scheduler survived the 2026 return to `llama-server` is **not fully verified** from public sources — a fork-point check (ledger Open Question 10) **[flagged uncertainty]** *(dossier: `runners/ollama.md`, §5.4)*.

**Refuse:** multi-tenant fairness machinery and preemption-by-recompute as doctrine (Chapter 3); engine-build rigidity (Chapter 2, NVIDIA's retreat as evidence); datacentre defaults worn as universals (0.9-of-VRAM KV fractions, 32K windows announced without their arithmetic); unauthenticated surfaces beyond loopback (llama-server's session surface must not be exposed as the product's own — ledger R12); and any "infinite memory" claim for a windowing policy. **(v2)** The second cut adds three priced refusals to this list: per-configuration compiled engine artefacts (R15, rejected on NVIDIA's own retreat); an NPU compiled-blob path as a *product dependency* (R16, deferred — closed kernels, curated catalogue, exclusive lock, unresolved licence conflict; Chapter 10 states the corrected two-paths picture); and append-only memory stores (R17, rejected on the supersession theorem of Law 5). The refusal list is not Luddism: every refused item is refused *for this objective function*, with its reopening condition named in Chapter 9.

## Chapter 9 — The ranked programme: an engineering sequence

This chapter restates the idea ledger's top tier (`synthesis/IDEA-LEDGER.md` — **second cut (v2): 42 ranked ideas, 17 rejected/deferred entries, 19 open questions**) as a *sequence with dependencies*, for the ledger's fixed target: the Ollama Memory Forge build on purplelongship — RTX 2060 Max-Q, 6 GB VRAM, single user, long character chats — under Volmarr's laws for that build (memory efficiency, speed, accuracy through memory use, robustness; anchor unpushable; resident context never reprocessed). The ledger scores every idea on expected gain *for that workload*, cost, risk (correctness first — silent quality loss outranks a crash, because a crash is at least honest), and evidence grade (A: peer-reviewed measurement or our own campaign records, with hardware stated; B: project-reported; C: reasoned inference — the gain, not just the mechanism, unmeasured on anything like the target). **(v2 mechanics)** Idea numbers are now permanent — in v1, number equalled rank; from the second cut an idea keeps its number for life and *rank* is table position, with a "Rank movement (v2)" audit line per entry. Ranks 1–14 are unchanged; every downward shift below rank 14 is displacement by the new insertions and one evidence-driven promotion, not a fresh negative judgement, unless an entry's own text says otherwise. This chapter is a narrative of the ledger, not a copy of it; the ledger remains the ranked record.

### 9.1 The foundation five, in dependency order

The top five are not the five largest wins; they are the five that make the rest possible, and they are scheduled as two parallel threads plus a substrate.

**First, together, as policy: the pinned anchor (#2) with loud disclosure (#6).** Neither needs new kernels, an allocator, or a cache. Together they convert the build's founding law from an intention into a tested property, and install the observability — reuse counts, eviction reports, effective-precision reporting — through which every later slice is graded. This pair is the only top-five work that is pure win with no quality trade-off to validate. Cost: days, not slices.

**The object everything attaches to: resident sessions (#1).** Prefix caching, persistence, stable-order injection, the post-history anchor, and per-persona drafters are all, structurally, properties of a session object; building any of them against the stateless API first means building it twice. Token-level residency (the committed-token watermark, Chapter 8) must be in the session's first design, because the seam loss it eliminates is invisible until real resends hit it. Sequence inside the slice: session object with watermark first, composition (anchor segments, injection) second, retention third.

**The arithmetic that decides what sessions can hold: q8_0 KV by default (#3).** A policy change over existing cache types, so it can land in parallel with the session work — but it must land *before* eviction scoring (#9 in the ledger's numbering; Chapter 4's composition) and before the persistence format freezes, because both inherit the cache precision: scoring operates on the cache this entry sizes, and a saved session is valid only for the precision it was saved at. Its worked case is the build's founding arithmetic, **restated in v2** (Chapter 4, §4.2): *headless*, FP16 does not meet the 8K minimum on this card (4,912 tokens) and q8_0 does (9,246); *display-attached* — the laptop as actually used — no cache precision reaches 8K for the flagship model, so the shipped configuration should be expected to pair this entry with #42 (a smaller, lineage-chosen weight quant) or #19/#20 (sub-8-bit KV), decided by #12's budget, per model. The decomposition *strengthens* the entry rather than moving it: on the desk configuration, q8_0-by-default is the difference between a product and an allocation failure. Validate per model family through the grading harness (#34) as it lands, not after.

**The top five stand — the v2 re-derivation, in one paragraph.** Each new dossier was checked as a potential challenger (ledger §4). The persona canon corroborates the five's workload premise and its one new surface (#37) enters at rank 15, below them, because it presupposes them. The reserve decomposition strengthens #3 specifically (above). TensorRT-LLM's retreat validates #4/#12's shared framing — planning at load time, inside the runner, over a portable artefact — by removing its only serious rival: NVIDIA deleted the build-time alternative itself. The strongest genuinely new candidate, MoE expert caching (#38), enters at rank 31, not the five: it serves a model class outside the target's primary catalogue and presupposes the arena and residency machinery the five build. Nothing banked since v1 reduces any top-five entry's gain, cost or risk by enough to reorder them; the honest change is in #3's numbers, not in the set or its order.

**The substrate: block allocation in one pre-sized arena (#4).** Prefix retention, block-granular eviction, spill and persistence, per-layer quotas, and hybrid snapshots are all operations *on blocks*. Without the arena + block table + reference counts, each invents its own allocation shape and the campaign pays five integrations instead of one. This is the deepest surgery in the five and the one to staff most carefully; its acceptance test is memory honesty, not speed.

**The retention layer: hash-chained/radix prefix cache, persona trunk plus branches (#5).** Last of the five by dependency, first by felt impact. It needs #4's blocks to share, #1's sessions to own the sharing, byte-stable rendering (#13) to hit, and #3's precision to afford keeping prefixes resident. Once it lands, the workload's economics invert: later turns cost the new turn only, swipes cost a suffix, and tiering has something worth tiering. The two dependencies to hold as law: *prefix caching before tiering* (tiering is a policy over retained prefix objects; earlier scheduling produces a persistence format with nothing stable to persist) and *q8_0 before eviction scoring* (do not tune a scoring policy against a cache whose size is about to halve).

### 9.2 The second movement: living within the budget

With the foundation laid, the programme spends the budget it can now count. ### 10.2 Frontiers: where a master watches next

Beyond the settleable questions, the corpus flags seven moving frontiers — directions where the ground itself is shifting, and where today's design must leave room. (V1 flagged four; v2 adds MoE serving, RoPE effective length, and the compiled-blob/NPU frontier, and carries CXL inside the memory-tier discussion.)

**MLA-class architectures.** GQA compressed the cache by sharing; MLA compresses it by *representation* — a learned latent smaller than even MQA's footprint with MHA-beating quality in ablation (Chapter 4). If latent-attention architectures proliferate beyond DeepSeek's line, runners gain a new cache kind (latent + decoupled RoPE key), a new `kv_rate` regime an order of magnitude kinder, and a new kernel contract (reconstruct-on-the-fly vs materialise). A runner whose KV manager assumes per-head K/V tensors will feel this first. Watch: conversions and uptraining recipes from existing checkpoints (absent from the MLA paper itself), and latent-cache support landing in the ggml ecosystem.

**SSM hybrids as the default shape.** The qwen35 record — most layers recurrent, attention at intervals — may be a preview of the general case rather than an exception: fixed-size state for most depth, paged KV for the rest, snapshots as the reuse primitive, per-component budgeting as the fit equation (Chapter 4, §4.6). SGLang's unified-tree work is the fleet moving the same way. The open design space is snapshot economics: at ~158.9 MB per 27B-scale snapshot, snapshot placement, bounding, and tiering become a memory discipline of their own, and branch-heavy interfaces must be priced in state copies, not block copies.

**UMA budgeting as a first-class runner feature.** As unified-memory machines (Strix Halo today, Apple Silicon throughout) become normal development hardware, the runner's budget stops being a fit check and becomes the machine's safety system (Chapter 8, law 6): explicit pool caps, declared fences, shedding orders, and co-tenancy awareness — including the standing rule this fleet learned on 2026-11-14 never to start massive work on a just-booted or actively-used machine. The corpus still owes a STREAM-class bandwidth measurement for purpleraider; without it, every UMA roofline in this book is drawn from reported, not measured, bandwidth **[flagged gap]**.

**MoE serving on personal hardware (v2).** §4.8's expert cache is today a traced policy over a static incumbent (`--cpu-moe` + a size knob). The frontier is whether request-level tracing/matching becomes standard local practice, how expert caches and KV caches are arbitrated as one pool when long context squeezes both (MoE-Infinity's own long-context results show the buffer shrinking under KV growth until the system falls back to on-demand fetching), and whether fine-grained MoE architectures — whose per-request working sets are far smaller fractions of their totals — make the 6 GB MoE case routine rather than heroic. Watch: expert-cache hit-rate instrumentation shipping in local runners, and GGUF MoE placement policy moving from placement to prediction.

**RoPE effective length (v2).** §4.7's three numbers — trained / scaled / allocated — will increasingly need a fourth: *effective*, the length at which the model still passes multi-needle and aggregation probes, which later benchmarks showed diverging from claimed length well before perplexity cliffs. YaRN and LongRoPE give the extension mechanisms; the open work is measurement discipline at the runner level — needle probes at a session's actual lengths, run at admission, reporting the effective length beside the allocated one. A runner that probes can be honest in a way a config number never can *(dossiers: `papers/yarn.md`, §5; `papers/longrope.md`, §§5–6)*.

**The compiled-blob regime and the NPU — with the two-paths correction (v2).** Three instances in the corpus share one shape *(dossier: `memory/npu-memory-and-dataflows.md`, §6)*: weights frozen at compile time into a device-specific artefact (Hailo's compiled format, OpenVINO's NPU blob, QNN's context binary); activations bounded by static shapes (dynamic dimensions are not an inconvenience to this regime but a contradiction of it); runtime allocation nearly disappearing into the compiler's memory planner; and fallback as graph surgery, not slowdown — an unsupported operator *amputates* the NPU path. The AMD case must be stated correctly, because secondary coverage — including some of AMD's own — conflates it, and **this volume explicitly retires the conflated single "hybrid NPU" story**: Lemonade contains **two distinct NPU paths** *(dossier: `runners/lemonade.md`, §3)*. *Path one, FastFlowLM:* the whole model — prefill, every layer, decode, head — runs on the XDNA2 NPU alone, from Q4NX packages whose kernel binaries (xclbins) are closed, shipped only for officially supported models, holding an exclusive NPU lock while loaded; context limits are per-model catalogue properties (32K–256K maxima across the catalogue, not a runtime guarantee). *Path two, Ryzen AI LLM:* ONNX Runtime GenAI on AMD's Ryzen AI stack, Windows-only, version-locked to the Ryzen AI release, with three modes — NPU-only, GPU, and the **hybrid mode: dynamic NPU+iGPU partitioning** aimed at best TTFT and tokens/sec *for that mode's class* (one hands-on measurement: 0.44 s TTFT, 24.0 tok/s on an 8B hybrid build — hybrid buys startup latency, not throughput). The sentence "prefill runs on the NPU, decode on the GPU" is true only as a loose description of path two's hybrid mode, **false** of FastFlowLM, and misleading of Lemonade itself, which splits nothing — it *routes whole models* to engines. A runner copying "the hybrid trick" would be copying a vendor execution provider's compiled-artefact machinery, not a scheduling policy a router can adopt for arbitrary GGUFs. The ledger's disposition stands (R16, deferred): the NPU's measured value on Z13-class hardware — ~2.4× slower sustained decode, ~2.3× faster first token, nearly contention-free beside the GPU (the one end-to-end comparison found; §6.3 of the Lemonade dossier) — is a *second worker* for background-class tasks, not a faster engine. Watch: the licence conflict's resolution (Q16), Linux FastFlowLM maturing from source-built driver stacks to turnkey, and whether any NPU path escapes the curated-catalogue regime.

**Tiered memory beyond the box: DRAM branches and CXL (v2).** The datasheet layer (§1.6) is not static: LPDDR generations move unified-machine ceilings, GDDR moves discrete cards, and CXL promises DRAM at the end of a fabric — capacity expansion first, pooling and sharing in its later generations, priced in added latency and finite link bandwidth *(dossier: `memory/memory-technologies-dram-cxl.md`, §7)*. For a personal runner the plausible first use is unglamorous and valuable: a CXL tier as the spill/persistence tier of §4.4 — colder than host DRAM, far warmer than disk — *if* the latency price lands where the published experiment direction suggests. Status, stated honestly in the dossier as of late 2026: a frontier to watch with the three-variable discipline in hand, not a design input for the current build.

**Agent-memory honesty.** The layer above the runner is where the field's hardest unsolved problem now sits: not capacity, but *truth over time* — extraction that invents, supersession that never happens, retrieval that faithfully serves rot (Chapter 8, law 5). The designs to watch are the editorial ones: bi-temporal invalidation with provenance (Zep/Graphiti), sleep-time consolidation (Letta's production direction), and benchmarks that grade knowledge *updates* and temporal reasoning rather than static recall (LongMemEval, LoCoMo). A personal runner that solves residency and anchoring in context while its persistent store quietly contradicts itself has moved the lie, not removed it. The corpus's position — runner guarantees in context, store guarantees across time, neither impersonating the other — is the frame in which to judge whatever arrives next.

### A. The measurement law, named: this book's citation constitution

Chapters 1, 9 and the honesty apparatus below have *practised* a discipline since the first draft — no number without its context, magnitudes as-reported, synthesis marked. Batch 4 gives the discipline its name, its source, and its teeth, and this volume now adopts it explicitly: **the measurement law — a number is only as real as its disclosed conditions** (`papers/mlperf-inference.md` §§3, 6; ledger #44, where it is a build requirement).

- **Scenario, phase, model+quant in bytes, workload profile, machine state, n and spread, faithfulness grade** — the minimal disclosure set (MLPerf dossier §6b). A blended tok/s is a scenario error: prefill and decode are different metrics, as MLPerf's scenarios are different metrics. **There is no summary score** — not in MLPerf, and not here: per-phase, per-scenario, per-quality-band results, or nothing.
- **No percentile without its n.** MLPerf derives its query counts from the percentile claimed: 270,336 inferences stand behind a 99th-percentile claim at 99% confidence, as-reported. A p99 latency claim on twenty requests is the local form of the fraud that rule exists to prevent. State n, or report a mean and call it one.
- **Predictions carry their validation error or the label "unvalidated"** — the Vidur half (`papers/vidur.md` §§3.4, 4, 6). A simulated or modelled number names what was profiled vs interpolated and its ground-truth anchor. Vidur's fidelity was *earned*: P95 latency predicted with up to 3.33% error on static workloads, <5% dynamic at 85% of capacity, degrading to 12.65% worst at 95% of capacity (LLaMA2-7B), all as-reported against a real vLLM at stated operating points. Error grows at the tipping point, so validation belongs at the edge — full context, spill onset, concurrent prefill — not in the comfortable middle. An unvalidated local estimate borrows none of those figures.
- **The optimum is a (model, workload) pair, never a model property.** Vidur's search found the same model's best batch at 256 on one trace and 64 on another, with a 2× penalty for crossing them, as-reported. Tuning records in this programme are keyed by workload shape from this cut. Chapter 3's scheduling numbers and Chapter 9's sequence should be read under this rule: a configuration is optimal *for a workload*, or the claim is incomplete in a named way.
- FastGen's "effective throughput" ethic (`runners/deepspeed-fastgen.md` §7.4 — count only requests meeting a stated SLA pair) is adopted as the local benchmark form of the same constitution, with its defence: the SLA pair is fixed and versioned in the test suite, never tuned to a result.

### B. Energy is a first-class metric

Chapter 1's wall is a bandwidth wall; batch 4 adds the second ledger every speed claim must now balance: **energy** (ledger #45; `papers/words-to-watts.md`, `papers/power-hungry-processing.md`).

- **Report tokens/joule beside tok/s.** The rankings genuinely disagree — in Samsi et al., A100 beat V100 on throughput for every model tested and drew more watts doing it, and raising shard count monotonically worsened joules/token, all as-reported — so a speed claim is never an efficiency claim without energy. The energy line is the disclosure set's eighth line: total energy and mean power with the instrument named, joules per output token *and* joules per response at a stated prompt/output profile (output length drives energy, so per-token figures compare only at matched profiles), scope labelled GPU-only vs whole-system and never pooled, idle/baseline draw stated or labelled unmeasured.
- **The power-cap knee, as-reported, re-measure locally.** Samsi et al. (LLaMA 65B, GSM8K, batch 64, 4× A100 80GB, GPU energy via DCGM): capping 250 W → 175 W cost **+6.7% inference time for −23.21% total energy** — ~7% slower for ~20% less energy — and the exchange deteriorates past the knee (at 150 W the time penalty roughly triples for ~1.5× the saving). An efficiency mode (lower power limit + output-length discipline + smallest adequate model) is therefore a real feature with a measured exchange rate *on other hardware*; the local knee on the RTX 2060 Max-Q — already a capped part — is open question Q20, and no local rate is quoted until it lands. Absolute magnitudes (e.g. ~3–4 J/token for sharded 65B) do not transfer to a quantised small model on one laptop GPU, and this volume will not carry them as expectations.
- **Model choice is an energy decision before hardware choice is** (Luccioni et al.: task-specific models beating multi-purpose ones by orders of magnitude on discriminative tasks, size-controlled, as-reported). On this fleet energy is heat in a chassis and charge in a battery, and at personal scale idle/baseline draw — a resident model, an awake GPU, divided by very few tokens — is most of the ledger. Chapter 8's personal-runner laws should be read with that term included.

### C. Chunked prefill, completed: the fuse half (Chapter 3, §3.3)

Section 3.3 as written carries Sarathi-Serve's half of the mechanism: split a long prefill into budget-sized chunks so decodes are never stalled by one prompt (the *split* half). Batch 4's second source completes it (`runners/deepspeed-fastgen.md` §§2, 7; ledger #18, v3 refinement). SplitFuse's *fuse* half composes **any mixture** — several short prompts, parts of prompts, and all pending decodes — to fill the token budget **exactly**: a token budget is a target to compose to, not a cap to fit under, because under-filled passes waste the weight read as surely as over-long passes stall decodes. The budget itself is **chosen at the knee**: profile throughput vs tokens-per-pass per (model, device), set the budget at the smallest pass size reaching near-peak throughput, bounded by the promised inter-token latency. FastGen publishes the method and **no numeric budget** — there is no SplitFuse number to copy; Sarathi's 512–2,048 remains the starting bracket, localised by profiling. Two limits are part of the amendment, not footnotes to it: exact-fill buys nothing when the queue is empty (the common single-user case, which is why #18's rank does not move), and FastGen's headline gains are vendor numbers in long-prompt/short-output traffic on a self-defined SLA metric against a pre-chunked-prefill vLLM, with a contemporaneous rebuttal — the composition rule and the knee method are stolen, not the multiplier. ### D. Speculation on small cards: the narrow-tree verdict (Chapter 6)

Chapter 6's family tree gains its width discipline (`papers/specinfer.md` §§4, 6; `papers/eagle-2.md` §§3–4, 6; ledger #46, rejection R18). **Tree verification pays at width ≈2–3 and low-tens total nodes (≈8–16), depth over breadth, budget measured locally.** SpecInfer's own results flatten hard past width 2–3, and width 2–3 is optimal once batch consumes the spare capacity — a 6 GB card at batch 1 is, in spare-capacity terms, closer to the loaded regime than the idle one. ### A. Estimation by dry run, and the heuristic it replaces (Chapters 3 and 8)

This book's budgeting chapters have prescribed measured placement since the first draft; the fifth cut names the mechanism, read from the incumbent's own code (`runners/ggml-backend-internals.md` §6; ledger #56, new). **At llama.cpp v1.0.0, the pre-load memory figure is not computed from architectural parameters — it is measured by dry-running the real machinery with allocation disabled.** The model loads in no-allocation mode (real metadata and buffer-type assignments, zero-sized dummy weight buffers); a real context reserves its worst-case compute graphs in size-only form (prompt-processing, token-generation, prompt-processing again, so resident buffers fit the larger); and the figure consumed by the fit logic is the context's **memory breakdown — model / context / compute, per device** — priced through each buffer type's own allocation-size function, so backend padding (CUDA's quantised-row expansion) is included where element-count arithmetic under-reports it. The estimate cannot silently diverge from the allocator, because it *is* the allocator run without materialising. ### C. Truncation is four behaviours, and there is no session (Chapters 5 and 8)

The fifth cut's paired Ollama reading (`runners/ollama-internals.md` §§5, 7, v0.40.16) corrects two framings this book inherited. **First, truncation.** The banked two-layer finding still holds, with three corrections that change how Chapter 5's account must be read: Layer 1 (message-level, system-immortal, last-message-immortal, 768 tokens per image, debug-log-only) exists in **two parallel implementations** — native and rendered chat — which diverge even in what their log fields count; Layer 2 (keep `num_keep`, default 4; free roughly half the remainder; warning-log-only; HTTP 400 when shift is off) lives in **Ollama's own Go wrapper on the completion path only**, while the native chat path delegates to llama-server's shift-or-reject underneath; and the **shift default is family-dependent** (on for every GGUF family except `deepseek2`), so the same over-long history is silently shifted for one model and a loud error for another. There is no single "Ollama truncation behaviour": there is a **four-behaviour matrix** (two Layer-1 twins × wrapper Layer 2 vs llama-server shift-or-reject), and the fork's anchor (#2) and disclosure (#6) work is designed against the full matrix in the ledger's fifth cut. **Second, sessions.** Ollama proper holds **no conversation state** — no session object, no identifier, no server-side history — and its legacy `context` array is a deprecated text round-trip already marked for removal. Chapter 8's session designs are therefore **construction, not refactor**, and are budgeted as such (ledger #1, v5 note). The same reading settles Chapter 8's open inheritance question against the optimistic answer: the new-engine era's *measured scheduling did not survive* into the GGUF path — layer placement is delegated to llama.cpp's fit inside the subprocess, and Ollama learns the outcome afterwards from the "offloaded X/Y layers" log line. A fork that needs to *decide* placement must drive the fit explicitly, drive the layer flag, or replace the boundary; it cannot adjust a counting routine in Ollama, because there isn't one.

The llama.cpp side of the boundary (`runners/llamacpp-kv-internals.md`, v1.0.0) contributes its own corrections to Chapter 3's lineage. Prefix sharing upstream is a **per-cell sequence bitset** — a cell lists the sequences it is visible to; same-stream copies are metadata-only; placement is a non-contiguous ring search; scattered cells are consumed through row-list attention — with **no block table and no reference counts**, and **defragmentation deleted at v1.0.0** (PR #30565), not merely reworked: any description of llama.cpp that includes a defragmenter is stale. Batch preparation is **transactional** (reserve everything or change nothing; decode returns a documented failure code) — a contract this book adopts as a principle for the fork's own allocator. Context shift at library level is **K-re-rotation** (positions move; one extra graph pass re-applies RoPE to stored keys), unavailable for M-RoPE and per-layer-RoPE-dimension families and — the quiet structural trade — **unavailable for interleaved-SWA models at their default shrunk SWA cache size**, since the composite may shift only when its two sub-caches are equal-sized: the memory saving and the continuation features are mutually exclusive upstream, and a fork that shrinks SWA caches must say so in user-facing terms. Quantised KV at v1.0.0 gains **f8_e4m3** (OCP FP8, PR #28392) with per-layer scale tensors, keeps the Hadamard rotation that buys its quality, and refuses quantised V without Flash Attention at construction. And the server's accounting trap is recorded for every operator chapter: `-np` in auto mode means 4 slots with unified KV on, while explicit `-np 4` leaves unified off — the same `-c 16384 -np 4` command line describes two different machines.

### D. Scratch retention is a ledger line, and degradation is a fit-time decision (Chapters 3, 4 and 8)

Two ggml findings (`runners/ggml-backend-internals.md` §§5, 7) enter this book's accounting and failure chapters together, because they are the same lesson at two layers: **what the allocator does not report, the operator cannot see; what the allocator cannot do, the product must decide earlier.** (1) **Scratch-pool retention.** ggml's CUDA/HIP scratch exists as two pools with different personalities — a legacy pool (best-fit free list, 5% look-ahead allocations, freed blocks retained up to 256 slots, whole-cache flush on OOM) and a VMM pool (one reservation up to 32 GiB, bump allocation, backing never unmapped during the pool's life) — and under both, **scratch VRAM is high-water-mark VRAM**: once a large scratch allocation has happened, it stays resident for the life of the backend context, invisible in upstream's own breakdown, visible only as reduced driver free memory. This book's VRAM ledger therefore carries **six numbers** from this cut (ledger #6, v5 note): the fifth-cut addition is *reserved-by-pools*, per pool. A long-lived server that reports five has understated its residency by exactly the amount it has accumulated. (2) **No run-time fallback exists.** Inside ggml's allocators there is no OOM→CPU path: assignment is a planning decision made before allocation; failures surface as null buffers, false reserve returns converted to thrown errors, or — the bump allocator, the scratch pools after one flush-and-retry — process aborts. The one designed retry (drop pipeline parallelism, rebuild the scheduler, re-reserve once) is the model for the fork's rule, stated in ledger #31's v5 note and rejected in its improvised form as R25: **degradation is decided at fit time, in the open** — fewer layers, smaller context, named CPU spill — priced by the dry run (§A), disclosed in the load record, never improvised after a failed reserve. Chapter 4's arena account gains the companion caution: upstream's graph-allocator buffers are sized to the largest graph reserved and **never shrink** outside a reset — a library's defensible default whose cost lands on exactly the small-VRAM machines this book serves; the fork sizes its reserve to configuration and treats re-reserving smaller as a supported, tested path.

### E. Workload shape completes the measurement law (third-cut amendment §A)

BurstGPT and FastServe (`papers/burstgpt.md`; `papers/fastserve.md`) add the input side to this book's citation constitution. **Arrival shape joins the workload profile** in the disclosure set (ledger #44, v5 note): BurstGPT's controlled comparison — two traces scaled to the *same* 0.326 RPS, the bursty one producing higher latency and resource occupation and specifically stressing KV-cache management, as-reported — is the demonstration that mean-RPS equivalence does not imply workload equivalence, and its scheduling result (rankings flipping between the conversation and API services of one provider) is the standing caveat on every scheduling magnitude in this book, FastServe's included. Version discipline travels with the dataset: **v3 statistics (5.29M traces / 121 days) and v5 statistics (10.31M / 213 days) are different populations and are never mixed in one sentence.** FastServe's durable exports are its framing and one mechanism, not its multipliers (throughput-at-SLO under Poisson arrivals on A100 clusters; v1's Orca JCT figures and v3's vLLM figures belong to different versions, baselines and metrics and are never merged): the **semi-information-agnostic** setting (input length known exactly, output length unknowable at arrival) is the correct design premise for any local scheduler, and **demotion by attained service** — priority decaying with generation already consumed, arrivals placed at the priority their known prefill cost earns, a starvation-promotion guard — is adopted in miniature as the per-session fairness rule for multi-persona contention (ledger #24, v5 note). The fleet verdict is also recorded fairly: **chunked prefill won** — it is the local, bounded-cost change that shipped everywhere — while MLFQ survives as instincts inside other schedulers, and its proactive KV swapping (peak KV 7× FCFS's, as-reported) is the part this book explicitly declines to miniaturise.

### F. The scaled number names its recipe (fourth-cut amendment §B)

The positional family's roots (`papers/position-interpolation.md`; `papers/ntk-aware-scaling.md`) complete the four-number rule's second term. **PI manufactured the scaled number as a product claim** (June 2023: uniform position compression, *m → m/s*, plus a ≤1,000-step fine-tune; 16× demonstrated, its effective-window evidence a lenient single-needle passkey standard, its original-window cost growing with *s* in its own tables). **NTK-aware made the scaled number nearly free** (same month: positions untouched, the RoPE base raised so high frequencies are effectively extrapolated and low frequencies interpolated; **no fine-tune at all**; its base an empirical choice practitioners set above nominal — Code Llama hand-set 1,000,000). From this cut, every scaled figure in this book names **its recipe, its fine-tune or its absence, and its pricing evidence** (ledger #50, v5 note), under the rule the lineage teaches: *the cheaper the recipe that mints a context number, the more of the evidentiary burden shifts to the benchmark.* Two citation disciplines ride with it. NTK-aware has **no paper**: cite the bloc97 post, the by-parts pull request, the emozilla Dynamic-NTK post, and YaRN §3.1 / Appendix A.2 as the formalisation — never a nonexistent paper, and never silently re-attribute the method to YaRN, which itself cites the artefacts as what they are. And the runner-side duty is detect-and-honour in its oldest, simplest form: honour the shipped factor/base **exactly** (never recompute it from the formula), never apply a copied scaling setting to a checkpoint that was not extended with it — both errors are silent at short lengths and appear only in the long sessions the setting exists for.

### G. Detect-and-honour, completed: learned memory and routed depth (Chapters 6 and 9)

The fourth-cut amendment §G's class — trained-in properties a runner must detect and honour, or refuse loudly — gains its two limiting members (`papers/infini-attention.md`; `papers/titans.md`; `papers/mixture-of-depths.md`). **Infini-attention** changes the attention operator itself (per-layer recurrent compressive state, linear-attention retrieval interleaved with dot-product attention, a learned per-head gate): if checkpoints ever ship, the duty is to implement the architecture exactly or refuse it — never approximate it by truncating to a sliding window while keeping the name. **Titans** goes one step further: its long-term memory is a small network whose *weights are the memory*, updated by an inner-loop optimiser **at inference time** — so its outputs are not a function of (weights, context) alone, and session boundaries, prefix caching, snapshot/restore and reproducibility all acquire a state object whose reset semantics the *runner* must specify, because the checkpoint format cannot. **Mixture-of-Depths** completes the depth axis: routing over tokens × depth with fixed per-block capacities, pre-training architecture with no retrofit path (ledger R23, extended) — honour declared capacities exactly, at prefill by expert-choice routing and at decode by its causal predictor form. The memory-doctrine half of this family — why none of these states may hold the *record* of a person — is stated in the MEMORY volume's fifth-cut amendment and rejected in ledger R26; this volume carries the serving half: **"infinite" in this literature is a statement about input length at bounded cost, never about recall capacity**, and a fixed-size state into which ever more bindings are superimposed loses distinctions by counting alone, whatever its probes report.

### A. The pressure tier, designed — and the ownership discipline under it (Chapters 3 and 8)

Chapter 8's residency account gains the mechanism its arbiter was missing, from TokenSpeed's design documents (ledger #57, new). **When the KV pool cannot fund an admission, the runner suspends a resident session by imaging it, byte-for-byte, to pinned host memory** — its computed frontier first published into the prefix index exactly as a finish would publish it (so the suspended session's prefix stays matchable, including by its own next turn), its unpublished state plus sampler, recurrent and speculative state written to a private, bounded, unevictable snapshot pool, and the freed pages granted to the specific blocked admission in the same scheduling pass. Restore copies the image into fresh pages and resumes at the exact position. Three properties make this a tier rather than an improvisation, and Chapter 8 now states all three as requirements: the pool is **provisioned at fit time** as a deliberate fraction of the device pool (TokenSpeed's default 0.1× of device KV capacity is the starting ratio, locally unmeasured); the image carries the validity envelope (an image restored under different weights or template is stale and invalidates, never converts); and failure is **visible** — if no victim's image fits, the newest resident is aborted with a client-facing error, never silently recomputed. This is the lawful sibling of the fifth cut's rejection R25: degradation designed before pressure arrives, in the open — and the opposite of recompute-as-policy at exactly the point that policy was priced.

Underneath it, Chapter 3's allocator account gains its contract layer (ledger #58, new): **cache resources carry explicit states and named transitions** (free / owned / published / pinned / in-flight), every "must not free while X" condition is derived from state rather than stored as a flag beside it, and the scheduler works only in logical, token-denominated quantities — physical geometry (page sizes, byte layouts, packing) lives on the storage side, and the scheduler is blind to it by construction. The gain claimed is not speed: it is the removal of the silent-corruption class (stale or double-owned KV) that a persistent-cache runner can least afford and least easily test for.

### B. Cache identity is tier-scoped (Chapter 4)

Chapter 4's prefix-cache account is amended in three places by this wave's pair of cache dossiers. **First, publication is a policy.** A reuse boundary is any token count at which a request *published* its state — page multiples plus a tail wherever it stopped — so the next turn's hit covers the whole previous turn (TokenSpeed §§4.2, 4.4; ledger #5, v6 note). The junction cut, the in-forward checkpoint (state snapshotted at the boundary the *next* turn's re-render will resume from), dead-checkpoint suppression and superseded-turn eviction are adopted as the chapter's publication discipline. **Second, the chained-hash form is now the doubly-instantiated one:** ExLlamaV3 keys each complete 256-token page by a 128-bit hash chained on its parent, shares pages across concurrent jobs by reference, retains them after finish with no TTL, evicts tree-at-a-time (orphaned chains first, then least-recently-used root, pruned leaf-first so the longest prefix survives), and spills through pinned-RAM and disk tiers under the same key (session pass Q1–Q2). **Third — the disposition this cut exists to state — identity is tier-scoped.** The chained hash is the identity *within* a configuration domain; V3's in-process tiers key on tokens alone because their domain is singular, while its disk tier is partitioned by a full configuration fingerprint (model including sampled weight contents, cache configuration and per-layer bit-widths, page size, format version, engine version). Wherever multiple effective configurations can coexist inside one domain — adapter sets and scales are the proven case — configuration enters the key or the domain is partitioned. The slogan "the prefix hash is the only identity state ever needs" is rejected as stated (ledger R28): its own source's disk tier refutes it. **A divergence is recorded, not harmonised:** TokenSpeed publishes partial-page tails as matchable; V3 shares only complete pages. Both cannot be the fork's granularity rule; the choice is deferred to the page-size sweep (ledger Q11), and this chapter does not pretend the two designs agree.

Chapter 4 also gains the in-flight complement (ledger #59, new): **single-flight shared prefill** — a request whose prefix another in-flight request is already prefilling waits and reuses that computation instead of duplicating it, the shared prefix remaining cached after the batch finishes (Magnitude 0.2.16, primary release notes). Its local case is the burst shape the fifth cut opened as Q30 — parallel subagents and sidecars sharing one system prompt. The vendor's pathological figures (8 concurrent requests sharing a 15K prompt, 226 s → 3.5 s each on an NVIDIA A10G, with draft model, as-reported) are recorded as the best case they are; the mechanism's value does not depend on the multiple. Cancellation semantics are part of the design: a join is an optimisation, never a dependency that can strand a queue.

### C. Prefill is budgeted in time (Chapter 3)

Chapter 3's chunked-prefill account changes its budgeting variable (ledger #18, v6 note). DeepInfer's scheduler does not fix a chunk size: each prefill step is given a **time budget**, and a fitted quadratic cost model — cost as a function of chunk tokens and the prefix length already computed — is solved for the largest chunk fitting both the time budget and the token ceiling. The user-visible quantity on this target was never tokens; it is the worst-case pause before the next token. The local form fits the two-parameter model at model load (a handful of timed chunks at two prefix lengths, recorded in the load record) and sizes each chunk to a stated inter-token latency budget, chunks shrinking as the resident prefix grows. The budgeting *rule* is adopted; DeepInfer's production magnitudes are not claimed — its chunk-solving is described for the production system in the deployment Q&A, and the shipped engine's visible half is the batched-token cap.

The same dossier generalises the speculation chapters' budgeting (ledger #32, v6 note): confidence — in a general runner, the acceptance history the runner already collects — combined with a profiled throughput curve sets the per-step verify budget, instead of a fixed draft length spent uniformly. The bound travels with the rule: DeepInfer's own table prices the prize at +4–7% at its production operating point and **−1.5% at its lowest measured concurrency**, and this target's concurrency is one. The rule is adopted as a late, telemetry-gated refinement; the expectation is refused.

### D. The trellis floor, and two cache settlements (Chapters 5 and 4)

Chapter 5's provisioning account is re-scoped at its low end (ledger #42, v6 note). EXL3 — trellis-coded quantisation of QTIP lineage, with randomized-Hadamard incoherence processing and LDLQ error feedback — extends the credible lineage below 3 bpw: coherent published artefacts at ~1.6 bpw on large models, MoE quants release-tested at 2.05 bpw, and independent measurement in the dossier supporting roughly **a bit per weight of quality-equivalent compression versus scalar allocation** (30B MoE: EXL3 at 3.0 bpw within 0.14 perplexity of a ~4.86 bpw GGUF K-quant; 12B at 4.00 bpw with lower mean KLD than Q4_K_M at fewer weight bytes — RTX 5090 stack comparison, all as-reported). The re-scope is conditional in three stated ways: per artefact family through the raw-oracle gate (the floor is a trellis property, not a scalar-tier property); **provisioning priced on total artefact bytes, all tables included** — EXL3's headline excludes its default 16-bit embedding table, and embedding-inclusive totals in the 12B comparison were near a GGUF Q5_K_M; and decode cost unproven on the target card class (trellis reconstruction spends register ALU per weight; ledger Q35 is the gate). A fork-side trellis quantiser is not scheduled: it is a format programme gated on a decode-kernel prototype on the target card class, per the dossier's own gate.

Chapter 4's asymmetric-KV account gains two shipped settlements (ledger #19, v6 note). Magnitude's **K8/V4** history codec (keys 8-bit, values 4-bit, affine, consumed directly by the decode kernel) is asymmetry as a fixed consumer-product default — corroborating the shape at a different operating point than KIVI-2's, with its quality claims recorded as unverified in its dossier's pass and its launch memory comparison not like-for-like in cache precision. ExLlamaV3 contributes **staged dequantisation**: cache precision as a near-continuous 2–8-bit dial, with prefill chunks dequantising the referenced window once into a bounded FP16 scratch (prefill within ~1–3% of FP16, as-reported) while decode always reads packed — and the interaction warning that a 4-bit cache costs more KL on high-bpw weights than on low-bpw ones, so cache precision is priced per (model, weight-rate) pair, never global-defaulted.

### E. Calibration with guardrails, and the attribution line (Chapters 8 and 9)

Chapter 8's calibration account is upgraded from discipline to shipped form (ledger #40, v6 note). Magnitude tunes per model **at download time, on the user's own chip**, under a hard ~60-second budget: the search ordered by an invocation-demand cost model, a challenger admitted only on a statistically significant lead across matched rounds, a numerical validator rejecting candidates whose outputs deviate beyond a declared rounding tolerance (non-float state bit-exact), and results persisted in a cache keyed by model digest + tuning version + device identity + implementation digest + served shapes. For a fork that does not own its kernels, the calibration surface is the knobs it does own — thread count, batch sizes, flash-attention on/off, KV type per K/V, offload split, draft length — worth honestly single-digit to ~20%, plus the occasional large win where a default is simply wrong for the hardware; and the search runs in the background when the device is free, never blocking first use (Magnitude's own history — 33–48-minute uncapped tuning on a slow host, an interrupted run leaving models silently untuned — is the cautionary record).

Chapter 9's measurement law gains its **attribution line** (ledger #44, v6 note), with Magnitude's disclosure trail as the worked case: a 2.17–2.19× decode headline that is defaults-versus-defaults and drafter-included, against a controlled independent measurement attributing ~1.12–1.20× to the tuned base engine (identical GGUF, M1 Max, drafter isolated, as-reported), ~2× additional to the drafter on predictable code, and a drafter *penalty* (0.90–0.93×) on long-context work. The rule: every comparative claim names **which mechanisms differ between the two sides**; where several differ, the figure may be reported only as a system comparison, never assigned to one mechanism; and a comparison in which the two sides run different cache precisions is not like-for-like and must say so in the same breath as the number.

### F. Persistence by reuse horizon, and the co-design boundary (Chapter 8)

Chapter 8's persistence account is partitioned by the axis it previously left monolithic (ledger #10, v6 note; the MEMORY amendment carries the doctrine form). DeepInfer's production split — global KV on SSD for days (TTL 3 days agent / 7 days chatbot, set from measured reuse intervals and priced in hit-rate points), sliding-window state in host DRAM for minutes, never persisted — miniaturises to: the **trunk** (persona/system prefix + completed turns) persists to disk under the validity envelope; the live window's transient state stays in RAM only; and TTLs are set from the runner's own counted trunk-hit age distribution, not by habit. The envelope takes its production form as the **validity tuple** — (deployment configuration, weights, user group), any KV-altering framework change invalidates, and **no migration path: a mismatch discards, never converts** — with the local tuple dropping the vacuous user-group term and adding adapter set + scales, template identity, cache precision and runner KV-affecting version.

The boundary of the DeepInfer transfer is stated as plainly as the transfer: its cross-layer KV sharing, FP4 KV cache (safe only because the checkpoint was quantisation-aware-trained for it), wide expert parallelism and disaggregated store are co-designed with one model and one fleet and do **not** transfer. What transfers is the discipline — persistence split by horizon, validity as a tuple with total invalidation, prefill budgeted in time, verification budgeted by confidence — each miniaturised in the sections above, and each recorded in the ledger with its evidence grade and its bound.
